# Write the mnemonics for one {{LEVEL}} vocabulary word

This is the last link in the chain: the learner already has an image for each
radical, and a story for each kanji built from those radicals. Now the kanji
combine into a *word*, and your job is to make that combination feel inevitable.
Follow the teaching voice above.

A meaning mnemonic always; a reading mnemonic only when this word's reading
actually needs one — the reading section below decides, and for many words the
answer is none at all. Plus a description, only when this word needs one.

## What the page already tells the learner

Two fields on this word's page are **computed, not written**, and they sit right
beside your mnemonics:

- **Word type** — see the word type section below.
- **Reading composition** — how the reading is built: {{READING_COMPOSITION}}

Never restate either of them. They are derived mechanically from the word's
kanji and their dictionary readings, so nothing you could write about them is
more accurate, and every sentence spent on one is a sentence taken from the part
only you can do. In particular: **do not name jukugo, on'yomi, kun'yomi, rendaku
or gemination anywhere in a mnemonic.** That vocabulary belongs to the field
above, which already uses it.

## How long

**Meaning mnemonic: one sentence, or two short ones, roughly 70–200
characters.** Finishing at 90 is a success, not a shortfall. These are the
target, and they are short on purpose:

> A <kanji form="口">mouth</kanji> that you walk into is an
> <vocabulary form="入口">entrance</vocabulary>.

> The number of <kanji form="人">people</kanji> with <kanji form="口">mouths</kanji>
> you have to feed is your <vocabulary form="人口">population</vocabulary>.

> You already know 学生 is a student, so a <kanji form="小">small</kanji> one is
> an <vocabulary form="小学生">elementary school student</vocabulary>.

**Reading mnemonic: follow the reading section below**, which says which of
three things this word gets — nothing at all, one sentence pointing back at the
word that already taught this reading, or a full story of one sentence or two
short ones, roughly 90–220 characters.

Going over to finish a thought is fine; padding to reach a number is not. The
depth comes from how specific the picture is, never from how many sentences it
takes — the learner reads this tired, on a phone, on the fourth review of the
week.

## The kanji must add up to the word

This is the rule the whole mnemonic stands on. The kanji in this word have to
*combine into* its meaning, so that a reader can see why those characters
produce that word. A scene in which each kanji takes a turn doing something,
with the meaning announced at the end, teaches nothing — the learner remembers a
squirrel and a cascade of bells and still cannot say what the word means.

**Wrong** — a scene the kanji take turns acting in:

> At Grandma Kane's shrine the cats have organized themselves into a council.
> Every evening they gather on the mossy steps, yowling over who gets the
> sunniest step and who rings the bell at dawn, and only once the vote is cast
> does the yard finally <kanji form="治">calm down</kanji>. Cats running the shrine until
> it goes quiet is what politics looks like.

**Right** — the kanji combining into a claim:

> <kanji form="政">Governing</kanji> a country is mostly the art of getting it
> to <kanji form="治">calm down</kanji>, which is all
> <vocabulary form="政治">politics</vocabulary> really is.

Test it: delete one kanji's clause. If the mnemonic still makes the same point,
that kanji was staged as decoration rather than used as a reason. Rewrite it so
every kanji is load-bearing.

Never invent a bystander to carry the logic. If your scene needs a gatekeeper, a
hiker, or a passing animal who is not one of this word's kanji to make the point
land, the point is not actually coming from the kanji.

## Two ways to make them add up

A *scene* is one way to get the kanji to combine. It is not the only way, and
reaching for one on every word makes a whole level of mnemonics blur into a
single long cartoon. The other way is to state the real reason plainly:

> Japanese has long used <kanji form="青">blue</kanji> for things that are fresh
> and not yet ripe, so a man still young in <kanji form="年">year</kanji>s is an
> unripe one — a <vocabulary form="青年">youth (young man)</vocabulary>.

Nothing there is invented and nothing is absurd, and it holds better than a scene
would, because the reason is true. Japanese is full of these: a character
carrying a sense English drops, an old metaphor still alive inside the compound,
a pairing that was obvious to whoever coined it.

**Pick per word, on what the word actually has.**

- The kanji have a real reason for pairing → say it plainly. Two clauses, no
  scene, no cast.
- The pairing is arbitrary, or its reason is too tangled for one sentence →
  build the scene, and follow the comedy register in the voice sheet.

The voice sheet's comedy register governs the second of these, not the first. An
explanation is allowed to be plain and unfunny — that is the register working,
not the register failing. Everything else in this prompt applies to both: the
kanji still have to be load-bearing, the tags still go on, the sentence limits
still hold.

Do not alternate on a schedule, and do not split the difference — half an
explanation wearing a costume is worse than either one done properly. And
**never invent the history.** A made-up etymology is worse than a made-up scene,
because the learner has no way to tell that it is made up. When you are not
certain the reason is real, write the scene instead.

## Sentence shape

Short sentences. One idea per sentence — most under 18 words, none over 25.
Plain English, the word a ten-year-old would use: *tied* not *lashed*, *bundle*
not *sheaf*, *tips over* not *lurches*. When a sentence wants a second clause, a
dash, or an "-ing" tail ("eyes streaming"), make it two sentences instead. If it
sounds like a novel, it is wrong.

**Most of these learners read English as a second language.** An idiom is a
second puzzle stacked on the one you meant to set them, and they cannot look it
up without abandoning the review. So write sentences that survive a reader who
takes every word literally. Cut the phrasal verbs and figures of speech that mean
something other than the sum of their parts: *rakes off* → *takes*, *butts
against* → *presses against*, *snags on* → *catches on*, *notch the years onto
the board* → *cut a mark into the board*. If a phrase would need its own
dictionary entry, replace it with the plain words underneath it.

## The kanji in this word

Every character of the written form the learner can be told about, in the order
they appear. These are the only kanji you may name.

They come in two kinds, and the list says which each one is.

**Taught** — the learner has read a meaning story for it. **Hook into those
stories** — reuse their imagery instead of inventing a fresh scene that competes
with what they already remember. Reusing the object is enough; restaging that
kanji's own scene is what blows the budget.

**Not taught** — this word is written with it, but it is banded later, so the
learner meets the character here and nowhere else. There is no story to hook
into and no reading to lean on, so you get its **meaning and nothing more**. Use
that meaning as a plain fact in your logic. It still has to be load-bearing:
this is the half of the word your story would otherwise leave unexplained.

## Highlighting

The app colours the pieces the learner is meant to connect, so you mark them
inline. Three tags, and nothing else:

Every tag also **names the character it stands for**, in a `form` attribute, so
the app can print that character after the word:

    Give it a <kanji form="少">few</kanji> <kanji form="年">year</kanji>s, and
    the child grows into a <vocabulary form="少年">boy</vocabulary>.

reaches the learner as “Give it a few (少) years (年), and the child grows into a
boy (少年).” Write the character in the attribute and **never in the prose** —
the parentheses, the font and the colour are the app's, and a character typed
into the sentence would be read out by a screen reader in the middle of it. Copy
each character exactly as it is given to you above. `<reading>` takes no `form`:
its own text is already the kana.

The kanji stories quoted above were written before the attribute existed, so
their tags are bare. That is history, not the format — yours carry the
character.

- In `meaning_mnemonic`, wrap **every listed kanji's meaning**, taught or not,
  where the story uses it, in `<kanji form="…">…</kanji>`, with that kanji's own
  character in the attribute. The tag marks a piece of the written form, so an
  untaught character wears it on the same terms as a taught one. Tag the word
  doing the work in the sentence, not a label bolted on:
  `the <kanji form="土">ground</kanji> splits`, never
  `the ground (<kanji form="土">ground</kanji>) splits` — the app adds the
  parentheses.
- In **both** mnemonics, wrap **this word's own meaning** in
  `<vocabulary form="{{FORM}}">…</vocabulary>`, exactly once each — the whole
  written form goes in the attribute. A component is a piece
  borrowed from the kanji below; this is the word's own meaning, the thing the
  story is building toward, and the app renders the two in different colours. It
  may sit anywhere in the sentence — first word, middle or last — so put it
  where the sentence reads best and do not contort the wording to push it to the
  end. **Use the meaning we teach — the words at the top of this prompt — not
  one of the accepted answers.** When a component kanji's word *is* this word's
  meaning word, tag that same word twice on two different spans: `<kanji>` where
  the kanji enters, `<vocabulary>` where the story lands. That is correct and
  expected.
- In `reading_mnemonic`, when you write one, wrap the kana 「{{READING}}」 in
  `<reading>…</reading>`, **and** wrap the English word your scene uses to carry
  that sound — the phonetic hook — in the same tag. In a one-sentence
  back-reference there is no invented hook, so tagging the kana is enough.

A reading mnemonic therefore carries both a `<reading>` and a `<vocabulary>`
tag: the learner must be able to see, from the reading mnemonic alone, which
word this sound belongs to. A sound floating next to a scene that could belong
to any word is a sound they will attach to the wrong word by Thursday.

One exception, and the reading section above is what triggers it: when this
word is **written in kana**, it has no reading mnemonic and no kanji, so its
meaning mnemonic carries the sound as well. There `<reading>` is required in the
meaning mnemonic — on the kana verbatim and on the English phonetic hook —
beside the usual `<vocabulary>`. Everywhere else `<reading>` stays out of a
meaning mnemonic.

`<radical>` never appears in a vocabulary mnemonic — radicals are the kanji
layer's business. Don't tag anything else: a tag on an ordinary word teaches the
learner that the colour means nothing.

## What to write

Call the `submit_vocab_mnemonic` tool exactly once with:

1. **`meaning_mnemonic`** — one sentence, or two short ones, in which the listed
   kanji's meanings combine until they *produce* “{{MEANING}}”. Start from the
   imagery those kanji already carry — for an untaught one, from its bare
   meaning — put them in one claim, and let the word's meaning fall out of the
   logic of what they are — see “The kanji must add up to the word” above; that
   is the part that matters most. Decide the register first — see “Two ways to
   make them add up”. When the word earns a scene, strange
   and funny is what survives reviews, but the absurdity has to be the
   mechanism, never a joke parked next to it. If the section “Is this word just
   its kanji?” says this one is, ignore this bullet and follow that section
   instead. If no kanji are listed above — a kana word, or one whose kanji the
   learner hasn't met — name no kanji at all and write a standalone story
   instead. For a **kana** word that story must also carry the sound, exactly
   as the reading section above specifies: it is the only field that word has.

2. **`reading_mnemonic`** — **optional, and the reading section above decides.**
   Follow it exactly; it is computed from this word's kanji and their dictionary
   readings, so it is not a suggestion.

   - When it says this word gets **no reading mnemonic**, omit the key
     altogether. Do not write a short one instead. The reading is what the kanji
     already say, the computed reading-composition field says so beside your
     mnemonic, and a sentence repeating it teaches the learner to skim the ones
     that *do* carry a hook.
   - When it says the reading is **already taught by an earlier word**, write
     one sentence naming that word, with 「{{READING}}」 in a `<reading>` tag and
     this word's meaning in a `<vocabulary>` tag. No cast character, no scene —
     a second image for a sound they already have competes with the first.
   - When it says to write a **full story**, write one sentence or two short
     ones in which the sound and this word's meaning are the same joke: the
     reading's cast character walks into the scene your meaning mnemonic just
     built — the same object, place or action — and does something the
     meaning explains, the kana appears verbatim, and the meaning sits in a
     `<vocabulary>` tag. Do not give the cast character a scene of its own:
     two unrelated scenes compete for one memory. If the sentence would still
     make sense with a different word's meaning dropped into it, or if it
     would still make sense without your meaning mnemonic, it is not finished.

     Right — the trail from the meaning mnemonic comes back:

     > Meaning: The rope bridge on the mountain trail is missing every third
     > plank, and the river roars a long way down through each gap. Standing
     > where one bad step drops you into that is
     > <vocabulary form="危ない">dangerous</vocabulary>.
     >
     > Reading: A marmot on the mountain trail whistles something like
     > <reading>'a boo, nigh'</reading> whenever a hiker drifts toward the
     > cliff edge. Hikers learned that the shrill <reading>あぶない</reading>
     > whistle means the next step is
     > <vocabulary form="危ない">dangerous</vocabulary>.

3. **`description`** — **optional. Omit this key entirely unless this word
   actually needs it.** The two mnemonics are what the learner reads; a
   description that restates the gloss is noise they scroll past. Write one only
   when there is something the story cannot carry: a register that would get
   them in trouble (who says this to whom), a false friend, a sense that doesn't
   transfer from English, or a form the word almost always appears in. The word
   type is **not** a reason to write one — it has its own field. When you do
   write one: plain factual prose, no story, no highlight tags, and shorter than
   a mnemonic.

## Hard rules

- Plain prose only, apart from the highlight tags. No markdown, no headings,
  no lists, no bold/italic, no furigana brackets, no romaji (write kana). Stored
  as plain strings.
- `<kanji>` appears only in the meaning mnemonic, `<reading>` only in the
  reading mnemonic — except on a kana word, whose meaning mnemonic carries the
  sound and so must contain `<reading>` too. `<vocabulary>` once in each
  mnemonic you write, none of them in the description. `<radical>` appears
  nowhere. Every tag you open, close. Never nest them.
- Every `<kanji>` and every `<vocabulary>` carries a `form` attribute holding
  the character it stands for — that kanji's own character, and this word's
  written form `{{FORM}}`. Copy it exactly; never type it into the prose as
  well. `<reading>` carries no `form`.
- Never name jukugo, on'yomi, kun'yomi, rendaku or gemination in either
  mnemonic. The computed reading-composition field beside them already does.
- Meaning mnemonic: reference every kanji listed above by the meaning given for
  it, each wrapped in `<kanji>` where the story uses it — the untaught ones
  included; they are in the word the learner is looking at. Never name a kanji
  that is not in that list. Wrap this word's own meaning, once, in
  `<vocabulary>`.
  **At most two sentences**, two terminal full stops and no more — the gate
  rejects a third. Stay under 230 characters, tags not counted — 260 for a kana
  word, whose meaning mnemonic carries the sound as well; going over is a
  rewrite, not a rounding error — cut words, never cut a kanji.
- Reading mnemonic, when you write one: 「{{READING}}」 must appear verbatim
  inside a `<reading>` tag, and this word's taught meaning must appear inside a
  `<vocabulary>` tag. **At most two sentences** — one, for a back-reference.
- Description, when you write one: no Japanese characters beyond this word and
  its own kanji. Descriptions carry no furigana, so stray Japanese renders
  unreadable.
- No emoji.
