You pick up a pen at the convenience store counter to sign something, and the ink barely shows — every stroke drags like the paper doesn't want it. In Japanese you don't complain that you're bad at writing; you say something about the pen itself.

```jp
このペンは書きにくいです。
```
*This pen is hard to write with.*

That にくい stuck onto the stem of 書く (書き) turns "write" into a built-in review of the pen — what it's like to use, not how one specific attempt went. やすい does the opposite job: it says a thing is easy to deal with, no matter who's holding it.

## How it works

Both にくい and やすい attach to the same spot on a verb: the stem, the part left over when you drop ます from the polite form (書きます → 書き). Drop the tense information entirely and you're left with the bare stem, then the pattern glues straight onto it.

```jp
書く → 書きにくい
```

There's no extra word needed to make that grammatical — にくい and やすい are already complete い-adjectives the instant they're built, with their own です, their own past tense, their own negative. Swap the ending and the meaning flips:

```jp
この本は読みやすいです。
```
*This book is easy to read.*

Think of it this way: the verb hands over its stem and steps aside, and from that point the adjective is in charge of describing the object, not the action. Because the whole word is now an い-adjective, it conjugates exactly like 大きい or 難しい does — nothing about the underlying verb changes anymore. Put it in the past and only the にくい/やすい part moves:

```jp
書きにくかったです。
```
*It was hard to write with.*

## Rules and exceptions

The pattern works on almost any verb whose stem you can pull out, which covers everything from 話す to 歩く, so you'll see it attached to a wide range of everyday actions:

```jp
この靴は歩きやすいです。
```
*These shoes are easy to walk in.*

The one mix-up that catches people early: にくい/やすい is not doing the same job as the potential form (読める, 話せる, できる — the word that says someone *can* do something). The potential form asks "am I capable of doing this?" — a fact about the person. にくい/やすい asks "what is this thing like to deal with?" — a fact about the object, independent of who's doing it. 読みにくい本 is a badly laid-out book that would frustrate anyone; 読めない本 is a book one particular person can't read, maybe because it's in a language they don't know.

> Once にくい/やすい is attached, treat the whole word as one adjective from then on. The negative is `書きにくくない`, built off にくい itself — never something like `書きませんにくい`, which tries to negate the verb underneath and just breaks.

One phrase built from this pattern is worth flagging on its own, because it shows up everywhere outside grammar explanations too:

```jp
彼の話は分かりやすいです。
```
*His talk is easy to understand.*

分かりやすい has basically become its own compliment — "clear," "easy to follow" — said about teachers, directions, books, anything that communicates well.

## Contrast

You already know かもしれない for "might happen" — a guess about one specific event, made with whatever information you have right now. にくい/やすい is a different kind of statement: it isn't a guess about whether something will happen this time, it's a claim about what the thing is generally like.

```jp
この皿は壊れやすいです。
```
*This dish breaks easily.*

```jp
この皿は壊れるかもしれません。
```
*This dish might break.*

The first sentence is a review of the dish itself — thin, fragile, badly made, whatever the reason, it tends to break. The second is a one-off worry: maybe today, maybe because someone's about to drop it, with no claim about the dish's overall build. Mix the two up and you'll either tell someone a solid dish is fragile, or make a fragile one sound like it's only at risk this once.

にくい/やすい shares its attachment point with another word you'll meet soon — すぎる, which turns 食べる into "ate too much." Same spot on the verb, same instinct: look at the stem, then decide what quality to bolt onto it.
