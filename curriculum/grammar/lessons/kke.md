You're heading out the door with a friend to catch a movie you both planned last week. You already worked out the start time this morning, texting back and forth about it — venue, time, who's driving — and now, phone back in your pocket, the time itself is just gone. Not the movie, not the friend, not the plan. Just that one number, slipped out of reach at the worst possible moment. This is exactly the gap っけ was built for: not asking something you never knew, but reaching for something you *did* know and lost.

```jp
映画は何時だっけ。
```

*What time was the movie again?*

You're not asking your friend to teach you something new. You're both trying to pull back a fact that already exists between you, one you both signed off on this morning. That distinction — brand-new information versus misplaced information — is the whole personality of っけ, and it shapes every rule below. Ask a stranger something with っけ and it sounds strange, because a stranger has no shared fact for you to be digging back up.

## How it works

っけ attaches to the **plain form** of whatever you're digging for: a verb, an i-adjective, or a noun/na-adjective riding on the copula だ (the word that works like *is/am/are*). Which tense you use tracks *when* the fact belonged to you, not when you happen to be asking about it — that part trips people up at first, because in English "again" does all the work regardless of tense, and here the tense itself is doing real signalling.

For an ongoing fact or a plan you already settled — something still true right now, you just can't hold onto it — plain non-past plus っけ does the job:

```jp
彼はいつ来るっけ。
```

*When was he coming again?*

He's still coming; the arrival hasn't happened yet. Only the specific *when* has slipped. That's why the verb stays non-past even though you're clearly recalling something from an earlier conversation — the fact itself is still future, only your grip on the detail is the part in the past.

For something that already happened, and you're trying to drag it back out of storage, use plain past plus っけ:

```jp
先週、彼と会ったっけ。
```

*Did I meet him last week?*

Here the event itself is finished, so the verb carries that with 会った before っけ ever gets attached. I-adjectives work the same way — past tense, because you're checking how something *was*, not asking how it is right now:

```jp
あのラーメン、辛かったっけ。
```

*Was that ramen spicy?*

You've presumably already eaten it. You're not asking a waiter to describe the menu; you're trying to reconstruct your own experience of a dish that's already behind you, which is why 辛い has to wear its own past tense, 辛かった, before っけ lands on top.

Nouns and na-adjectives can't take っけ directly; they need the copula だ to carry it, the same way they need だ for almost everything else that isn't a verb or an i-adjective. Non-past だっけ checks a fact still true now; past だったっけ checks something that was true, or decided, at some point before now:

```jp
今日の予定は何だっけ。
```

*What was today's plan again?*

```jp
彼女の名前、何だったっけ。
```

*What was her name again?*

The first one is asking about a plan that's still live today — the day hasn't finished happening — so だ stays non-past. The second is asking about something fixed in the past: her name was decided (by her parents, presumably) long before this conversation, so だ becomes だった. The same logic would apply to any na-adjective — 元気だっけ asks whether someone's doing all right *right now*, something you'd forgotten to check on, while 元気だったっけ asks whether they were doing all right back when you last actually knew.

Notice that none of these sentences are in polite です/ます form. That's not an accident — it's the subject of the next section.

## Rules and exceptions

っけ only works when the fact was, at some point, genuinely yours. If you never actually knew the answer, っけ is the wrong tool — you'd just ask the plain question, no っけ attached. Save it for the specific feeling of an answer sitting just out of reach, not for a question that's brand new to you. Asking a store clerk your own store's opening hours with っけ would sound bizarre; you never had that fact to lose in the first place.

Adjective conjugation is where people trip most often. An i-adjective carries its own past tense the same way だ carries tense for nouns, so it needs that tense *before* っけ lands — you can't bolt だ onto an i-adjective the way you would onto a noun. `辛いだっけ` is wrong; `辛かったっけ` is correct, because 辛い already has its own way of marking past tense, and だ was never part of it to begin with. This is the single most common mistake learners make with this pattern, mostly because だ feels like the "default" tense-marker once you've gotten comfortable with nouns.

> Casual as っけ is, it survives inside polite speech almost untouched — you just dress the copula in です instead of だ. でしたっけ (polite past plus っけ) shows up constantly with coworkers, shopkeepers, anyone you'd normally speak politely to but still need to fumble for a forgotten fact in front of:

```jp
明日の試験は何時からでしたっけ。
```

*What time was tomorrow's exam from again?*

That's the one genuinely surprising exception: っけ itself never gets more formal — there's no keigo upgrade for the particle — but the words carrying it can, and でしたっけ is common enough that it's worth learning as its own chunk rather than rebuilding it from scratch every time you need it. Plain だっけ toward a teacher, though, still reads as too blunt; keep でしたっけ for anyone you owe polite speech to.

## Casual speech

Outside that でしたっけ pocket, っけ lives entirely in casual registers. You won't see it in a business email or a news broadcast — only in speech, and in writing that's imitating speech: texts, casual posts, dialogue. There's no further contraction to learn here the way です shrinks to じゃ or わからない shrinks to わかんない; っけ already *is* the low-effort, casual version of asking. There's nowhere further down to contract to — it's already sitting at the bottom.

What does vary is how much sentence gets attached to it. In fast conversation, っけ often rides on almost nothing but a question word and the copula, with everything else left for context to fill in:

```jp
それ、何だっけ。
```

*What was that again?*

No verb, no full clause — just enough to point at the gap and let っけ do the rest. A friend holding up an object you clearly used to know the name of gets exactly this, nothing more, and it lands as completely natural rather than clipped or rude, because っけ's whole job is already doing the heavy lifting of "help me find this."

## Contrast

によると reports what an outside source told you; っけ reports what your own memory is failing to hand back. They can describe the very same fact from two completely different angles, and it's worth seeing them side by side rather than treating them as unrelated:

```jp
先生によると、明日試験がある。
```

*According to the teacher, there's an exam tomorrow.*

```jp
明日、試験があったっけ。
```

*Wasn't there an exam tomorrow?*

The first sentence has a source standing outside you: the teacher said it, so によると frames it as secondhand and dependable, something you're passing along rather than vouching for out of your own memory. The second sentence has no source but your own fraying memory — you once knew this, and you're checking whether it still holds. Swap the two and the sentence stops making sense: you can't によると your own memory, because there's no outside source to cite, and you can't っけ someone else's fresh announcement, because there's nothing of yours to be recalling yet. Keeping this distinction straight matters more than it might look, because both patterns can trail a sentence that's ostensibly about the exact same exam, and only the source of the certainty tells you which one fits.

Every っけ question is doing one job: reopening a fact you already filed away, out loud, so someone — maybe just you — can hand it back. Hold onto that reaching-for-a-misplaced-fact feeling; it's a close cousin of んです, which you'll meet soon, and which explains facts you're pulling out for the *first* time instead of digging them back up.
