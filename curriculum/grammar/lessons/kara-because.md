You're at a friend's place for dinner, and the food is genuinely great. Your friend asks why you're going back for a third helping — and you want to say *because it's delicious*. But hold on: you don't have adjectives like "delicious" yet in your toolkit, so let's pick a reason you *can* say. Your friend asks why you're not drinking the beer, and the honest answer is: *because I'm a student* (and, let's say, too young). That "because" is から.

から is the workhorse "because" of Japanese. You take your reason, say it as a full sentence, and stick から on the end. That's the whole trick — no rearranging, no special verb form. The reason comes first, then から, then (often) the result.

## How it works

The structure is refreshingly flat: **a full clause + から**. Whatever sentence states your reason, you just append から to it.

Because the clause in front is a complete sentence, it can end in a verb, an adjective, a noun + です, whatever — から doesn't care. Here's a noun-predicate reason (someone *is* something):

```jp
私は学生ですから。
```

*Because I'm a student.*

Notice から sitting right after です. That's the polite pattern, sometimes listed as ですから — but it's really just です (the word that works like *is/am/are*) plus から glued on. Nothing new to memorize.

You can put the reason and the result in one breath. Reason first, から, then the consequence:

```jp
今日は忙しいですから、昼ご飯は肉です。
```

*Because I'm busy today, lunch is meat.*

Here the reason ends in the い-adjective 忙しい ("busy"). から attaches straight to it — again, no reshaping the word. The comma after から is just breathing room; it's optional.

You can also flip the order. Say the result, then tack the reason on afterward as its own sentence ending in から:

```jp
昼ご飯は肉です。忙しいですから。
```

*Lunch is meat. Because I'm busy.*

This after-the-fact version is extremely common in speech — you blurt the point, then justify it. Both orders mean the same thing.

## Rules and exceptions

The big thing to internalize: から attaches to a **whole clause**, not to a noun by itself. English lets you say "because of the rain," gluing "because" onto a noun. Japanese から can't do that alone — it needs a real predicate in front. So you say 寒いですから ("because it's cold"), not 寒さから for "because of the cold."

You have a choice of politeness on the clause before から. Both of these are fine:

```jp
今日は寒いですから。
今日は寒いから。
```

*Because it's cold today.* (polite / plain)

The first keeps です for a polite tone; the second is the plain form. Here's the useful rule of thumb: match the clause before から to the formality of the sentence you're in. In a polite conversation, ...ですから feels smoother; among friends, the plain ...から is normal.

> から carries a slightly *subjective*, personal-reason flavor — it's *your* reason, sometimes with a hint of insistence. That's fine in most speech, but be aware that leaning on it hard ("...から！") can sound like you're pushing your excuse. When you want a softer, more neutral "because," Japanese has another tool (see the closing note).

One more trap: don't confuse this reason-から with the から that means "from" (a starting point). Same three sounds, totally different job. The "from" one attaches to nouns (a place, a time); the "because" one attaches to a clause. Context and what's in front of it tell them apart.

## Casual speech

In casual Japanese, you mostly just drop the です and keep the plain clause + から:

```jp
今日は忙しいから。
```

*Because I'm busy today.*

You'll also hear だから standing alone at the start of a reply — literally "because (it) is" — used like "that's why" or, with attitude, "like I said." And a blunt だから何？ means "so what?" These are set phrases built from the same だ + から; you don't need to produce them yet, just recognize them.

## Contrast

You haven't learned any sibling patterns yet, so there's nothing to swap から against inside your current toolkit — which actually makes this the cleanest possible time to lock in one idea: **から = reason, and it lives at the end of the reason clause.** Reason, then から, then result. Get that shape into your ear now.

The only contrast worth flagging is the internal one from the rules section — reason-から (clause + から) versus from-から (noun + から). Keep them mentally in separate drawers.

Because you have this now, a whole category of sentences opens up: any time you can state a fact, you can turn it into a reason. Lock in the shape — reason clause, then から — because the very next "because" you meet, ので, lives in exactly the same space but sounds softer and more matter-of-fact. When you learn it, you'll define it against today's から.
