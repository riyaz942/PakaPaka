You're describing your morning to a friend, and you want to say two things that happened in a row: you woke up, and then you ate breakfast. In English you'd just drop an "and" between them. In Japanese, you don't reach for a word that means "and" — you bend the first verb into a new shape called the て形 (te-form), and that shape does the connecting for you.

Watch it link two actions into one smooth sentence:

```jp
起きて、朝ご飯を食べます。
```

*I wake up and eat breakfast.*

The first verb 起きる became 起きて, and that little て is doing all the work of "and then." The second verb stays polite and normal (食べます), carrying the tense for the whole sentence. That's the core idea: the て形 is a connector that hands the tense and politeness off to whatever comes after it.

## How it works

The て形 (the connective form — the shape a verb takes when it needs to hook onto something) is built from the plain dictionary form of the verb. How you build it depends on which group the verb belongs to.

**Ichidan verbs** (the "る-droppers", like 食べる and 見る) are the easy ones. Drop the る, add て. That's it.

```jp
食べる → 食べて
```

*to eat → eating / eat and…*

So 見る becomes 見て, 起きる becomes 起きて, 寝る becomes 寝て. One clean rule, no surprises.

**Godan verbs** are messier, because the ending sound changes depending on the last kana. This is the part everyone has to drill, so here's the whole map:

- く → いて (書く → 書いて)
- ぐ → いで (the voiced version keeps its voicing: で, not て)
- ぶ・む・ぬ → んで (飲む → 飲んで, 遊ぶ → 遊んで)
- す → して (話す → 話して)
- う・つ・る → って (買う → 買って, 待つ → 待って, 帰る → 帰って)

Here's the intuition, not just the chart: the sounds cluster by what's easy to say. The nasal-ish endings (ぶ・む・ぬ) all collapse into んで, and the "small tongue-tap" endings (う・つ・る) all collapse into って. You're not memorizing five random rules — you're memorizing three sound families.

```jp
書いて
飲んで
待って
```

*writing / drinking / waiting (all te-form)*

**The irregulars** are just two verbs, and you already know both. 来る becomes 来て, and する becomes して. 行く looks godan but breaks the く→いて rule — it goes to 行って, not `行いて`. That one exception bites everyone, so tuck it away now.

Put it all together and you can chain a whole morning:

```jp
毎朝起きて、朝ご飯を食べます。
```

*Every morning I get up and eat breakfast.*

Notice again: only the *last* verb is 食べます. Everything before it rides on the て形, and the politeness plus the tense live entirely in that final verb.

## Rules and exceptions

The biggest thing to understand: **the て形 carries no tense and no politeness by itself.** It's a hinge. Whether the sentence is polite or plain, present or past, is decided by the verb at the very end.

That means to talk about the past, you don't touch the て形 at all — you only change the final verb.

```jp
起きて、朝ご飯を食べました。
```

*I got up and ate breakfast.*

Same 起きて as before. Only 食べます turned into 食べました, and suddenly the whole thing is in the past. The て form never conjugates for tense — resist the urge to invent a past te-form.

> 行く → 行って is the one you'll get wrong. By the く→いて rule you'd expect `行いて`, but it isn't. It's 行って, patterning with the う・つ・る family instead.

One more constraint: don't use ます-form before the て. Learners fresh off the polite endings sometimes write `食べますて`. That's not a thing. The て attaches to the plain verb stem, never to ます.

## Casual speech

The te-form itself doesn't contract much, but it has one very common everyday job: it becomes a soft command or request on its own. Said with a rising, gentle tone, a bare て形 means "do this, please" in casual speech.

```jp
起きて。
```

*Get up. / Wake up. (casual, spoken to someone close)*

You'd say this to a family member who's oversleeping, not to your boss. It's warm and informal. The fuller polite request form is coming later, but this stripped-down version is what you'll actually hear at home.

## Contrast

You already know ます, ました, and ません — the polite endings that finish a sentence and lock in the tense. The te-form lives in a completely different spot: it never finishes a sentence (except the casual command above), it *connects* to more.

Compare a sentence that stops after one action with one that chains two:

```jp
毎朝、朝ご飯を食べます。
```

*Every morning I eat breakfast.*

That's complete — ます closes it. Now put an action *before* it, and the earlier verb has to become て形 to make room:

```jp
昨日起きて、コーヒーを飲みました。
```

*Yesterday I got up and drank coffee.*

The final verb 飲みました handles "yesterday" and the past tense for both actions. 起きて just links in front of it. Swap the final verb's ending — to 飲みます, 飲みません — and you've changed the whole sentence's tense or polarity without ever retouching 起きて. That's the division of labor: **te-form connects, the final verb decides.**

Hold onto this. Once you meet the plain past た形 soon, you'll notice the te-form and the ta-form are built from nearly the same sound rules — learn て cleanly now and た comes almost free.

That's the whole pattern: bend the earlier verb to て形, let the last verb carry tense and politeness, and you can string your day together in one breath.
