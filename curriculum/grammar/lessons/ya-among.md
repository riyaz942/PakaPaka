You just walked into a massive, sprawling shopping mall. The store shelves are overflowing with hundreds of different sections, and you spend an entire afternoon just browsing. Later that evening, a friend asks you what you looked at.

If you try to list every single thing you saw, you are going to be talking for a long, long time. You don't want to recount the entire floor plan of the building. You just want to give them a sampler platter of your day: "I looked at books and magazines, things like that."

When you first start learning a language, you often feel a bit like a lawyer. You have to be overly precise because your grammatical toolbox is still small. If someone asks what you did or what you saw, you try to list every single thing to be completely accurate. Alternatively, you leave things out and feel a slight twinge of guilt, as if you are hiding the truth.

But native speakers do not talk like lawyers in their everyday lives. They speak in broad, relaxed strokes. They give a few key details, a couple of prominent examples, and then they let the listener's imagination fill in the rest of the gaps.

You already know how to use the particle `と` to say "and." But `と` has a massive catch: it creates a closed, airtight loop. If you say you looked at books `と` magazines, you are telling your friend that you looked at exactly those two things, and absolutely nothing else in the entire store. If you actually looked at photographs as well, using `と` makes your statement technically untrue.

When you want to give a few examples without claiming it is the whole exhaustive list, you need a different grammatical tool. Enter the particle `や`.

## How the open list works

The particle `や` sits right between two nouns, in the exact same physical spot where `と` would normally go. But instead of locking the list down and throwing away the key, `や` props the door wide open. It tells your listener, "Here are a couple of examples of what is going on, but please do not hold me to this being everything."

```jp
本や雑誌を読みます。
```
*I read books, magazines, and things like that.*

By using `や` instead of `と`, you leave a little ghost space at the end of your sentence. The listener inherently knows that there might also be newspapers, manga, or internet articles involved in your reading habits, even though you did not bother to explicitly name them.

Sometimes, you want to make that "ghost space" a little more obvious. You can do this by sticking the word `など` at the very end of your list, right after the final noun. The word `など` translates roughly to "etcetera," "and so on," or "and the like."

```jp
朝ご飯にパンや果物などを食べます。
```
*I eat bread, fruit, and the like for breakfast.*

Adding `など` does not dramatically change the core meaning of the sentence—the `や` already did the heavy lifting of making the list open-ended—but it rounds out the phrasing beautifully. It acts like a little verbal wave of the hand, confirming out loud that the list trails off into other unspoken items. You will hear native speakers use this combination constantly.

Grammatically, when you use `Noun A + や + Noun B`, Japanese treats that entire clustered chunk as one single, giant mega-noun. The grammatical machinery that comes afterward—like the object marker `を` or the subject marker `が`—does not care how many items are packed inside that basket. It just attaches to the very end of the cluster (or to `など`, if you used it) and treats the whole thing exactly the same way it would treat a single, solitary word.

## The rules of the sampler platter

Japanese grammar is notoriously strict about keeping different kinds of words in their own separate lanes. English is quite lazy by comparison. In English, the word "and" is a universal Swiss Army knife. We use it to connect nouns ("cats and dogs"), adjectives ("big and red"), and verbs ("I ate and slept").

Japanese absolutely does not work this way. The most important rule you must remember for `や` is that it **only connects nouns**.

You cannot use it to connect adjectives, and you absolutely cannot use it to connect verbs. If you want to say that you wake up and eat, you have to use an entirely different grammatical machine. Using `起きるや食べる` is a fast track to sounding completely broken. A native speaker will hear the `や` and immediately expect another noun; when a verb comes out instead, their brain will skip a beat. Keep this particle strictly confined to people, places, and things.

> You might be tempted to string together four, five, or six nouns with `や`, just like you might freely do in English ("I bought apples and bananas and oranges and grapes"). Resist this urge. In Japanese, chaining more than two or three items with `や` sounds extremely clunky, robotic, and overwhelming. Give two strong examples and let the particle do the rest of the unspoken work.

Let's look at another scenario, this time using locations instead of objects. You are planning your weekend out in the city and chatting with a coworker about your plans.

```jp
明日、海や公園へ行きます。
```
*Tomorrow, I will go to the sea, a park, and so on.*

This paints a picture of a relaxed, flexible, and open itinerary. If you end up going to a movie theater or a coffee shop as well, you haven't broken any promises or lied about your weekend, because the `や` kept your schedule completely open to additions.

## The closed box vs. the open basket

Because you now have two different ways to say "and" with nouns, let's put them right next to each other so you can truly feel the difference. This is what language teachers call a minimal pair: two sentences that are completely identical except for the one single piece of grammar we are swapping out.

First, let's look at the particle you already know, the exhaustive (closed list) `と`.

```jp
今日、肉と魚を買います。
```
*Today, I will buy meat and fish.*

Think of `と` as a closed box. Imagine you are running an errand for a very strict chef. If you give them this sentence, you are stating as an absolute fact that your entire grocery purchase will consist of meat and fish, and absolutely zero other items. There is no room for interpretation. If you also buy vegetables, you have violated the boundaries of the `と` box.

Now, swap in our new pattern, `や`.

```jp
今日、肉や魚などを買います。
```
*Today, I will buy meat, fish, and so on.*

This is the open basket. If you are just describing your general grocery shopping plans to a friend, this is the one you want to use. It means that meat and fish are the headliners of your shopping trip, but you are probably also going to pick up some water, maybe some vegetables, or whatever else catches your eye. You are simply painting a general picture of your errands.

If you are ever trying to decide which one to use in a live conversation, take a split second to ask yourself: "Am I giving an exact, restricted inventory, or am I just giving a few examples?" If it is a strict inventory, use `と`. If it is just a sampler platter, reach for `や`.

## Casual speech and structural glue

In casual Japanese, especially when you are speaking quickly with close friends, you probably already know that speakers love to drop grammatical particles. Target markers like `に`, object markers like `を`, and subject markers like `が` frequently vanish into thin air when the context makes it obvious what is happening.

However, `や` is structural glue. It holds the core meaning of the nouns together, so it does not get dropped.

Imagine you are hanging out with a friend, and they ask what you usually drink on a hot day.

```jp
水やお茶飲む。
```
*I drink water, tea, and so on. (Casual)*

Notice how the object marker `を` is completely gone, which is incredibly common in casual speech, but the `や` remains untouched. It is essential for letting your friend know that water and tea are just a few options among many.

## Connecting people

Finally, keep in mind that this open basket is not just for objects and places. `や` works beautifully with people, too. Imagine you are at a large school event and you are describing the massive crowd gathering in the courtyard.

```jp
学校で先生や学生に会います。
```
*I meet teachers, students, and others at school.*

You might also meet parents, station staff, or police officers on your commute, but teachers and students are the primary groups you are choosing to highlight. The target particle `に` clips right onto the end of the `先生や学生` block without skipping a beat.

Hold onto this concept of making open-ended lists. Right now, you are building these lists with `や`. Soon, you will meet the particle `も` (also / too), which you can use to explicitly stack even more additions onto these kinds of open-ended scenarios. Until then, enjoy the freedom of not having to list every single noun in your vocabulary!
