Your phone buzzes. A friend texts: 出かける？ You already have your かばん in one hand and your 靴 on your feet, standing right by the ドア, keys warm from your palm. There's only one honest answer, and it isn't a statement of general intent — it's a report on exactly where you are, right this second.

```jp
今、出るところです。
```

*I'm just about to leave.*

Not 出ます, which only states a plan with no sense of *when*. ところ does something narrower and more useful: it freezes the clock and names the exact instant you're standing in — a beat before an action starts, right in the thick of it, or a beat after it's already ended. You've probably met ところ before as a plain noun meaning "place" — 図書館の近くのところ, roughly "the area near the library." This grammar use borrows that same idea and narrows it, from physical space down to a single point in time: not where you're standing, but where you're standing *inside the action*.

## How it works

### The moment before: dictionary form + ところ

Attach ところ straight onto a verb's plain dictionary form and you get the instant right before the action starts. Everything is lined up for it — you've decided, your body is already halfway into motion — but nothing has actually happened yet. That's the shape in the opening line above, 今、出るところです. Here's the same shape with a different verb.

```jp
今、電話するところです。
```

*I'm just about to make a phone call.*

You can feel why 出ます and 出るところです land so differently now. 出ます could be true at any point today. 出るところです is only true for the few seconds you're actually saying it.

### The middle: ~ているところ

Swap in ている, the ongoing-action form you already know from describing habits and states, and ところ moves right along with it. Now you're not standing before the action — you're inside it, mid-motion, and someone asking you a question would be interrupting.

```jp
今、勉強しているところです。
```

*I'm right in the middle of studying.*

```jp
今、掃除しているところです。
```

*I'm right in the middle of cleaning.*

This is the version you'll reach for most in daily conversation, because "I'm doing X right now" is a far more common thing to report than "I'm about to" or "I just did." Notice that ている alone would already mean roughly the same thing — ところ just sharpens the focus onto this precise moment, the way pointing at a spot on a map is sharper than gesturing at a whole city.

### The moment after: た-form + ところ

Use the plain past た-form you already know from ことがある, and ところ steps to the other side of the action — the instant right after it ended, close enough that there's no real gap between finishing and speaking.

```jp
今、朝ご飯を食べたところです。
```

*I just finished eating breakfast.*

```jp
今、宿題をしたところです。
```

*I just finished my homework.*

Say either of these an hour late and something feels off, the way it feels off to say "I just woke up" at three in the afternoon. たところ is honest about the clock — it only works when the gap really is close to zero.

The shape never changes once you know which side of the action you're describing: keep whichever verb form fits the moment, then set ところ right after it.

```jp
出る → 出るところ
```

*leave → about to leave*

## Rules and exceptions

ところ describes one instant, not a stretch of time, and it doesn't get along with words that imply repetition, like 毎日 (every day). Something that happens over and over isn't a single moment you can point at — `毎日勉強しているところです` reads like a contradiction, because "every day" pulls toward a habit while ところ insists on right now, once, and nowhere else.

> ところ only ever follows a conjugated verb — never a plain adjective sitting on its own. `暑いところです` isn't a way to say "it's hot right now"; for that you just need the adjective and です by themselves (今、暑いです).

In casual speech, the です at the end can drop the way it does after most plain-form sentences, so a friend might just say 今、出るところ with the sentence trailing off. But ところ itself never contracts or shortens — it's the polite ending around it that relaxes, not the word doing the actual work.

## Contrast

You already know ばかり for "just did" — Ta-form + ばかり. It sits close enough to たところ that it's easy to reach for the wrong one without noticing. たところ insists on zero gap: you're speaking in almost the same breath as the action ending. ばかり is looser — the action could have wrapped up a few minutes ago, even longer, and you'd still reach for it as long as it still feels recent to you.

```jp
宿題をしたばかりです。
```

*I only just finished my homework (and it still feels recent).*

Say it the instant the last page closes, and 宿題をしたところです is exact. Say it an hour later, still a little pleased with yourself, and 宿題をしたばかりです is the one that's honest — たところ at that point would be quietly lying about the clock, claiming a freshness that's already worn off.

ところ is a stopwatch, not a calendar — it only ever names the instant you're standing in, whether that's a beat before an action, right inside it, or a beat after it lets go of you. Hold onto that instinct for freezing a single moment; you'll want it again soon, when 間 asks you to describe not one instant but the whole stretch of time something else was happening around it.
