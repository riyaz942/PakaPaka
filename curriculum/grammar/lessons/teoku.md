Your friend is coming over tonight, and right now the room is a mess and the fridge is empty. You've got a couple of hours before they show up, which means everything you do now is really *for later* — you're not cleaning because someone's watching, you're cleaning so that later, when they arrive, it's already done.

Japanese has a dedicated way to mark that kind of action: te-form + おく.

```jp
部屋を掃除しておく。
```
*I'll clean the room ahead of time.*

The cleaning happens now, but the sentence's whole point is the *later* — you're doing it so that future-you doesn't have to.

## How it works

`おく` on its own is a normal verb meaning "to put / place" (置く — you'll have seen 置く with this exact kanji). Attach the te-form of any verb to it, and the meaning shifts from "put something down" to "do this now, for a reason that shows up later."

```jp
ビールを買っておく。
```
*I'll buy beer in advance.*

The structure is mechanical: take the te-form you already know, then add おく. 買う → 買って → 買っておく. Nothing about the te-form itself changes — おく just rides on the back of it.

```jp
窓を開けておく。
```
*I'll leave the window open (ahead of time).*

Notice something here: with an already-open-ended verb like 開ける, ておく can land on either of two related jobs — "do this in preparation" (I'll open it before the guests arrive) or "do this and then leave it that way" (I'm opening it and not touching it again). Context decides which one you're hearing; the grammar itself doesn't distinguish them.

```jp
お金を持っておく。
```
*I'll have money on me (just in case).*

That "just in case" flavor is common — ておく covers not just "prepare for a known event" but also "take care of this now so I'm not caught out later," a slightly broader net than "prepare" alone suggests.

## Rules and exceptions

おく conjugates exactly like the plain verb 置く does, because that's what it is — a u-verb ending in く. So you get おく → おかない → おきます → おいて, all the ordinary patterns. The only thing special is that it's riding on a te-form host, not standing alone.

> A very common wrong guess is treating おく like an auxiliary that doesn't conjugate — writing something like `掃除しておくます` instead of 掃除しておきます. おく still takes its own conjugation ending; only the verb in front of it stays frozen in te-form.

ておく wants a *deliberate* action with a *purpose* pointing forward. It's a poor fit for things that are simply true or that just happened to end up a certain way — for that job you already have てある and ている, which we'll get to in the contrast section below.

## Casual speech

In relaxed spoken Japanese, ておく contracts hard — the て and おく fuse into とく. It's extremely common; you'll hear it constantly among friends and family.

```jp
窓、開けとく。
```
*I'll leave the window open.*

Same meaning as 窓を開けておく, just faster and more casual — drop the と for です/ます-level speech, since とく reads as clearly informal.

## Contrast

You've met てある for "has been done, and the result is sitting there" — the difference is *whose intention the sentence is tracking*. てある describes the state as a fact you're reporting; ておく describes the deliberate act of setting that state up, with your reason for doing so implied.

```jp
窓が開けてある。
```
*The window has been left open.*

Compare that to 窓を開けておく from earlier — same window, same open state, but that version is about *you doing the opening on purpose, now, for later*. てある just notes that it's open; it doesn't care why or by whom.

ている (progressive) is a different trap entirely — it's about an action unfolding in real time, not something staged for the future.

```jp
今、部屋を掃除している。
```
*I'm cleaning the room right now.*

Swap しておく in for している here and the sentence stops describing what's happening this second — it starts describing something you're doing *because of what comes next*, which is a completely different scene.

ておく is the one you reach for whenever the real content of the sentence is "I'm doing this now so that later goes smoothly" — hold onto that shape, because てしまう later covers the flip side: doing something now and living with the consequences, whether you meant to or not.
