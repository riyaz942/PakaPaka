Picture texting a friend about Saturday's get-together: "Is everyone coming?" Almost everyone — one person can't make it. You want to say "everyone except Ken," and Japanese has a clean, single word for that job: 以外 (igai), meaning "except" or "other than." It attaches directly onto whichever noun you're pulling out of the group, so you never have to build a whole clause around it.

```jp
私以外はみんな来ます。
```
*Everyone except me is coming.*

Notice what happened there: 以外 sits right after 私 (me) — no verb, no connector, just a person and then the word that says "not counting this one." That's the entire shape of the pattern: **Noun + 以外**.

## How it works

以外 always follows a noun — whatever you're carving out of a larger group. Once it's attached, everything else in the sentence is understood to apply to the rest of that group, not to the noun sitting in front of 以外.

If what you want to exclude is an action rather than a thing, you can't hand 以外 a bare verb — it needs a noun first, so you build one with こと (a little word that turns a whole verb phrase into a thing you can talk about):

```jp
泳ぐ → 泳ぐこと
```
*to swim → swimming (the thing itself)*

```jp
泳ぐこと以外、スポーツが苦手です。
```
*Other than swimming, I'm bad at sports.*

Two shapes show up constantly, and they're really just 以外 riding on a particle you already know:

- **以外に** — leans toward "besides / in addition to." Common with a positive verb ("besides X, also Y") and just as common with a negative one ("nothing besides X").
- **以外は** — leans toward "as for everything except X," setting X up as the one exception to whatever comes next.

```jp
音楽以外に趣味がありません。
```
*I don't have a hobby besides music.*

```jp
日本語以外に英語も話せます。
```
*Besides Japanese, I can also speak English.*

```jp
土曜日以外は毎日勉強します。
```
*I study every day except Saturday.*

に and は aren't doing anything new here — they're the same particles you've used since week one. に leans toward "additionally," は leans toward "as for the rest of the group." Neither one changes what 以外 itself means; they just tint the sentence around it.

## Rules and exceptions

The one rule that trips people up: 以外 wants a noun immediately in front of it. In English, "except" can follow almost any kind of phrase, but Japanese draws a harder line. A bare plain-form verb can't feed straight into 以外.

> Don't write `泳ぐ以外` on its own to mean "other than swimming." A verb needs to be turned into a noun first — usually with こと — before 以外 can attach: 泳ぐこと以外.

以外 pairs naturally with a negative predicate to mean "only this one." That's not really a separate rule — it's just what "not X except Y" collapses to once you say it out loud:

```jp
彼以外は誰も知りません。
```
*No one knows except him.*

Read literally, that's "as for other than him, nobody knows" — which lands as "only he knows." Once you notice that shape, 以外 plus a negative keeps showing up wherever the real meaning is "only."

> 以外 marks what's outside the group — it says nothing about how big the group itself is.

```jp
魚以外は好きです。
```
*I like everything except fish.*

That sentence tells you fish is out. It doesn't tell you whether "everything" means two dishes or twenty — 以外 draws the boundary, not the headcount.

以外 doesn't care about politeness level either. It sits comfortably in plain speech and in です・ます sentences without changing shape, so you don't need a separate casual version to learn — the word itself never bends. In a quick spoken answer, people will sometimes drop the は or に and let bare 以外 carry the whole thought as a fragment — someone asks what you don't eat, and 魚以外。 is a complete answer on its own.

## Contrast

Everything else you've learned so far — それでも, そこで, そのため, すると — links two full sentences together. 以外 works differently: it's a small word riding directly on a noun, not a bridge between clauses. You can't swap it in for one of those connectors and expect the sentence to hold together; they're built for different jobs.

Where they do overlap is in mood, and それでも is the closest one to check yourself against. それでも says "even given that, X still happened" — it concedes something and pushes past it:

```jp
頭が痛かった。
それでも仕事に行った。
```
*I had a headache.*
*Even so, I went to work.*

以外 doesn't concede anything; it just draws a line around one item and says "not this one." If you ever reach for それでも to mean "except for this," that's the moment to reach for 以外 instead — それでも answers "what happened anyway?" while 以外 answers "what's left out?"

以外 also isn't the same word as 他 (ほか), even though both get translated as "other." 他 is a plain noun — "another one" — that can stand on its own and take its own particles, like a subject or an object. 以外 never stands alone; it only exists attached to the noun it's excluding.

This is worth holding onto beyond today: 以外 belongs to a small family of patterns that bolt straight onto a noun instead of linking two sentences. You'll meet another member of that family at N3 in 以来, which uses the same "Noun +" shape to mean "ever since."
