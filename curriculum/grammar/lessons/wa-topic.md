You're at a friend's place, pointing at yourself to introduce who you are. You want to say "I'm a student." You've got the words — 私 for *I*, 学生 for *student* — but how do you glue them together so it sounds like a real sentence and not two nouns dropped on the floor? That glue is は, the topic marker.

は is the very first particle most learners meet, and for good reason: it's how Japanese says "okay, here's what I'm talking about — now let me tell you something about it." You name your topic, stick は after it, and then make your comment.

```jp
私は学生です。
```

*I'm a student.*

That's the whole shape. 私 is the topic, は marks it, and 学生です is the comment about it. Notice one thing right away that trips everyone up the first week: this particle is written with the character は but pronounced *wa*, not *ha*. That's normal — it's a historical spelling quirk, and it only happens when は is doing this topic job.

## How it works

The structure is simple: **Noun + は + comment**. The noun is what you want to talk about. は announces it as the topic. Everything after は is your statement about that topic.

Think of は as drawing a line: "As for this thing — here's the deal." A loose but useful translation of は is "as for." *As for me, student.* Clunky in English, perfectly natural in Japanese.

```jp
これは本です。
```

*This is a book.*

Here これ (this one) is the topic, and 本です (is a book) is the comment. The comment doesn't have to be short, and it doesn't have to be a です sentence either — but everything you know so far ends in です, so that's what we'll lean on.

You can make the topic a person's name, a pronoun, or any noun at all:

```jp
彼は学生です。
```

*He is a student.*

```jp
あなたは学生です。
```

*You are a student.*

The pattern never changes based on what the topic is. は itself doesn't conjugate, doesn't shift form, doesn't care whether the topic is singular or plural. It's one of the most stable pieces of machinery in the whole language — it just sits there marking the topic, every single time.

## Rules and exceptions

The biggest thing to internalize: は marks the **topic**, not necessarily the grammatical subject. In simple sentences these overlap so much that you won't feel the difference yet, but keep the word "topic" in your head. It'll matter soon.

Because は sets up "what we're talking about," Japanese loves to drop things once the topic is clear. If it's obvious you're talking about yourself, you can leave off 私は entirely and just say 学生です. That's not lazy — it's how the language breathes. The topic, once established, doesn't need repeating.

> は is pronounced *wa* when it's the topic marker, even though it's written with the character for *ha*. When は is just part of a word (not a particle), it's read *ha* normally. This one spelling quirk confuses everyone at first — you're not misreading it.

One more caution: don't sprinkle は onto every noun in a sentence. A sentence usually has **one** topic. Marking two different nouns with は in the same breath will sound strange unless you're deliberately drawing a contrast, which is a move you'll learn later.

## Casual speech

Good news here: は as a topic marker stays は in casual speech. It doesn't contract or drop the way some particles do. What changes around it is the *comment* — casual speakers often drop the です at the end.

```jp
これは本。
```

*This is a book. (casual)*

Same topic, same は, just a more relaxed comment with です chopped off. So the particle you're learning now carries straight over into casual speech unchanged — one less thing to relearn later.

## Contrast

You haven't met other particles yet, so there's nothing to line は up against just now. But here's the tease worth holding onto.

Very soon you'll meet が, the subject marker. It looks like it does the same job as は — and the difference between は and が is one of the genuinely hard, genuinely famous puzzles in Japanese. The short version: は says "as for X, here's the news," while が points a spotlight and says "*X* is the one." You don't need that distinction yet, but plant a flag here, because when が arrives, the first question will be "wait, how is this different from は?"

You'll also soon meet を for marking the object of a verb, and の for linking nouns together. All three live in the same neighborhood as は — little particles doing the quiet structural work — but は is the one that sets the stage for everything else.

Recap: name your topic, add は (say it *wa*), then comment. That's the backbone of the Japanese sentence, and you'll use it in nearly everything you ever say.
