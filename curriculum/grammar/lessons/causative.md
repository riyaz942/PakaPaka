Your little brother is staring at a plate of vegetables like it personally insulted him. You've tried asking nicely. You've tried bargaining. Tonight you're done negotiating — he's going to eat them, whether he likes it or not. English hands you one verb, "make," and expects tone of voice to do the rest. Japanese hands you a whole new verb form built for exactly this moment.

That form is the causative — a version of the verb that puts *you* in charge of someone else's action instead of describing your own. Where 食べる means "eat," 食べさせる means "make (someone) eat" — or, in gentler situations, "let (someone) eat." Same ending, two very different dinners.

```jp
母は弟に野菜を食べさせる。
```

*Mother makes her little brother eat vegetables.*

You'll hear this shape constantly from whoever gets to call the shots in a given relationship — parents, teachers, doctors, bosses. It isn't rude on its own; it's just the grammar of someone directing someone else's action, the way "have someone do something" works in English. Whether the mood underneath is a stern order or a small kindness is a separate question, one you'll unpack a little further down.

## How it works

The causative attaches to the same verb stem you already know from potential and passive — you're not learning a new attachment point, just a new thing to attach.

For an ichidan verb (the ones ending in an -eru or -iru you can just drop), knock off る and add させる:

```jp
食べる → 食べさせる
```

*to eat → to make (someone) eat*

For a godan verb, shift the final syllable back from the "u" row to the "a" row, then add せる — the exact same vowel shift you already did for potential and passive, just with a different auxiliary bolted on:

```jp
行く → 行かせる
```

*to go → to make (someone) go*

The two irregulars don't follow either pattern, so they're just worth memorizing outright:

```jp
する → させる
```

*to do → to make (someone) do*

```jp
来る → 来させる
```

*to come → to make (someone) come*

> 来させる looks like plain 来る with させる stuck on, but the reading underneath shifts — 来る is くる, while 来させる is こさせる. It's the same quirk you've already met in 来ない (こない); the kanji stays put, the reading moves.

Once you've built the causative verb, it behaves like any other verb ending in -eru — it takes ます, ない, た, all the endings you already use every day. 食べさせます, 食べさせない, 食べさせた. Nothing new to learn there.

That する rule pays off well beyond the bare verb "to do." Every noun-plus-する verb you know — 掃除する, 勉強する, 練習する — swaps its する for させる the exact same way: 掃除させる, 勉強させる, 練習させる. You've already memorized dozens of these nouns; you're not adding new vocabulary, just reusing the one irregular conjugation across all of them.

The one genuinely new decision is which particle marks the person being made to act. It depends on whether the verb is *transitive* — one that takes a direct object, like 食べる (eat *something*) — or *intransitive*, one that doesn't, like 走る (run). With a transitive verb, the object keeps its を and the person doing the acting gets に, same as sentence one above: 弟に野菜を. With an intransitive verb, there's no object competing for を, so the person takes it instead.

```jp
先生は学生を走らせる。
```

*The teacher makes the students run.*

```jp
私は弟に薬を飲ませる。
```

*I make my little brother drink the medicine.*

Notice 走る has nobody else to give を to, so 学生 takes it. 飲む already has 薬 sitting on を, so 弟 gets に instead. Once you see the object competing for the particle, the split stops feeling arbitrary — check whether the verb already needs a を, and hand the leftover particle to whoever's being made to act.

This scales to any verb you already know without extra memorizing. 話す has an object (you speak *something*), so its causee takes に. 泳ぐ doesn't, so its causee takes を. You're not learning a new fact per verb — you're just asking the one question you already know how to answer from ordinary sentences: does this verb take an object or not?

## Rules and exceptions

Nothing in the grammar itself tells you whether a causative sentence is bossy or generous — that's entirely down to context. The same structure that forces your brother to eat vegetables can just as easily describe a parent being soft.

```jp
母は子供を公園で遊ばせる。
```

*Mother lets her child play in the park.*

Nobody has to twist a child's arm to get them into a park. Swap the scene to homework and the identical grammar reads as strict instead of kind — the sentence doesn't change; your mental picture of the situation does.

> The single most common slip is dropping the さ on an ichidan verb — writing `食べせる` instead of 食べさせる. Ichidan causatives are always -させる, never a bare -せる; the shorter form only happens on godan verbs, where せる is already doing all the work after the vowel shift.

The causee particle rule has no exceptions worth worrying about, but it does have one boundary worth stating outright: whatever the verb, the person being made or allowed to act is never marked with が. That particle stays reserved for the thing someone wants, likes, or is capable of — categories that belong to entirely different patterns, not this one. If you find が creeping into a causative sentence, you've drifted into potential without meaning to.

Because the causative puts you above someone else in the sentence, it can land harshly if you aim it at a superior — a boss, a teacher, someone older. Softening that is a matter of surrounding vocabulary and tone rather than a rule the grammar itself enforces, so for now, just notice that the bare causative describes *who's directing whom*, not how anyone involved feels about it.

## Contrast

You've actually met れる・られる twice already — once for potential, once for passive — and both times it attached to the same stem you're using now. せる・させる sits right next to them structurally, which is exactly why it's easy to blur the three together under pressure. The fix is remembering who the sentence is about: potential and passive both put the subject on the receiving end of the verb; causative puts the subject in the driver's seat, making the action happen *to* someone else.

```jp
弟は野菜が食べられる。
```

*My little brother can eat vegetables.*

食べられる and 食べさせる are one vowel apart and mean almost opposite things — one says he's capable of it, the other says somebody's forcing the issue. When you catch yourself unsure which one you meant, ask who's actually chewing. If it's the subject of the sentence, you want potential. If the subject is pulling strings from outside the action, you want causative. Notice, too, that 野菜 sits on が in the potential sentence and on を in the causative one — the particle swap you just learned to spot for causees works as a quiet tell for which pattern you're looking at.

The same test travels to any verb pair you already know. Take 走らせる from earlier: swap さ for れ and you'd be saying the student is capable of running, 走れる, not that anyone ordered him onto the track. The vowel is the whole difference between an ability and an instruction.

Sometimes the two even stack: someone gets made to do something, and *they're* the one stuck experiencing it rather than causing it. That flip side — being on the receiving end of somebody else's causative — gets its own form soon: せられる・させられる, the causative-passive. Today's job is just getting comfortable putting other people in the driver's seat you're steering from. The version where somebody else steers *you* can wait.
