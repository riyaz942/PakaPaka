Your phone buzzes. It's your mom, and she's talking fast. You hang up, and your roommate asks what she wanted. You don't need to act out the whole call. You just need to hand over her words, and Japanese has one small particle for that: と.

Here is the call, repeated word for word:

```jp
母は「明日帰るよ」と言った。
```

*Mom said, "I'll be home tomorrow, you know."*

The brackets 「 」 hold her exact words, よ included. と closes the bracket and tells the listener "that was the thing she said". Then the verb 言う (to say) tells them what she did with those words.

You can also retell it in your own sentence, with no brackets at all:

```jp
母は、明日帰ると言った。
```

*Mom said she'd be home tomorrow.*

Same news, two styles. The first is a **direct quote** (her words, untouched). The second is an **indirect quote** (you report the content, and the brackets disappear). と does the same job in both. That is the whole pattern. The rest of this lesson is about what may sit in front of と and which verbs may come after it.

Why does Japanese need a particle here at all? Think about what happens in spoken English: your voice changes, or you write quotation marks. Japanese lets a small word do that work, so even a quote with no brackets and no pause has a clear edge. Everything before と is the content, and everything after it is you talking again.

## How it works

The shape never changes:

- **Quote** + と + 言う / 聞く / 書く

The three verbs cover the three things you can do with words: say them, ask them, or write them down. We will meet each one below.

First, the part in front of と. In an indirect quote it is a full sentence in **plain form**. That is the casual, dictionary-style form of a verb or adjective, the one with no ます or です attached. Think of it as the words with the politeness switched off. The reason is simple: a quote is a small sentence living inside your bigger sentence, and the small one is kept in its bare, unpolished state while the big one carries the politeness. Each word type looks like this before と:

- **Verbs** keep their plain form: 行く, 食べる, 行った (went), 行かない (won't go).
- **Nouns** take だ, the casual cousin of です: 学生だ, 先生だ.

Adjectives follow the same idea. You will add them here when you learn their plain forms.

Let's watch the noun case in action. Your brother points at someone across the street:

```jp
兄は、あの人は先生だと言った。
```

*My brother said that person is a teacher.*

先生 alone is not a full sentence, so in a retelling it needs its だ before it can stand in front of と. Keep the だ and the quote stays a complete thought.

Now the tense. English likes to shift "is" into "was" when you report something. Japanese does not. The quote keeps the tense of the original statement, while 言った is past simply because the speaking already happened. If what she reports already happened, the quote itself is past:

```jp
母は、昨日駅へ行ったと言った。
```

*Mom said she went to the station yesterday.*

This one trips everyone up the first week, and it's normal: your English brain wants to change the tense of the quote. Leave it alone. If it helps, picture yourself holding up the original sentence like a photograph and setting it inside yours without touching it.

## Rules and exceptions

### Asking and writing

言う is only one of the three verbs. 聞く works the same way, and in this slot it means *ask*:

```jp
母に「晩ご飯は何」と聞いた。
```

*I asked Mom, "What's for dinner?"*

There is no か and no question mark. The word 何 makes it a question, and the quote simply ends with と like any other. Be aware that 聞く also means *listen* or *hear*, so the surrounding words decide which one you get. A question word inside the quote is a strong hint that you are asking. In fast conversation you will also see the quote end in か, such as when you pass along a yes-or-no question, but for now the bare version above is the one to learn.

For written words, such as letters, notes and signs, use 書く:

```jp
父は手紙で、来週帰ると書いた。
```

*Dad wrote in his letter that he'd be home next week.*

### When you don't know what was said

Swap the whole quote for 何 and と still sits right behind it:

```jp
彼は何と言いましたか。
```

*What did he say?*

Here 何 stands in for his entire answer. Notice that 言いました is polite, because you are talking to someone else. That leads to the first sharp edge.

> In a retelling, the quoted words stay in plain form even inside a polite sentence. Politeness goes on the final verb only: 母は明日帰ると言いました is polite, but the quote is still 帰る, not 帰ります. Put 帰ります inside the quote only when Mom really did say it politely.

Next, the little word itself:

> Do not skip と. `母は「明日帰る」言った` gives the listener no signal that her words have ended, and it sounds broken. Even a very short quote needs it. If you only remember one habit from this lesson, make it this one: quote, と, verb, every time.

### Honest about the mess

Japanese drops subjects all the time, and quotes are no exception. 母は明日帰ると言った does not strictly tell you who is coming home. Most of the time it is Mom. In the right situation it could be someone she mentioned. The context carries that information, not the grammar.

The same slot also works for thoughts. 彼は明日来ると思う means "I think he'll come tomorrow". 思う is not a speech verb, but と packages a thought exactly the way it packages speech.

## Casual speech

In everyday talk, と often gets squashed into って. 母は明日帰るって言った means the same as the version with と. This is casual only, so keep と in anything you write for a teacher or a stranger.

You will also hear って alone at the end of a sentence, as in 明日帰るって. The verb has vanished completely, and the meaning becomes "I hear she's coming home tomorrow". Context supplies the missing 言う. Learners often hear this in the first week of real conversation and wonder where the verb went. Nothing is wrong. The speaker simply trusts you to fill in the obvious.

## Contrast: と, に and から

You already know に for the person on the receiving end. Verbs of speaking have a person on the receiving end too, and に marks them, while と marks the words themselves. Put both in one sentence and each does its own job:

```jp
母に「明日帰る」と言った。
```

*I told Mom, "I'll be home tomorrow."*

に points at Mom, the one who hears it. と points at what was said. Keep the two roles apart and you will never confuse them.

The tricky case is 聞く. Its meaning changes with the particle you put on the person:

```jp
母に聞いた。
```

*I asked Mom.*

```jp
母から聞いた。
```

*I heard it from Mom.*

に marks the one you asked. から (from) marks where the information came from, so this time you are the one receiving it. Same verb, opposite direction of travel. When a sentence is ambiguous at first glance, look at the particle on the person: に means you went to them, から means the information came to you from them.

One more neighbour is よ, the tag for emphasis. In the very first example, よ lives inside the brackets, so it is part of what Mom said. Move it outside, as in 母は明日帰ると言ったよ, and now it is you adding the emphasis: "Mom did say she'd be home tomorrow, I'm telling you."

## Closing thought

Put what was said, asked or written in front of と, finish with 言う, 聞く or 書く, keep a retold quote in plain form, and let に name the person. Hold onto this for later: when you meet より, the word for comparing, you will be able to report opinions like "she said this one is better than that one".
