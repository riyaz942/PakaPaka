You've been going to the pool every weekend since spring, and this morning you crossed the whole thing without stopping once. A friend asks how it's going. You could say 泳げます — it's true, and it's flat. It reports today and says nothing about the four months of not being able to do it. What you actually want is the word for the *arrival*.

```jp
私は泳げるようになりました。
```

*I've become able to swim.*

That's ようになる. It takes a verb and says: this wasn't happening before, and now it is. The whole pattern is about the line you crossed, not about where you're standing.

## How it works

The machinery is small. Take a verb in plain dictionary form, add よう, then attach なる — the same なる you already use for 寒くなる and 上手になる. よう by itself means something like *way* or *manner*, so the literal shape is "it becomes the way where you swim." That's clumsy English but a useful picture: the situation reshapes itself around the verb.

There are two flavours, and which one you get depends entirely on what you put in front of よう.

**Potential form + ようになる** is new ability. You couldn't, now you can. This is why the pattern shows up constantly in language-learning talk.

```jp
漢字が少し読めるようになりました。
```

*I've become able to read a little kanji.*

読める is the potential form you already know; ようになりました is what turns a static ability into a story with a before and an after.

**Dictionary form + ようになる** is a new habit or tendency. Nobody gained a skill here — the behaviour simply started happening where it didn't used to.

```jp
弟は毎朝早く起きるようになった。
```

*My younger brother has come to get up early every morning.*

He could always get up early. He just didn't. 毎朝 is doing a lot of work in that sentence — habit words like 毎日, 毎晩, よく and いつも sit very naturally with this flavour, because a habit needs repetition to be visible.

The subject doesn't have to be a person, and the change doesn't have to be anyone's project:

```jp
猫が私のベッドで寝るようになりました。
```

*The cat has taken to sleeping on my bed.*

Now the tail. なる is an ordinary う-verb, so it conjugates normally and carries the whole tense and politeness load for the sentence: ようになります, ようになりました, ようになった, ようになって. In practice you'll write the past form more than anything else, because you usually notice a change after it's finished happening. ようになります points at a change you expect later — after this course, after the operation, after next month.

```jp
一人で電車に乗れるようになりましたか。
```

*Have you become able to ride the train by yourself?*

For the negative — the verb *stops* happening — you can build 〜ないようになる, and it's grammatical:

```jp
父はコーヒーを飲まないようになりました。
```

*My father has stopped drinking coffee.*

Hold that one loosely, though. It's the form the textbook shows, and it's not what most people say out loud. More on that at the end.

## Rules and exceptions

The first sharp edge is the one everyone hits: **choosing the wrong flavour**. If you mean an ability and you write the dictionary form, you've said something different but perfectly grammatical, so nothing warns you. `泳ぐようになりました` means you started swimming — took it up, go regularly now. 泳げるようになりました means you couldn't and now you can. Two true sentences about the same summer, and only one of them is your sentence.

> Verbs that already carry a potential meaning inside them don't take a potential form first. 分かる, できる, 見える and 聞こえる go straight in: 分かるようになりました, できるようになりました. `分かれるようになる` is a form you'll be tempted to build once and should never build again.

The second edge is **politeness placement**. Everything before よう stays plain, always. `泳げますようになりました` is wrong in a way that sounds jarring to a native ear — です and ます can't live in the middle of the sentence like that. Put the politeness on なる, where it belongs, and leave the front end alone.

Third: this is a pattern about **change over time, not a single event**. A verb under ようになる should be repeatable or continuous. "I came to arrive at the station at 8:15 yesterday" isn't a thing anyone says in any language, and it isn't a thing ようになる can express either. If the event happened once, on one day, you want a plain past tense and nothing more.

Fourth, and this is where the pattern is genuinely mushy: ようになる leans towards changes that happened **to** you rather than changes you announced. Ability arriving after months of practice, a habit that crept in, a cat that made a decision. It's not a rule you can violate and get an error — plenty of native sentences use it for deliberate changes — but the default colour of the word is gradual and slightly outside your control, which is why だんだん sits so comfortably in front of it.

On casual speech there's less to say than you might expect: ようになる doesn't contract. What changes is the tail you already know how to change — ようになりました becomes ようになった, and that's the whole story. You'll occasionally hear the よう squeezed almost to nothing in fast speech, but there's no separate casual form to learn.

## Contrast

The closest thing you already know is ように, and the two are one syllable apart for a reason — they're the same よう, pointing in opposite directions in time. ように is the **goal you're aiming at**; ようになる is the **goal you reached**.

```jp
泳げるように毎日練習します。
```

*I practise every day so that I'll be able to swim.*

That's ように: the swimming hasn't happened yet, it's the reason for the practice. Swap the tail and the practice disappears from the sentence entirely, replaced by its outcome — 泳げるようになりました. If you find yourself unsure which you want, ask whether the verb in front of よう has actually happened. Not yet, and it's the point of some other effort? ように. Already true, and it wasn't before? ようになる.

The second comparison is with the bare potential form on its own. 泳げます is a **state**: true today, no claim about last year. 泳げるようになりました is an **event**: the moment the state switched on. This is why answering どうですか about your Japanese with 話せるようになりました is warmer than 話せます — you've told the person there was a struggle.

One last piece of housekeeping. You already say 上手になる and 元気になる without any よう, and you might reasonably wonder why verbs need the extra part. 日本語が上手になりました — *I've gotten good at Japanese* — is exactly that: no よう anywhere. Adjectives and nouns can attach to なる directly, because なる wants a description of a resulting state and that's exactly what they are. A verb isn't a description — it's an action — so よう does the conversion, packaging "swim" into "the state of things where swimming happens." Same なる, one extra bridge.

So: ようになる marks the switch flipping, potential form in front for a new ability, dictionary form for a new habit. And that negative you're holding loosely — 飲まないようになりました — is about to get a shorter, much more natural rival when we meet なくなる, which does the same job in fewer syllables and is what your father would actually say about the coffee.
