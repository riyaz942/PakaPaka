You are sitting at a table with a classmate, and you notice a book left behind. You want to ask who it belongs to, or perhaps declare that it belongs to you. In English, you would use an apostrophe-s ('s) or the word "of". 

In Japanese, you handle this with a single, incredibly useful particle: の.

## How it works

The particle の is a linker. Its primary job is to connect two nouns together, turning them into one single noun block. The most common way you will use this is to show possession.

The rule is strict: the owner or describing noun always comes first, followed by の, and then the thing being owned or described.

```jp
これは誰の本ですか。
```
*Whose book is this?*

```jp
それは私の本です。
```
*That is my book.*

Just like in English, you can chain these connections together as much as you need to. You just keep inserting の between the nouns.

```jp
私の友達の猫は魚を食べます。
```
*My friend's cat eats fish.*

## Dropping the obvious noun

Sometimes, repeating the noun feels clunky. If someone points to a coffee and asks whose it is, you do not need to reply "It is his coffee." You can just say "It is his."

Japanese does the exact same thing. You can drop the final noun completely, leaving の hanging at the end of the phrase, as long as the context makes it obvious what you are talking about.

```jp
このコーヒーは誰のですか。
```
*Whose coffee is this?*

```jp
彼のです。
```
*It is his.*

> When you drop the noun like this, の acts like "mine", "yours", "his", or "hers". Just remember that the item you are dropping must be clearly understood from the conversation.

## More than just ownership

Because Japanese rarely lets you smash two nouns directly together, you will use の for much more than just possession. It is the universal glue for any situation where one noun describes another.

If you want to say "Japanese teacher" or "school photographs", you cannot just say `日本語先生` or `学校写真`. You have to bridge them with の.

```jp
日本語の先生は明日来ます。
```
*The Japanese teacher is coming tomorrow.*

```jp
学校の写真を見ます。
```
*I will look at photos of the school.*

## Don't mix up the order

The biggest trap for English speakers is reversing the order when translating phrases that use the word "of". In English, you say "the window of the room". The main thing (the window) comes first.

In Japanese, the main thing you are talking about always comes last. 

If you say `部屋の窓`, you are talking about the room's window. If you accidentally say `窓の部屋`, you are talking about a room made of windows. Always put the modifier first.

## The difference a particle makes

Since you already know は (the topic marker), let's look at how replacing it with の completely changes your sentence. 

```jp
私は友達です。
```
*I am a friend.*

```jp
私の友達です。
```
*(They are) my friend.*

The first sentence uses は to make "I" the topic of the whole sentence. The second sentence uses の to link "I" and "friend" into a single concept before the sentence even finishes.

Now you can talk about all the things that belong to you or your friends. Hold onto this ability to link words together—it will be crucial when we learn how to talk about going *to* a specific location, like a friend's house, using に next.
