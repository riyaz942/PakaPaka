You are waiting at a coffee shop to meet a friend, and they are ten minutes late. You text another friend to ask if they know where he is. You need to tell them he hasn't shown up.

```jp
彼はまだ来ていません。
```
*He hasn't come yet.*

## How it works

To say you haven't done something *yet*, or that something hasn't happened *yet*, you use まだ (which means "still" or "not yet") followed by the て form of the verb, plus いない (or the polite いません).

```jp
私はまだ昼ご飯を食べていません。
```
*I haven't eaten lunch yet.*

Think of it as literally saying "the state of having eaten hasn't happened." Because the result of the action hasn't been achieved yet, we use the continuous state form (the て form) and make it negative.

```jp
あの映画はまだ見ていない。
```
*I haven't seen that movie yet.*

```jp
その本はまだ買っていません。
```
*I haven't bought that book yet.*

## Didn't do vs. Haven't done yet

This one trips people up a lot. Why not just use the regular past tense negative to say you haven't done something?

In Japanese, the past negative means the window of opportunity is firmly closed. The event is over. If you say `宿題をしませんでした` (I didn't do the homework) to a teacher, it sounds like "I didn't do it, and I'm not going to."

If you want to imply that you still plan to do it—that the window is still open—you must use the まだ〜ていない pattern.

```jp
まだ宿題をしていません。
```
*I haven't done the homework yet.*

> Always use まだ〜ていない if there is still a chance the action will happen. Using the simple past negative can accidentally sound defiant!

## Casual speech

In casual Japanese, speakers constantly drop the い in ていない. The pattern shrinks down to てない.

```jp
友達はまだ起きていない。
```
*My friend hasn't woken up yet.*

While the written sentence above keeps the い, in actual conversation with friends you will almost exclusively hear the contracted version: まだ起きてない.

## Connecting it up

Right now, you're learning how to talk about things that haven't happened yet. Soon, you'll learn the exact opposite pattern: もう〜ました (already did). Together, these two patterns will let you coordinate schedules, check on tasks, and make plans naturally. Keep まだ in your back pocket until then!
