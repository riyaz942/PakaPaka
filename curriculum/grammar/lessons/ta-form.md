You are at a friend's house. They point to an empty box and ask if you finished the sweets. You want to say "I ate it." If you say 食べました, you will sound like you are giving a formal report to your boss. You need the casual past tense.

This is where the た形 (ta-form) comes in. It is the plain, casual equivalent of ました. But here is the best news you will hear all day: if you survived learning the て-form (the connective form), you already know exactly how to make the た-form.

## How it works

The rule is completely identical to the て-form. You just take the て-form of any verb and swap the final て for **た**. If the te-form ends in で, you swap it for **だ**. That is the entire rule.

```jp
食べる → 食べて → 食べた
飲む → 飲んで → 飲んだ
買う → 買って → 買った
行く → 行って → 行った
```
*Eat becomes ate.*
*Drink becomes drank.*
*Buy becomes bought.*
*Go becomes went.*

Let's look at how this sounds in a normal conversation when you are talking to a friend about what you did recently.

```jp
昨日友達と昼ご飯を食べた。
先週海に行った。
```
*Yesterday, I ate lunch with a friend.*
*Last week, I went to the sea.*

## Rules and exceptions

Because this is the plain form, it is strictly for casual speech. You use this with your friends, your family, or people younger than you.

> Do not use the plain た-form alone at the end of a sentence with a teacher, a boss, or a stranger. It strips away all the politeness and will sound overly familiar. Stick to ました for those situations.

Also, keep in mind that this is only for affirmative past actions ("I did"). The casual negative past ("I didn't do") is a completely different pattern that builds on the ない-form instead.

## Contrast: ました vs た

Both of these patterns tell the listener that an action is finished and in the past. The only difference is your relationship with the listener.

Notice how the exact same meaning changes its outfit depending on who you are talking to. If you are reporting to your teacher, you use ました. If you are chatting with a friend, you drop down to た.

```jp
今日学校で先生に会いました。
今日学校で友達に会った。
```
*Today I met my teacher at school.*
*Today I met my friend at school.*

You also already know the て-form, which looks incredibly similar and shares the exact same conjugation rules. Remember that て connects ideas, while た finishes a thought in the past. You will often see them working together in the same sentence.

```jp
手紙を書いて、寝た。
```
*I wrote a letter, and went to sleep.*

Mastering the た-form unlocks a massive chunk of everyday, natural Japanese conversation. You will soon see that it isn't just for verbs, either — hold onto this concept, because we will soon see how adjectives have their own past tense rules to say things like "it *was* big". For now, get comfortable swapping your て for a た.
