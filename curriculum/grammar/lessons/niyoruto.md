You're deciding whether to bring an umbrella tomorrow, and you haven't looked at the sky yourself — you're going by what the newspaper said this morning. That's the whole job によると does: it hangs a claim off a source, so nobody mistakes the information for your own opinion or guess.

```jp
新聞によると、明日は雨です。
```
*According to the newspaper, tomorrow it will rain.*

You didn't decide it would rain. The newspaper did. によると is the little tag that keeps that distinction visible every time you repeat something you heard, read, or were told, instead of quietly presenting it as your own conclusion.

This matters more in Japanese conversation than English speakers expect. Stating something flatly, with no source attached, can sound like you're claiming personal authority over it — "it will rain tomorrow," full stop, as if you checked yourself. によると lets you soften that into "here's what I was told," which is both more honest and, often, more polite.

It also protects you. If the newspaper turns out to be wrong, "the newspaper said so" is a very different claim from "I said so." Attaching a source up front means you're never stuck defending information you never generated in the first place — you were just the messenger, and によると makes sure everyone knows it.

## How it works

によると attaches straight onto a noun — the person, place, or medium the information came from. No の, no な, nothing wedged in between; it just glues on directly, the way a label sticks to a jar.

You already know と for a natural, automatic result: if X happens, Y follows as a matter of course. によると is built from that same と. により is a slightly formal way of saying "based on / by way of," and stacking と onto it gives you "if you go by [source], then [what follows] comes out like this." Read that way, the literal shape of the phrase and its English meaning line up almost exactly — you're not memorizing an arbitrary chunk, you're recognizing a pattern you already know wearing a more formal outfit.

```jp
ラジオによると、今日は雪です。
```
*According to the radio, today it's snowing.*

The source doesn't have to be a single word. Any noun phrase can sit in front of によると, as long as it still names where the information came from rather than describing what happened.

```jp
友達の話によると、テストは来週です。
```
*According to what my friend said, the test is next week.*

友達の話 — "my friend's talk" — is one noun phrase, so によると attaches to the whole thing exactly the same way it attaches to 新聞 or ラジオ. Swap in 先生の話 or 医者の話 and nothing else about the sentence changes. A bare person also works just as well as a media source: a teacher, a doctor, anyone whose word you're relaying rather than your own.

```jp
先生によると、宿題は明日までです。
```
*According to the teacher, the homework is due tomorrow.*

```jp
医者によると、病気はすぐ治ります。
```
*According to the doctor, the illness will heal soon.*

Notice that what follows によると is always a plain, complete statement — 明日は雨です, 病気はすぐ治ります. によると doesn't reach into that statement and change its tense or its politeness level; it just frames where the statement came from. You can put a past-tense claim, a present-tense claim, or a plain guess after it, and the source phrase in front never has to match that tense. 医者によると is present tense, but the doctor could just as easily have been describing something that already happened — the tense of the source and the tense of the claim are independent of each other.

That independence is also why によると sits so comfortably at the very front of a sentence. It's setting up context — where this information is from — before you get to the actual news, the same way "according to the newspaper" leads an English sentence rather than trailing at the end.

## Rules and exceptions

によると only takes a noun. It can't hang off a full sentence the way から can — you can't say something like `雨が降るによると` and expect it to mean "according to the fact that it's raining." If the source you want to name is an event or a whole idea rather than a person, place, or piece of media, によると usually isn't the tool for the job; you'd need to turn that idea into a noun phrase first, the way 友達の話 turns "my friend talked" into something によると can attach to.

> Because によると always marks somebody else's information, `私によると` — literally "according to me" — sounds strange when it's meant as "in my opinion." The whole phrase assumes the claim is being relayed from an outside source, not generated on the spot by the speaker. For your own view, you'd reach for a completely different pattern, not によると.

によると also has a near-twin, によれば — same meaning, same attachment rule, fully interchangeable in most sentences. によれば leans a little more formal and shows up more often in writing, announcements, and news broadcasts than in casual chat, but nobody will blink if you use either one in either setting. Some learners assume the two must split some subtle difference in meaning, the way many near-synonym pairs do — here, that's mostly not the case. Pick whichever sounds smoother to your ear once you've been exposed to enough real sentences.

```jp
友達によれば、彼は元気です。
```
*According to my friend, he's doing well.*

That's the same structure as every other example so far — noun, then によれば, then the plain statement being relayed — just with the more formal-sounding half of the pair. Try reading it back with によると instead; nothing about the meaning shifts, only the register does, and only slightly.

## Contrast

から states your own reason for doing something; it's your logic, your conclusion. によると states someone else's information; you're not vouching for it, just passing it along. Swap one for the other and the sentence stops meaning what you intended, because they answer different questions — "why are you doing this?" versus "where did this claim come from?"

```jp
天気が悪いから、傘を持って行きます。
```
*Because the weather is bad, I'm bringing an umbrella.*

天気が悪いから is something you concluded yourself, probably from looking outside or checking the sky. 新聞によると is a fact somebody else supplied. If you tried to say 天気が悪いによると, it wouldn't parse as "because the weather is bad" — によると needs a noun sitting directly in front of it, not a whole clause, and から is doing a fundamentally different job in the first place: giving a reason, not naming a source.

について marks what's being talked about; によると marks where the talk came from. The two answer different questions and can even sit in the same conversation without stepping on each other.

```jp
天気について話しました。
```
*I talked about the weather.*

天気について話す just names the topic — weather — and says nothing about who told you what. That's the exact gap によると fills. Imagine reporting both pieces at once: what the conversation was about, and where the claim inside it came from. について handles the first job, によると the second, and neither one can do the other's work.

Put all of that together and by the time you're a few weeks past this lesson, the choice between によると, から, and について should feel less like a grammar rule and more like a reflex: ask yourself whether you're naming a source, giving your own reason, or flagging a topic, and the right particle chain more or less picks itself.

によると is a small, specific job: crediting a source before you hand over a claim. It's not about being formal for its own sake — it's about being accurate regarding whose information you're actually repeating. Hold onto that instinct; it comes back once we reach 限り, another phrase built around "as far as," except that one marks the edge of what you personally know rather than where you heard it.
