You are three weeks into a new job, and a friend asks how it's going. The honest answer isn't one thing — the pay is genuinely good, but you're barely home before nine most nights. English reaches for "but" and moves on without marking the shape of that sentence any further. Japanese has a word built specifically for this kind of honesty: 反面, literally "the opposite (反) side (面)" — the flip side of the same coin, not two unrelated facts you happen to be listing together.

```jp
彼女の新しい仕事は給料がいい反面、休暇が短い。
```

*Her new job pays well, but the flip side is that vacation is short.*

That's the whole idea: state one fact, attach 反面, then state the fact sitting on the other side of it. Both halves describe the same subject — the job — so 反面 isn't just noting that two things differ, it's pointing at a trade-off baked into one thing. Once you notice the shape, you'll hear it everywhere: good but, easy but, fast but — always two true things about one situation, pulling in different directions.

## How it works

After a verb or an i-adjective, 反面 attaches straight onto the plain form. No extra piece, no particle in between — you just say the fact, then 反面, then the other fact.

```jp
この店の料理はおいしい反面、値段が高い。
```

*This restaurant's food is delicious, but on the other hand the price is high.*

Negative plain forms work exactly the same way. 反面 doesn't care whether the fact attaching to it is positive or negative — only that it's a complete plain-form clause it can hook onto.

```jp
彼女はお酒を飲まない反面、よく食べる。
```

*She doesn't drink alcohol, but on the other hand she eats a lot.*

Na-adjectives are the one place the shape changes a little: you need な between the adjective and 反面, the exact same な you already use before any noun with a na-adjective.

```jp
この町は便利な反面、人が多い。
```

*This town is convenient, but on the other hand there are a lot of people.*

Potential verbs — the "can do X" forms like 話せる or 書ける — are just verbs as far as 反面 is concerned, so they attach the same way any plain verb does.

```jp
彼は日本語を話せる反面、書くのが苦手だ。
```

*He can speak Japanese, but on the other hand he's bad at writing.*

You'll also run into 反面 opening a fresh sentence as その反面, pointing back at something already said instead of attaching to the clause right before it. The logic is identical — it's the same flip-side idea, just given a full sentence break instead of a comma. Treat それ-style openings as the same pattern wearing a different collar; nothing about what 反面 is doing changes.

## Rules and exceptions

反面 only works when both halves are true about the same subject or topic at the same time. It isn't a general-purpose "but" for any two facts you happen to be putting next to each other — it specifically flags that one thing has two sides.

```jp
兄は頭がいい反面、あまり勉強しない。
```

*My older brother is smart, but on the other hand he barely studies.*

That sentence holds together because both facts belong to one person's one situation — smart, and yet barely studying. If the two halves described different people, or two events with no real connection, 反面 stops making sense and you'd reach for でも or しかし instead.

> Don't drop the な after a na-adjective. `便利反面` reads as broken — you need `便利な反面`, the same rule that governs every na-adjective standing in front of a noun.

反面 also leans formal and written. You'll see it constantly in essays, news writing, and careful spoken explanations where someone is laying out a considered trade-off. In a fast, casual conversation between friends, けど or でも usually carries the same contrast with far less weight behind it — reach for 反面 when the point is that this really is a built-in trade-off, worth stating plainly, not just a passing however.

One more thing worth being honest about: native speakers don't use 反面 for every small difference. If the gap between the two facts is minor or unremarkable, plain だが or でも does the job without making the contrast sound like a big deal. Save 反面 for the moments where the two sides genuinely feel like they belong to one thing and pull against each other.

## Contrast

You already know とか for stringing together loose, similar-feeling examples — things that belong to roughly the same category, without any claim about how they relate to each other.

```jp
彼は忙しいとか大変とか言っている。
```

*He keeps saying things like "I'm busy" and "it's rough."*

とか doesn't care whether the items it lists agree with each other — 忙しい and 大変 are just two flavors of the same complaint, tossed out loosely, no structure implied beyond "here are some examples." 反面 does close to the opposite job: it takes two facts that pull against each other and insists they both belong to one subject.

```jp
彼の仕事は忙しい反面、楽しい。
```

*His job is busy, but on the flip side, it's fun.*

Swap 反面 for とか in that sentence and the meaning falls apart. 忙しいとか楽しいとか言っている would just mean he vaguely mentions being busy and having fun sometimes, with no claim that the two sit in tension. 反面 is doing real work in the original: busy and fun, both true, pulling in different directions, about the same job.

Hold onto that feeling of two things pulling against each other inside one fact — you'll meet it again soon in ところが, for when the turn is less of a built-in trade-off and more of a flat-out surprise.
