You're proofreading a Japanese translation of a company report before it goes out to the head office. One line reads "work is on the rise," and your first instinct is 仕事が増えている — plain, ている, the kind of Japanese you'd say out loud to a coworker over lunch. But everything else on the page is stiff and formal, full of long compound nouns and careful phrasing, and ている suddenly sounds a little too relaxed sitting next to it. You need something that reports the same fact — a change still happening — but dressed for the occasion.

```jp
会社の仕事が増えつつある。
```
*Work at the company is on the increase.*

That sentence would sit comfortably in a business report or a news script without anyone blinking. つつある means "in the process of" — but that translation undersells it a little. It insists that whatever it's attached to is a change caught mid-motion: already visible, still climbing, with no ending in sight yet. Every time you reach for it, you're telling the reader "watch — this is happening as we speak."

You'll run into つつある constantly once you start reading real Japanese — news articles about the economy, weather reports tracking a storm, magazine pieces about a town slowly changing. It's one of those patterns that's rare in speech but everywhere in print, which is exactly why it's worth learning properly now rather than picking it up by accident later and guessing at how it works.

## How it works

つつある attaches to the same piece of the verb you already used for にくい, やすい, and すぎる — the stem, the part left over once you strip ます off. (If that term is new to you: the stem is the base a verb reduces to before an ending gets glued on, like 増え from 増えます.) That shared attachment point is worth noticing, because it's the one thing all four of those patterns have in common even though their meanings have nothing to do with each other.

```jp
増える → 増え
```
*増える, "to increase," reduces to its stem 増え.*

Glue つつある onto that stem and the sentence stops being a snapshot of how things stand today and starts being a report on a trend that's still climbing, with the writer clearly aware it hasn't leveled off yet.

```jp
経済が悪くなりつつある。
```
*The economy is gradually getting worse.*

Look closely at that one: なる ("to become") is also dropping to its stem — なり — before つつある attaches. Whatever sits directly in front of つつある has to be that stem shape. Never the plain dictionary form, and never the て-form; those belong to other patterns you already know, not this one. Mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes learners make with つつある, so it's worth double-checking the shape of the verb every time you use it.

This is also why つつある reads as heavier than a simple "-ing" translation suggests. English "is increasing" can describe something that started five minutes ago or has been true for a decade — the tense alone doesn't say. つつある always points at a trajectory: a before-state, a visible shift away from it, and an after-state that hasn't arrived yet. The sentence is only doing its job if all three of those are true at once.

```jp
世界は変わりつつある。
```
*The world is changing.*

You'll notice something about all three of those sentences: none of them are things a person would casually announce over dinner. つつある carries a built-in seriousness. Native speakers reach for it in essays, newspaper columns, formal speeches, weather bulletins — anywhere the writer wants to sound like they're documenting a shift, not just chatting about one. Seeing it in a passage is a strong signal that you've stepped into formal, written Japanese.

## Rules and exceptions

That seriousness cuts both ways. つつある is genuinely stiff, and dropping it into casual conversation with a friend reads a little like narrating your own life on the evening news — technically understandable, but strange. For everyday speech, 〜ている still does the job fine; save つつある for writing and formal speaking, the way you'd save "in progress" for a memo instead of a text message.

The ある at the end of つつある is a real verb, not just a fixed ending stapled on, and it conjugates the way ある always does. Drop the whole pattern into polite speech and ある becomes あります, exactly as it would on its own:

```jp
仕事は増えつつあります。
```
*Work is on the increase.*

> つつある only describes a change that's still in motion. Once something has finished changing, the moment for つつある has passed — the plain past tense takes over instead, and reaching for つつある there would read as a contradiction, like calling something "still rising" after it's already landed.

It's also easy to assume つつある only belongs on something huge, like an economy or "the world." It doesn't. A small, private, slow-motion change qualifies just as well — the pattern cares about the shape of the change, the fact that it's gradual and unfinished, not its size or importance.

```jp
名前を忘れつつある。
```
*I'm gradually forgetting the name.*

That's the real shape of つつある: something worth reporting mid-change, still climbing or still slipping, formal enough to put on a page, whether the subject is a whole country or one name slipping out of memory.

One more thing worth flagging: つつある doesn't take a negative form the way most verb patterns do. There's no clean way to say "is not in the process of changing" by negating つつある itself — if you need that idea, you restructure the sentence around a plain negative verb instead. It's a small gap, but it trips people up the first time they go looking for it, because almost everything else they've learned so far negates cleanly.

## Contrast

You already know すぎる, and it grabs the exact same stem つつある does — which makes the two easy to mix up if you're only half paying attention to the ending. But すぎる closes the book on an action that already went too far; it's a verdict delivered after the fact.

```jp
仕事が増えすぎた。
```
*Work increased too much.*

Swap つつある in for すぎる and the whole meaning flips. Instead of a finished excess with a judgment already attached ("too much"), you get a trend that's still unfolding, with no verdict yet on how far it will ultimately go. すぎる looks back at what already happened; つつある is still watching it happen.

かもしれない sits on the other side of つつある for a completely different reason. It's a guess about something that hasn't happened yet, offered with real uncertainty attached, not a report on something you can already see with your own eyes.

```jp
仕事が増えるかもしれない。
```
*Work might increase.*

つつある never guesses. Everything it describes is already visible — you're not speculating about tomorrow the way かもしれない does, you're pointing at something happening right now and saying so, formally and without hedging. Line the three patterns up side by side and the difference becomes obvious: すぎる looks backward at a finished excess, かもしれない looks forward at an unconfirmed possibility, and つつある stands in the present, watching a change happen and reporting it exactly as it is.

Back to that report you were proofreading: once you swap ている for つつある, the whole line reads like it belongs on the page it's sitting on. That's the entire job of this pattern — not saying anything new, just saying the same true thing in the register the rest of the document already expects.

つつある reports a change caught mid-stride: formal, visible, and still unfinished. Hold onto that "not finished yet" feeling — it's close to the opposite of あげく, which you'll meet soon for describing what finally happens once a long effort runs its course and the change you were watching resolves into a result.
