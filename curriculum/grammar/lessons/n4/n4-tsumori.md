Your friend leans over during lunch and asks what you're doing this weekend. You already know the answer — you're not guessing, you're not hoping, you've already decided. What you need is the phrase for *I'm planning to*, and that's exactly what つもり gives you. It takes a plain verb and turns it into a stated intention, something you're announcing as your own decision rather than a fact about the world.

```jp
週末、映画を見るつもりです。
```

*This weekend, I'm planning to watch a movie.*

## How It Works

つもり attaches straight onto the dictionary form of a verb — the plain, unconjugated form you'd find listed in a dictionary. There's no te-form, no stem change, nothing to memorize beyond the shape you already know. You say the verb exactly the way you'd think it to yourself, and つもり rides along right after it.

```jp
明日、図書館で勉強するつもりだ。
```

*Tomorrow, I'm planning to study at the library.*

Notice the だ sitting at the end there. That's the plain copula (the word that works like *is/am/are*), and its job is to turn つもり from a bare noun-like word — closer to "intention" on its own — into a complete sentence: "it's my intention." Drop だ entirely and bare つもり still reads as a finished statement in speech; swap だ for です and the sentence gets politer without the meaning shifting even slightly.

```jp
来年、日本語を勉強するつもりです。
```

*Next year, I'm planning to study Japanese.*

So つもり, つもりだ, and つもりです are all accepted, and choosing between them is purely a question of how formal the room is — a plain つもり with friends, つもりです with your teacher or a stranger. The dictionary form doing the heavy lifting in front of it is really the whole grammar lesson here.

```jp
見る → 見るつもり
```

*see → planning to see*

Whatever the plain form looks like — 見る, 勉強する, 帰る, 引っ越す — that's exactly what goes in front of つもり. No exceptions, no irregular attachments to track.

## Rules and Exceptions

Here's the part that's easy to miss at first: つもり describes what's inside your head *right now*, not a fact about how the future will actually go. That's a real difference from a fixed schedule — つもり can describe an intention that quietly falls apart later, and saying it doesn't promise anything actually happens. It just reports your current resolve, honestly, at the moment you say it.

That subjective feel matters even more when you're talking about someone else's plans instead of your own.

> Using つもり to describe someone else's intention can sound like you're speaking for them with total certainty. Among close friends who talk about everything, that's completely normal — toward someone you barely know, it can come across as oddly presumptuous, like you're announcing their decision before they have.

```jp
彼はもう帰るつもりだ。
```

*He's already planning to go home.*

A common wrong guess, especially early on, is trying to attach つもり directly to a noun or a plain adjective, the way `静かつもり` gets typed by someone reaching for "I intend to be quiet." That doesn't work — つもり only follows the dictionary form of a verb, full stop, never a bare noun or adjective standing on its own.

## Casual Speech

In relaxed conversation, だ often gets swapped for だよ, which softens a flat, declarative statement into something warmer and more conversational — roughly the effect "you know" has tacked onto the end of an English sentence.

```jp
今日は早く寝るつもりだよ。
```

*I'm planning to go to bed early today, you know.*

That だよ isn't required and it isn't more "correct" than だ — it's just the sound of someone talking to a friend instead of reading off a form.

## Contrast: つもり vs なければならない

You already know なければならない for the things you have no real choice about — homework due tomorrow, a deadline nobody's moving for you. つもり lives in completely different territory: it's the plan *you* chose, not the obligation life handed you. Swap one pattern for the other in an otherwise identical sentence and the entire feel of it flips.

```jp
明日、宿題をしなければならない。
```

*I have to do my homework tomorrow.*

```jp
明日、宿題をするつもりだ。
```

*I'm planning to do my homework tomorrow.*

Same homework, same day, same person saying it — but the first sentence carries the weight of a burden, and the second sounds like someone quietly proud of a decision they've already made. That contrast is worth sitting with, because English "I'm going to do my homework" flattens a distinction Japanese keeps sharply separate.

```jp
私は来月引っ越すつもりです。
```

*I'm planning to move next month.*

つもり is how you tell someone what's already settled in your own head, whether or not the day itself ever cooperates. Hold onto that self-directed, decision-first feel — it's exactly the muscle ようにする leans on later, once the intention needs ongoing upkeep instead of a single statement.
