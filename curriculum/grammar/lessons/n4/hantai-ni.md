You tell a friend the forecast said sun today, then you step outside straight into rain. That gap — between what you expected and what actually happened — needs its own word in Japanese, and that word is 反対に (はんたいに), "on the contrary" or "conversely." It doesn't soften bad news, and it doesn't add a reason for anything. It just flags a flat reversal: you thought one thing, reality delivered the opposite.

You'll hear 反対に just as often in conversation as you'll see it in writing — it's not a stiff, bookish word. Someone recapping their day, correcting a guess you made, or comparing two people's reactions will reach for it naturally, the same way you'd reach for "on the contrary" in English without thinking twice.

## How it works

反対に is built from the noun 反対 (the opposite, or opposition) plus the particle に — the same に that turns a lot of nouns into adverbs, the way 元気 becomes 元気に or 自由 becomes 自由に. Once you see that seam, the meaning falls out naturally: 反対 is "the opposite thing," so 反対に is "in the opposite way."

```jp
反対 → 反対に
```

The structure is simple: 反対に sits in front of the statement that carries the reversal. Everything before it sets up an expectation — a guess, a plan, a prediction — and everything after 反対に knocks that expectation down.

```jp
今日は暑いと思った。
反対に、とても寒かった。
```
*I thought today would be hot.*
*On the contrary, it was very cold.*

You'll almost always see a comma right after 反対に in writing. It isn't required by any rule, but it marks the small pause before the reversal lands, and nearly everyone writes it that way. 反対に also shows up mid-sentence, right after a contrasting が or けど, doing the exact same job without starting a new sentence:

```jp
彼は忙しいと言ったが、反対に暇だった。
```
*He said he was busy, but on the contrary he was free.*

Here's the part that trips people up the first week: 反対に never conjugates. It isn't an adjective bending through forms, and it isn't a verb taking a tense. It sits frozen, exactly as you see it, whether the sentence around it is past, present, polite, or plain — only the sentence *following* 反対に carries tense and politeness.

That frozen shape is also what separates 反対に from the bare noun 反対, which does show up on its own and means "opposed" or "against" — someone's stance, not a reversal of an expectation.

```jp
彼女はその話に反対だ。
```
*She is against that story.*

That sentence never sets up something to flip; it just states a position. 反対に only earns its place when the sentence has a "you'd think X, but actually Y" shape underneath it — no setup, no job for 反対に to do.

You'll also spot plain 反対 sitting in front of a noun with の, as in 反対の意見 (the opposing opinion). That's still the ordinary noun doing ordinary noun-modification work — describing which opinion, not flipping a whole sentence — so don't let the shared kanji fool you into treating it like 反対に.

There's also a difference in *scope* worth noticing. 反対に reaches across the whole clause that follows it, not just the next word. In 反対に、とても寒かった above, the reversal covers "it was very cold" as a complete idea — not just 寒かった in isolation. That's part of why it can't glue onto a single adjective or verb the way a suffix would; it needs a full statement on the other side to reverse.

## Rules and exceptions

反対に needs two sides to work: the assumption, stated or implied, and the reversal it introduces. Drop the setup entirely and 反対に reads like a connector with nothing to connect — a reversal of nothing in particular.

> Don't reach for 反対に just because two facts happen to differ. It marks a genuine flip — heat turning to cold, "easy" turning into "hard" — not a second, unrelated detail tacked onto the first sentence.

This is also why 反対に almost never opens a conversation cold. It leans on something already said or already assumed — a forecast, a rumor, a first impression — and answers it. Say it with nothing behind it and a listener will wait for the part they missed, the expectation you're supposedly correcting.

A common beginner slip is trying to fuse 反対に directly onto the word before it, as if it were a suffix, something like `暑い反対に`. That's not how it behaves: 反対に always stands alone at the head of its own clause, never glued onto the adjective or verb next to it.

```jp
テストは簡単だと思ったが、反対にとても難しかった。
```
*I thought the test would be easy, but on the contrary it was very difficult.*

Notice that the two halves genuinely oppose each other — 簡単 and 難しい are opposite qualities, not just two facts that happen to sit in the same sentence. That's the test worth running before you commit to 反対に: can you state the flipped expectation as a clean opposite? If not, a plain でも or しかし usually fits the sentence better.

It also helps to know what 反対に is *not* doing. It never explains a cause the way そのため does — そのため links a reason to its result ("it rained, so the game was cancelled"), while 反対に never tells you why anything happened. It only reports that the outcome ended up facing the other direction from what you expected. Reach for 反対に when you want to correct an assumption, not when you want to justify an outcome.

## Contrast: 反対に vs それでも

You already know それでも for "even so" — it keeps an action going despite an obstacle, without changing what actually happened. 反対に is a different animal entirely: it says the actual result flipped to the other side, not that something pushed through in spite of it.

```jp
雨が降っていた。
それでも、彼は散歩した。
```
*It was raining.*
*Even so, he went for a walk.*

Here the rain never stopped, and the walk happened anyway — それでも just reports that the plan survived the obstacle. Now compare a scenario where the obstacle itself turns out to be the wrong guess:

```jp
雨が降ると思った。
反対に、いい天気になった。
```
*I thought it would rain.*
*On the contrary, it turned into good weather.*

No walk, no obstacle overcome — the rain simply never happened, and something else took its place. That's the test to run in your head: if the expected thing still occurred and something pushed through it anyway, reach for それでも. If the expected thing just didn't happen, and its opposite did instead, reach for 反対に.

Swap the two connectors in either sentence above and you'd be describing a different day entirely. それでも in the second sentence would claim the rain came and someone still got good weather out of it, which makes no sense — the two facts can't coexist. That's the giveaway that these aren't stylistic alternatives; they're built for opposite jobs.

Predictions are wrong all the time — the weather, someone's mood, how a test goes. Now you have the word for saying so plainly, no hedging and no soft landing, just 反対に and the truth.

One habit worth building now: before you write 反対に, say the expectation out loud first, even silently. If you can't finish the sentence "I thought ___" before it, there's nothing for 反対に to reverse, and the sentence probably wants でも, しかし, or それでも instead. That one-second check is the whole rule, and it holds up in every example in this lesson.
