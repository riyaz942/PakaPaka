You've been texting a friend about a weekend trip, and they still haven't given you a straight yes or no. You aren't asking them the question again — you already asked it once — you just need one sentence you can say to someone else about the situation: "I don't know if they're coming." Asking outright a second time would be pushy, and just reporting "they didn't answer" loses the actual content of what's bothering you: whether they'll show up. That's the exact shape かどうか is built for: packing a whole yes-or-no question inside a bigger sentence, so it can sit there as information instead of as a question you're firing at someone.

```jp
友達が来るかどうか分からない。
```

*I don't know whether my friend is coming or not.*

Break it down. 来る sits in plain form — the dictionary form, the one a verb is listed under — and かどうか glues straight onto the end of it. か is the same question particle you already use to turn a statement into a question; どうか adds a sense of "either way, one way or the other." Together they build a self-contained embedded question — not something you're asking out loud, but a whole yes-or-no question sitting inside the sentence, doing the job a noun would do. 分からない is just sitting downstream of it, reacting to that packaged question the way it would react to any ordinary noun: you don't know it.

## How it works

Any plain-form verb takes かどうか directly, present or past, with nothing extra glued in between. You'll usually find it sitting right in front of a small family of verbs about knowledge and checking — 分かる (to understand or know), 知る (to know), 教える (to tell someone), 聞く (to ask or listen). Think of かどうか as the question itself, packaged up, and those verbs as what you're doing with it: you know it, you don't know it, you're asking someone else to answer it, or you're telling somebody the result.

This is different from just tacking か onto the end of a sentence to ask a question outright. か on its own, standing alone at the end, is a real spoken question aimed at whoever's listening — it wants an answer right now. かどうか never asks anybody anything directly; it just names the question as a topic, the same way a noun names a thing, so the rest of the sentence can talk about it. That's the whole trick behind calling this "embedded" — the question gets folded into a bigger sentence instead of standing on its own.

```jp
明日雨が降るかどうか分からない。
```

*I don't know whether it will rain tomorrow or not.*

い-adjectives behave the same way — plain form straight onto かどうか, nothing to remember beyond that. い-adjectives never had a だ to begin with, so there's nothing to trip over here; the habit of just tacking かどうか onto whatever plain form you already have works cleanly.

```jp
彼が忙しいかどうか教えてください。
```

*Please tell me whether he is busy or not.*

Nouns and な-adjectives are where people trip. Their plain form technically ends in だ — 学生だ, 元気だ — but that だ disappears the moment かどうか shows up. You say 元気かどうか, never `元気だかどうか`. かどうか already carries its own weight; it doesn't need だ standing in front of it to sound complete. It helps to remember that だ's whole job is to close a sentence off, to say "and that's a fact." かどうか is doing the opposite — holding the sentence open, refusing to close it off one way or the other — so a word whose entire purpose is closure just doesn't belong there anymore.

```jp
彼が元気かどうか知りたい。
```

*I want to know whether he is doing well or not.*

```jp
彼が学生かどうか分からない。
```

*I don't know whether he is a student or not.*

かどうか is just as comfortable sitting on a past-tense plain form, for when the thing you're uncertain about has already happened, or already been decided, and you simply haven't heard the result yet. The uncertainty isn't about whether the event happened — it did, one way or the other — it's about which way it went, and that you haven't been told.

```jp
試験に合格したかどうか、まだ分からない。
```

*I still don't know whether I passed the exam or not.*

Timing matters here in a way that's easy to gloss over. 合格するかどうか would put the exam result itself still up in the air — you haven't taken it yet, or the results simply aren't decided. 合格したかどうか, past tense, means the result already exists somewhere; you're only missing the news of it. Same pattern, same かどうか, but the tense on the verb in front of it tells the listener exactly where the uncertainty actually lives.

## Rules and exceptions

かどうか only handles yes-or-no uncertainty — situations where the honest answer is "yes" or "no," even if you don't currently know which. The moment a question word like 誰 or 何 enters the sentence, かどうか breaks, because that word is already doing the work of asking a question on its own; どうか has nothing left to add. Drop the どうか and keep plain か.

> `誰が来るかどうか` doesn't work — 誰 already turns the sentence into a question by itself.

```jp
誰が来るか分からない。
```

*I don't know who is coming.*

The same logic covers いつ, どこ, どの, and every other question word on your list — none of them ever combine with どうか. It's worth sitting with why: どうか is really asking "one way, or the other way?" That only makes sense when there are exactly two ways to land, yes or no. 誰 opens up a whole crowd of possible answers, not two, so どうか simply has nothing to attach its "either way" to.

かどうか is also neutral about politeness on its own — it doesn't make a sentence more or less formal by itself. Whatever comes after it carries the register: 分からない is plain and casual, 分かりません would be the polite version of the exact same sentence, and かどうか sits unchanged in front of either one.

## Contrast: かどうか vs って

You've met って as a casual way to quote or bring up something someone said. It's tempting to blur it with かどうか, since both sit right after a plain-form verb, but they're doing opposite jobs. って hands over someone else's words as settled fact — as far as the report goes, that's that. かどうか is the opposite: it flags that nothing is settled yet, that you genuinely don't know which way things will go.

```jp
友達は来るって言っていた。
```

*My friend said they were coming.*

```jp
でも、友達が本当に来るかどうか分からない。
```

*But I still don't know whether my friend is really coming or not.*

Notice what changed between those two lines: nothing said out loud contradicts what your friend told you. かどうか isn't calling them a liar — it's just honest about the gap between what was said and what you actually know. This is the kind of mess that's genuinely part of speaking Japanese honestly: people report things with って constantly, and かどうか is exactly the tool for admitting, right afterward, that a report isn't the same thing as a fact you've confirmed yourself. Native speakers lean on both in the same breath all the time, precisely because plans fall through and words don't always match what happens.

Whenever a sentence needs to hold an unanswered yes-or-no question inside it — the thing you don't know, the thing you're asking about, the thing you're checking on — plain form plus かどうか is the tool. Hold onto that lone か sitting at the front of it; you'll run into it again, doing a much smaller job, inside か何か.
