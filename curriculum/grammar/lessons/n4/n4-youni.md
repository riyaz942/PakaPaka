You've taped a note to the fridge door and you're halfway through writing it when you remember who's going to read it: a six-year-old. So you start over and make the letters twice as big.

Nothing about *what* you wrote changed. What changed is the reason behind how you wrote it — and that reason isn't a word, it's a whole little sentence: *so that the kid can read it*. Japanese hangs that sentence in front of the main one and glues it on with ように.

```jp
子供にも読めるように、大きく書きました。
```

*I wrote it big so that even a child could read it.*

読める is the potential form you already know — *can read*. Everything before ように is the situation you're aiming at. Everything after it is what you actually did to get there. The comma is doing real work: it's the seam between goal and action.

## How it works

ように attaches to a plain, non-past verb. That's two options: dictionary form (読める, 聞こえる, 分かる) when you want something to happen, or ない form (遅れない, 忘れない, 入らない) when you want something *not* to happen. Then a comma, then the thing you did or will do.

The negative version is at least as common as the positive one, and it's usually the first one learners actually need — most of daily life is arranging things so a small disaster doesn't occur.

```jp
電車に遅れないように、早く家を出ました。
```

*I left home early so that I wouldn't be late for the train.*

Look at 遅れない. It's plain, even though the sentence ends politely with 出ました. That's not sloppiness — it's the rule. More on that in a moment.

Now the part that decides whether your sentences sound native or slightly off: **the verb in front of ように is normally something nobody directly controls.** Potential forms live here happily (読める, 聞こえる, 見える, できる, 分かる), and so do intransitive verbs — verbs that describe something simply happening rather than someone doing it to something (入る, 遅れる, 止まる, 無くなる).

```jp
みんなに聞こえるように、大きい声で話しました。
```

*I spoke in a loud voice so that everyone could hear.*

聞こえる is *to be audible* — you can't order someone's ears to work. You can only adjust your voice and hope. That gap between "what I can do" and "what I want to come about" is exactly the space ように fills.

That also means the two halves of the sentence often have **different subjects**, which surprises people the first time.

```jp
猫が入らないように、窓を閉めます。
```

*I'll close the window so that the cat doesn't get in.*

The cat is the one entering; you're the one closing. Two people (well, one person and one cat) in one sentence, and Japanese doesn't mind at all. This is typical, not an edge case.

```jp
犬が水を飲めるように、皿を庭に置きました。
```

*I put a dish in the garden so the dog could drink water.*

Same shape: dog drinks, you place. Potential form before ように, deliberate action after it.

And when the subject *is* the same on both sides, the verb before ように is still something outside your direct command:

```jp
忘れないように、ノートに書きます。
```

*I write it in my notebook so I won't forget.*

You can't decide to remember. You can only decide to write. That's the whole logic of the pattern in one sentence.

## Rules and exceptions

**Never put a past-tense verb in front of ように when you mean purpose.** Not because it's ungrammatical, but because it quietly turns into something else. 忘れたように means *as if he'd forgotten* — a comparison, not a goal. So `忘れたように、ノートに書きます` doesn't say what you think it says.

**Politeness lives at the end of the sentence, never before ように.** `読めますように、大きく書きました` is wrong mid-sentence. The clause before ように is always plain; the final verb carries the politeness for the whole thing.

> There is one place where ますように is not only fine but standard: at the very end of a sentence, alone, as a wish. It's a fossil — a leftover shape that survived because people kept saying it. See the casual section below.

The most common wrong guess is putting a **fully controllable action** before ように. Something like `日本語を勉強するように、毎日ノートに書きます` sounds strange, because studying is a thing you simply decide to do — there's no gap for ように to bridge. The fix is usually to reach for the potential form and describe the *state* you want instead: 日本語が分かるように. Rule of thumb: if you could stick "I'll just decide to" in front of the verb and it still makes sense, ように is the wrong tool. Japanese has a separate pattern for deliberate-action goals, and you'll meet it later.

> Watch out for ように after a noun plus の. 猫のように歩く means *to walk like a cat* — that's a comparison, a completely different animal that happens to share a spelling. Purpose ように always sits on a verb.

## Casual speech

Here's the good news: ように is one of the rare patterns that doesn't contract, shorten, or shift at all. 忘れないように is 忘れないように in a boardroom and in a group chat. What changes is only the *end* of the sentence — 忘れないように、ノートに書きます becomes 忘れないように、ノートに書く, and that's the whole difference.

The one form worth memorizing on its own is the standalone wish, which keeps ます even among friends:

```jp
明日雨が降りませんように。
```

*Please, let it not rain tomorrow.*

No main clause, no comma, nothing after it. You'll hear this at shrines, before exams, and muttered at the sky by anyone who left the laundry out.

## Contrast

You already know ば, and it can look deceptively close, because both patterns pair up two events.

```jp
早く出れば、電車に遅れません。
```

*If you leave early, you won't be late for the train.*

Compare that with 電車に遅れないように、早く家を出ました from earlier. ば puts the condition first and the automatic consequence second: leave early → not late, as a general truth. ように flips the emotional order. The goal comes first because the goal came first *in your head*, and the second clause is the choice you made because of it. ば describes how the world works; ように describes why you did something.

The causative せる・させる is worth a second look too, because both patterns are about making things happen.

```jp
子供に薬を飲ませました。
```

*I made the child take the medicine.*

```jp
子供が一人で飲めるように、薬を水に入れました。
```

*I put the medicine in water so the child could drink it alone.*

The causative applies force to a person: I acted on you, you did the thing. ように applies force to *the situation*: I rearranged the world until the thing became possible, and then it happened on its own. One is a push, the other is clearing the path.

So: goal in front, action behind, plain non-past verb at the seam, and something you can't directly control sitting before ように. That last condition is the one to keep hold of — because when the thing that was impossible finally *does* become possible, Japanese has a pattern built out of this exact よう, and you'll want to see it coming.
