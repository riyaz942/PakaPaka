You are three minutes into your slot at the weekly meeting. People have finally put their laptops down, you have reached the part that actually matters, and your phone goes off in your pocket — loudly, so everyone hears it. That exact feeling, an action already rolling along and something barging straight into it, is what 最中に is built to describe.

```jp
会議の最中に、携帯が鳴った。
```

*My phone rang right in the middle of the meeting.*

最中 (さいちゅう) on its own is a noun. It is written with the characters for *most* and *middle*, and that is a good picture of it: not the start, not the end, but the busiest, most-in-it middle of something that is underway. Add に and you get a time expression that frames one action as getting interrupted — you were deep in the middle of X, and then Y happened.

## How it works

Only two things attach to 最中に, and both of them describe something that is genuinely in progress. Very often the same situation can be built either way; the noun version is shorter, and the verb version pins down exactly which part of the activity you were in.

**A noun plus の.** Pick a noun that names an activity with a clear beginning and end — a meeting, a meal, a class, an exam, a phone call — and join it on with の.

```jp
食事の最中に、社長から電話が来た。
```

*A call from the company president came in right in the middle of dinner.*

The noun carries the whole span by itself. 食事 already means the act of having a meal, so 食事の最中に is at the busiest point of that meal. No verb needed.

**A verb in its ている form.** ている is the shape Japanese uses for an action in progress — the piece that works like the *-ing* in an English word like eating. 最中に insists on that shape, and the reason is simple: 最中に points at a midpoint, and only an ongoing action *has* a midpoint you can stand inside. A dictionary form is the whole action as an idea; a past form is the action already finished. Neither one has a middle to be in. So take the plain verb, move it into ている, then add 最中に.

```jp
食べる → 食べている最中に
```

*eat → in the middle of eating*

```jp
昼ご飯を食べている最中に、部長が来た。
```

*The department head showed up right in the middle of my lunch.*

```jp
映画を見ている最中に、地震が起きた。
```

*An earthquake hit right in the middle of the movie.*

Look at how each of these sentences ends: 来た, 起きた, 鳴った. Every time, something lands on the scene from outside. That is not a coincidence — it is the heart of the pattern, so it gets its own section.

## The interruption rule

The grammar note for this point is one word: *interruption*. 最中に does more than place two things at the same time. It says the first action was cruising along and the second one cut across it — nearly always as a surprise, and usually as a nuisance.

```jp
友達と電話している最中に、赤ちゃんが泣いた。
```

*The baby cried right in the middle of my phone call with a friend.*

You were on the call. The crying was not on your schedule. That gap — between what you were calmly doing and what suddenly grabbed your attention — is what a listener hears the moment you say 最中に. Picture the same shape anywhere: mid-exam and a phone starts ringing two desks over; halfway through cooking dinner and someone is at the door. The pattern is waiting for the thing that breaks in.

> If the second half of your sentence is calm and expected — you drank your tea, you carried on reading your book — 最中に is the wrong tool. The pattern promises that a disruption is coming. If nothing disrupts anything, choose another way to say while.

## Rules and exceptions

**The front part has to be genuinely ongoing.** This is where people slip first. You cannot hang 最中に off a plain dictionary form or a plain past form — `食べる最中に` and `食べた最中に` are both wrong. For a verb it is always the ている form: 食べている最中に. For a noun it is always noun + の: 食事の最中に.

**It marks one stretch of activity, not a long era.** 最中に fits an afternoon of work, a single conversation, one exam. It does not fit the whole of summer or your years at university. Stretch the span that wide and a native speaker simply will not use 最中に here — it sounds like you are trying to fold a whole season into one afternoon.

**Both halves share one timeframe.** The interrupting event has to fall *inside* the span of the first action — not in the seconds before it begins, not just after it wraps up. If your friend called the moment the meeting ended, that is not 会議の最中に.

**The pattern never bends for politeness.** 最中に sits in the middle of the sentence and stays frozen. All the politeness work happens on the final verb: 鳴った in casual writing, 鳴りました in polite speech, with 最中に unchanged in between.

## Contrast with what you already know

**ついでに — while I'm at it.** You met this one recently, and it is the pattern most likely to be confused with 最中に, because both land in English as while.

```jp
買い物のついでに、銀行にも行った。
```

*While I was out shopping, I also went to the bank.*

ついでに means: I am already doing A, so I will attach B and knock both out in one trip. B is a bonus, and *you* chose it. 最中に has the opposite temperament — you did not choose the second event, it landed on you and broke your flow. Swap them in the sentence above (買い物の最中に、銀行に行った) and it turns odd, as though going to the bank were an ambush that wrecked your shopping. ついでに is tidy multitasking; 最中に is your activity getting hijacked.

**上で — after / on the basis of.** Also recent, also about two events, but 上で lines them up in a deliberate order.

```jp
よく考えた上で、答えます。
```

*I'll give you an answer after I've thought it over carefully.*

上で takes a た-form and says: A is finished, and standing on that result I now do B. It is planned and sequential. 最中に is neither — the two events overlap, and the second one shows up with no warning. One pattern is A is done, therefore B; the other is B crashed into A while A was still going.

A quick field test: if you can retell the sentence in English as *just as I was doing X — and then, bang — Y*, 最中に fits. If the natural English is and also, or and then as planned, it does not.

## Recap

最中に = a ている verb or a noun + の, followed by something that jolts that action from outside. Build the first half as an action still in motion, and make the second half land hard. If nothing gets interrupted, you want a different pattern.
