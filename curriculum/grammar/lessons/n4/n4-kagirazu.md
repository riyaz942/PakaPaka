You tell 彼 that your 会社 has a lot of 残業, half expecting some solidarity about how uniquely bad this is. He just shrugs — 銀行 apparently has exactly the same problem, and probably every 会社員 he knows too. That move he just made — naming the one case everyone would assume is the whole story, then saying the real picture is wider than that — is exactly what に限らず (*not limited to*) does, packed into a single word.

```jp
残業は会社に限らず、銀行でも多いです。
```

*Overtime isn't limited to companies — it's common at banks too.*

に限らず sits right after a noun with nothing extra needed — no の, no な, nothing to conjugate first. It flags that noun as the obvious-but-incomplete case, and whatever shows up later in the sentence, usually marked by も or でも, is the part that widens the picture. You'll run into this shape constantly in writing that's trying to describe a trend rather than one person's afternoon — company notices, articles, that kind of thing.

Notice, too, that に限らず still contains に — the same に you already use to mark a target or a point of comparison in dozens of other sentences. That's not an accident; the whole phrase is built on "limited to [noun]," and に marks exactly the noun the limiting would apply to, if the sentence weren't busy denying it. Once you see that, the word stops feeling like a fixed chunk to memorize and starts feeling like something you could have half-guessed.

## How it works

The whole structure boils down to **Noun + に限らず**. You pick the narrow case first — the one thing people would guess on their own without being told anything else — attach に限らず directly onto it, and then say what else belongs in the same picture. There's no extra step in between; the noun and に限らず sit right next to each other.

```jp
動物が好きなのは女の子に限らず、男の子も好きです。
```

*Liking animals isn't limited to girls — boys like them too.*

Look at the second half of that sentence: 男の子も好きです. The も there is doing the actual work of widening things — it's saying "boys, too." に限らず names the narrow case (女の子); the second half names the case being added on top of it. Take the も away and the sentence stops making its point, because nothing is left to pick up what に限らず set up in the first place. This is the detail that trips people up the first week — に限らず on its own only sets the stage; something later has to walk through the door it opened.

```jp
甘い物が好きなのは学生に限らず、先生も好きです。
```

*Liking sweets isn't limited to students — teachers like them too.*

Same skeleton every single time: narrow case + に限らず, then a broader case marked with も, or でも when what's being added is a place rather than a person or a thing — that's what 銀行でも was doing back in the first example. Whichever particle shows up, its job is always identical: confirm that the picture doesn't stop at the noun you just named.

It's worth pausing on why でも shows up for 銀行 but plain も shows up for 男の子 and 学生 and 先生. でも is で (the particle marking a place, a means, or a context) plus も — so it's reserved for spots where you'd normally mark that noun with で anyway, like a location where something happens. 男の子 and 学生 aren't places, so they just take a bare も. Once you notice that, you're not really learning a new rule here at all — you're just applying particles you already know on top of a case that's already been set up by に限らず.

## Rules and exceptions

に限らず only ever attaches to a noun. It doesn't bolt onto an adjective or a verb directly, and it isn't built for widening a single action or a one-off event — it's built for widening a category of people, places, or things. If what you actually want to say is closer to "not only did this happen, that happened too," you need a different tool; this one is strictly for "not only this group, that group as well."

The noun in front of に限らず also tends to name a group or a category rather than one specific individual. 女の子, 学生, 会社員, 大人, 日本 — every noun you've seen so far names a whole class of people or places, never "that one particular 先生 standing by the door." That's not a hard grammatical wall so much as a habit that falls out of what the pattern is for: you reach for に限らず when you're making a claim broad enough to need widening in the first place.

> に限らず reads like something pulled from a report or a news article — it's a formal, mostly written pattern. In everyday speech, people reach for だけでなく far more often; に限らず tends to show up describing a trend across a whole group, not a friend complaining to you over coffee.

```jp
忙しいのは会社員に限らず、学生も忙しいです。
```

*Being busy isn't limited to office workers — students are busy too.*

```jp
病気になるのは大人に限らず、子供もなります。
```

*Getting sick isn't limited to adults — children get sick too.*

Both of those read a little like lines from a health pamphlet, and that's the point — に限らず is most comfortable making a general claim about two groups, phrased almost like a small piece of trivia, rather than narrating one specific afternoon in one specific 家. That's also why you won't see it much in casual chat: nobody opens a text message to a friend with a sentence that sounds like a public health notice.

## Contrast

You already know だけでなく for "not only X, but also Y," and に限らず is reaching for the exact same target. Swap one for the other and the meaning barely moves:

```jp
動物が好きなのは女の子だけでなく、男の子も好きです。
```

*It's not just girls who like animals — boys like them too.*

That sentence says almost the same thing as the に限らず version a few paragraphs back, but the register isn't the same at all. だけでなく sounds like something a friend would actually say to you across a table; に限らず sounds like a line from a report describing a pattern across an entire population. Neither one is more "correct" than the other — they're just dressed for different rooms, and picking the wrong one for the room is the kind of small mismatch that makes otherwise fluent Japanese sound slightly off.

There's an honest bit of mess here worth naming: plenty of native speakers use に限らず in speech too, especially anyone used to giving presentations or writing reports for work, and plenty of written Japanese leans on だけでなく when the writer wants something a little softer. The formal-versus-casual line is real, but it's a leaning, not a locked gate — treat it as your default guess, not a rule you'll never see broken.

```jp
環境問題は日本に限らず、世界の問題です。
```

*Environmental problems aren't limited to Japan — they're a global issue.*

That's the register に限らず is most at home in: describing something bigger than the one example everybody reaches for first. 環境問題 stops being a story about one country the moment に限らず shows up in front of 日本 — the sentence is already pointing you toward 世界 before its second half even arrives. だけでなく could sit in that same sentence and still be grammatical, but it would read like a casual aside instead of the opening line of a serious piece of writing.

You've now seen に限らず do the same job seven different ways — companies, animals, sweets, busy schedules, getting sick, and finally a whole country's worth of 環境問題 — and every single time the shape was identical: name the narrow case, attach に限らず, then let も or でも carry in whatever else belongs. That repetition isn't an accident of this lesson; it's the actual rhythm of how the pattern gets used in real writing, sentence after sentence, report after report.

に限らず tells the listener that the case you just named isn't the whole story — something wider is going on beyond it. Hold onto that same formal, report-like feel: に関して leans on the exact same register once you get there.
