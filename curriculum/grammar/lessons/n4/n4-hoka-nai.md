It's past midnight and you're standing outside the station. The gate is shut, the trains have stopped for the night, and the one taxi that passed didn't slow down. Your phone is dead, so calling anyone is out. Home is a long way off on foot, but there is genuinely nothing else left to try. The Japanese sentence that fits this exact feeling is short:

```jp
歩いて帰るほかない。
```
*I have no choice but to walk home.*

ほかない takes a situation where every other option has been stripped away and names the one that remains. It isn't a preference and it isn't advice. It's the last door still open, and you're walking through it only because the rest are locked.

## How it works

The build is about as plain as N4 grammar gets. Take a verb in its plain **dictionary form** (the shape you'd look up in a dictionary — 行く, 待つ, 帰る) and attach ほかない straight onto the end. Nothing changes shape, nothing conjugates.

```jp
行く → 行くほかない
```
*go → have no choice but to go*

The ほか in there is the ordinary noun meaning "other", the one you already know from 他の人, "other people". The ない on the end is just the negative of ある, "to exist". Put literally, the phrase says "(doing this) — there is no other". No other road, no other move. Once that literal picture is in your head, the meaning stops needing to be memorised.

Here is the station scene filled out a little more:

```jp
電車が止まったから、駅で待つほかない。
```
*Since the trains have stopped, I have no choice but to wait at the station.*

And a weekend version — the kind of Sunday where the plan quietly falls apart:

```jp
今日は雨だから、家にいるほかない。
```
*It's raining today, so I have no choice but to stay home.*

For a polite context — speaking with a teacher or a customer, or writing a formal email — swap ない for ありません. The meaning is identical; only the register moves up.

```jp
彼に謝るほかありません。
```
*I have no choice but to apologize to him.*

Those two endings, ほかない and ほかありません, are the entire toolkit you need here. There is no past-tense or negated version to learn for this course.

## Rules and exceptions

The attachment point is strict: a **plain dictionary-form verb**, and nothing else. Not the past, not the negative, not the polite ます-stem, and not a noun or a na-adjective standing on its own.

- `行ったほかない` — wrong; the past tense doesn't belong here
- `行きますほかない` — wrong; no polite stem in front of it
- `学生ほかない` — wrong; ほかない doesn't clip onto a bare noun like this

If what you want is "there's no choice but *this thing*" with a noun, you need a different construction. ほかない is built for actions, not objects.

> ほかない is not emotionally neutral. It carries a clear undertone of *this is not what I wanted*. If the outcome actually suits you, or you're choosing it freely, ほかない will sound strangely bleak. Keep it for the forced hand.

Because the reason for the trap usually matters, ほかない very often follows a から or ので clause, or a なら. The clause before explains why the other options are gone; the pattern delivers what that leaves you with.

```jp
誰も来ないなら、一人でするほかない。
```
*If no one comes, I'll have no choice but to do it alone.*

## Register and small variations

This is a formal pattern with a written feel. You'll meet it in newspaper opinion pieces, in reports, in a manager passing on a decision that's already been made. Between friends, Japanese speakers usually pick something lighter, so don't sprinkle ほかない through casual conversation.

You may also see は slipped in for weight: ほかはない. It's the same pattern with a little more stress on the "no other" part — nothing new, just a form to recognise.

When the situation is about redoing something from scratch, the pattern still simply clips onto the dictionary form:

```jp
もう一度やり直すほかない。
```
*There's nothing for it but to start over once more.*

## Up against のみ

The closest thing you've already learned is のみ, the formal "only". Both narrow the field to a single item, so they can feel swappable — until you actually swap them and watch the meaning tilt.

のみ is a flat statement of fact: *this and nothing else*, with no feeling attached. It sits on a noun.

```jp
待つのみだ。
```
*There is only waiting.*

ほかない sits on a verb and adds the resignation: *every other option is gone, so this one is forced on me*.

```jp
待つほかない。
```
*There's no choice but to wait.*

"There is only waiting" can be calm, even disciplined — an athlete steadying themselves before a result. "There's no choice but to wait" is someone who wanted to act and can't. Same corner, different feeling. Want the bare fact? Use のみ. Want the trapped feeling? Use ほかない.

## The one-line version

ほかない = plain dictionary verb + "and there is no other move" — the single option left standing once the rest have been ruled out. When we reach 上で later, you'll see the other side of this: settling on a course *after* weighing everything, instead of being handed only one.
