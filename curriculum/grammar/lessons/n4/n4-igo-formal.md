Picture this: you moved to Tokyo three years ago, and someone at a party asks how long you've been in Japan. You could just give a number, but what you actually want to say is "ever since I got here, I've been living in this city" — one clause that ties the moment you arrived to everything that has been true since. That job belongs to 以来 (いらい), literally "since."

## How it works

以来 attaches directly to a noun that names a point in time, or to the te-form of a verb (the -て form — the connecting shape you already use for "and" and for requests). Either way, it marks the moment something started, and everything after it in the sentence describes what has stayed true from that moment up to right now.

With a verb, you first build the te-form, then add 以来 right after it.

```jp
来る → 来て
```

*to come → having come*

```jp
日本に来て以来、毎日忙しいです。
```

*Ever since I came to Japan, I've been busy every day.*

Look at the shape: [action in te-form] + 以来 + [ongoing state]. The second half is never a one-time event — it's something that has kept being true since the te-form clause happened. That's the whole engine of 以来: a starting line, then an unbroken stretch of time after it, right up to the present.

The same mechanic works for する-verbs, where the te-form is 〜して.

```jp
卒業する → 卒業して
```

*to graduate → having graduated*

```jp
卒業して以来、先生に会っていません。
```

*I haven't seen my teacher since I graduated.*

With a noun, you skip the te-form entirely and attach 以来 straight to the time word — no verb needed at all.

```jp
先月以来、病気です。
```

*I've been sick since last month.*

That's the whole mechanic, in two shapes: noun + 以来, or verb-te-form + 以来, always followed by something ongoing.

## Rules and exceptions

以来 is built for change that sticks. If nothing has been continuously true since the starting point, 以来 is the wrong tool — it doesn't just mean "at some point after X," it means "from X, unbroken, until right now."

```jp
結婚して以来、毎日料理をしています。
```

*Ever since I got married, I've been cooking every day.*

> Only the て-form works here — a plain past tense like `来た以来` is not something you'll hear native speakers use. If you can build the て-form, you can use 以来; if you're reaching for 〜た, stop and switch back to 〜て.

The other trap is scale. 以来 wants a starting point big enough to carry a stretch of time after it — a life event, a season, a named date — not a five-minute gap. Nobody reaches for 以来 over something that small; that's what plain て-form clauses without 以来 are for.

```jp
子供が生まれて以来、あまり寝ていません。
```

*Ever since our child was born, I haven't slept much.*

This one trips people the first week, because English "since" covers both jobs — the one-off "since Tuesday I've been at my desk" and the reason-giving "since you're here, help me" — and only the first of those is 以来's job. The second is a completely different word, and mixing them up is a very natural mistake to make early on.

## Contrast

You already know 以外 (except / other than), and it's an easy one to misread at a glance — both words start with 以, and both feel like they're marking some kind of boundary. But 以外 draws a line around what's excluded from a group; it has nothing to do with time at all.

```jp
日本語以外は分かりません。
```

*I don't understand anything except Japanese.*

そのため is a different kind of neighbor: it links a cause to its result — that happened, so this happened — while 以来 links a starting point to everything that has followed it. そのため cares about *why* something happened; 以来 cares about *since when* something has been true.

```jp
病気になりました。そのため、会社を休みました。
```

*I got sick. Because of that, I took the day off work.*

Swap 以来 in for そのため there and the sentence stops making sense — "sick, and 以来 I took the day off" throws away the cause-and-effect link that そのため was carrying, and replaces it with a claim about an ongoing state that was never there.

以来 opens a sentence by naming when an ongoing state began. Hold onto that starting-point feeling — it comes back when you meet 反対に, which flips a situation around instead of anchoring it in time.
