Your teacher hands back your essay with a note in the margin, and later you're telling a friend how it went. In English you'd probably say "my teacher praised me" — but Japanese has an instinct that flips the sentence around, putting you in the position of receiving the action instead of your teacher in the position of giving it.

```jp
先生に褒められました。
```

*I was praised by my teacher.*

That's the passive: something happens to the subject of the sentence, and whoever's doing it gets marked with に instead of standing at the front as the subject. Once you can build れる・られる, you can talk about getting complimented, getting your things borrowed, even getting rained on — every situation where you're on the receiving end rather than the one acting. English usually needs "was" plus a past participle to do this; Japanese does it with one verb ending.

## How it works

The passive attaches to the verb stem, and which stem you use depends on the verb's group — the same three-way split you already learned for the potential form. The idea behind all three groups is the same: take the verb, shift it into a stem that no longer commits to a subject deciding to act, and let れる or られる mark that something simply occurred, with you or someone else on the receiving end.

### Ichidan verbs

Drop the final る and add られる.

```jp
食べる → 食べられる
```

*to eat → to be eaten*

```jp
弟にパンを食べられた。
```

*My bread got eaten by my little brother.*

Notice something odd there: パン still has を on it, exactly the marker it would carry in the plain sentence 弟がパンを食べた (my brother ate the bread). The passive didn't promote パン to subject — it just layered "and this happened to me" on top of the original sentence. Hold onto that; it matters again in a minute.

Once you notice the shape, you'll see it everywhere: 見られる (to be seen), 教えられる (to be taught), 忘れられる (to be forgotten) are all the exact same stem+られる move, no extra mechanics required.

### Godan verbs

Take the verb's final う-row sound, shift it back one row to あ, and add れる.

```jp
読む → 読まれる
```

*to read → to be read*

```jp
手紙が読まれた。
```

*The letter was read.*

This one has no に anywhere — nobody's named as the reader. That's normal for a godan passive describing an event rather than a grievance: the letter got read, full stop, and who did the reading isn't the point.

Verbs ending in う are the one spot where this shift looks strange, because う doesn't have a matching あ-row sound to fall back on — it borrows わ instead.

```jp
使う → 使われる
```

*to use → to be used*

You've actually met this あ-row shift before: it's the same vowel step you use to build the negative. 読む becomes 読まない the same way it becomes 読まれる — once you can build one, the muscle memory for the other is already there. The same pattern covers 話す → 話される and 呼ぶ → 呼ばれる without anything new to learn.

### Irregular verbs

する and 来る don't follow either pattern; you just have to know them.

```jp
する → される
```

*to do → to be done*

```jp
来る → 来られる
```

*to come → to have someone come*

```jp
友達に来られた。
```

*A friend came over on me, and it was inconvenient.*

That last one is the strangest sentence in this lesson if you translate it word for word, because English won't let you passivize "come" — nobody "gets come to." Japanese doesn't care. れる・られる isn't reserved for things being done to an object; it can mark that an event, even one nobody aimed at you, landed on you and cost you something.

> The passive on an intransitive verb like 来る or 降る almost always signals mild inconvenience. 友達に来られた reads less like "a friend visited" and more like "I got visited on, and it threw off my evening."

## Rules and exceptions

に marks whoever performed the action — the teacher who praised you, the brother who ate your bread. Drop it and the sentence becomes a plain, agentless description of something happening, exactly what 手紙が読まれた is doing. You'll drop it constantly: most passive sentences you'll actually use skip the agent, because either it's obvious from context or naming it would sound oddly pointed, almost like an accusation.

The を you saw stay put in 弟にパンを食べられた is a real exception, not a typo. When the passive expresses something being done to your belongings rather than to you directly, the item keeps its を and you keep your ordinary subject slot (an implied 私は here). Force the item into が instead — `パンが食べられた` — and you get a completely different, agentless sentence about bread going missing, not about your brother's crime.

> For ichidan verbs and for 来る, the passive and the potential share the exact same conjugation. 食べられる can mean "gets eaten" or "can eat" depending on nothing the verb itself tells you — only the sentence around it decides.

Don't reach for れる・られる just because an English sentence happens to be passive. "The window was broken" usually sounds more natural in Japanese as an active or plain statement unless you specifically need to flag that someone was affected by it — this pattern exists for when being affected is the whole point.

That's also why the passive shows up so often around family and friends rather than strangers and objects: 弟にパンを食べられた only makes sense as a sentence because your bread going missing is *your* problem, not an abstract fact about the world. Compare that to 手紙が読まれた, where nobody's inconvenience is on the table — just an event, reported plainly.

## Contrast: passive vs. potential

You already know れる・られる as the potential form, and the shape really is identical — which is exactly why context has to do the work the verb itself can't. There's a reason the two share a form: both describe the subject *not* being the one driving the action — either the action simply becomes possible on its own, or it simply happens to someone. English treats those as two unrelated grammar categories; Japanese never bothered to split them.

```jp
これは食べられる？
```

*Can this be eaten?*

Nothing here is happening to anyone; nobody's marked with に, and the whole sentence is asking about a quality of これ rather than reporting an event. That's the tell: potential sentences ask about ability or possibility and rarely carry an agent acting on someone, while passive sentences almost always have — or clearly imply — someone whose action lands on someone else.

You'll also hear ら-less shortcuts like `食べれる` in casual speech. That belongs to the potential only — dropping ら is never done for the passive, so 食べられる keeps its full form no matter how casual the conversation gets.

So the real question with any れる・られる sentence isn't just "can I build this," it's "what's actually happening to whom." Keep that question in mind and the two readings stop colliding — you'll find yourself scanning for an agent and a に before you even finish reading the sentence.

This shape isn't done evolving, either. Fuse it with the causative later and you get せられる・させられる, the "made to do it against your will" pattern, which takes exactly the inconvenience you just met in 友達に来られた and turns the volume all the way up.
