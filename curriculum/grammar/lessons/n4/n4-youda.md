Picture this: you knock on a friend's door after texting them you'd stop by. No footsteps. No light spilling out from under the door. You wait a beat and knock again — still nothing. You don't actually know for a fact that they're out; nobody told you so, and you haven't called to check. You just have every reason to think so, based on what you can see and hear standing right there on the step.

That's exactly the job ようだ does. It's the grammar you reach for the moment you're drawing a conclusion from evidence sitting right in front of you — not stating a plain fact, not guessing out of nowhere, but reading a situation and saying, out loud, what it looks like from where you're standing.

```jp
誰も家にいないようだ。
```
*Looks like nobody's home.*

Notice the shape: the plain, everyday form of the verb — いない, meaning it doesn't exist here — with ようだ tacked on the end. That's the whole trick, and it holds for almost everything that follows. ようだ rides on top of the same plain form you already use talking casually with a friend, and it turns a flat statement into a read on the situation: here's what the evidence in front of me suggests.

## How it works

ようだ attaches after the plain form for verbs and i-adjectives, exactly as they already are — nothing new to memorize beyond what you already know. Na-adjectives and nouns each need one small adjustment, and those two are worth slowing down for:

- Verb, plain form (dictionary, past, or negative)
- I-adjective, plain form, unchanged
- Na-adjective, stem + な
- Noun + の

Start with a verb in its dictionary form, the same shape you'd use talking to a close friend. You look up and heavy grey clouds are building fast over the park:

```jp
雨が降るようだ。
```
*Looks like it's going to rain.*

Past tense works exactly the same way — just swap in the plain past form instead of the dictionary form. Nothing else in the sentence changes. You step outside later and the ground is dark and slick:

```jp
雨が降ったようだ。
```
*Looks like it rained.*

I-adjectives plug straight in with no changes at all — no だ to drop, nothing to swap. Everyone around the office has their coat zipped up to the chin and their hands jammed in their pockets:

```jp
今日は寒いようだ。
```
*Seems cold today.*

Na-adjectives are the one spot where the plain form needs a small correction, and it catches almost everyone the first time. A na-adjective's plain form technically ends in だ, as in 元気だ or 暇だ — but ようだ doesn't want that だ at all. It wants な instead, the exact same connector a na-adjective uses right before a noun, like 暇な時間 (free time).

```jp
先生は暇なようだ。
```
*The teacher seems free.*

> Don't write `先生は暇だようだ` — that's the single most common slip with this pattern. Drop the だ, add な instead. It trips people up because every other plain form you've learned walks straight into ようだ completely unchanged; na-adjectives are the one exception.

Nouns get the same treatment as na-adjectives, but with の instead of な — this is the noun plus の plus ようだ shape written out formally, the one you'll see most often as the textbook version of this rule. Your friend opens the door pale, quiet, and barely upright, and you don't need them to say a word:

```jp
友達は病気のようだ。
```
*My friend seems to be sick.*

Swap だ for です anywhere in this whole pattern and you get the polite register, ようです — same evidence, same conclusion, nothing about the meaning changes, it's just dressed up for someone you don't know well, like a coworker or a stranger you're making conversation with:

```jp
先生は忙しいようです。
```
*The teacher seems busy.*

## Rules and exceptions

The one non-negotiable ingredient here is evidence you picked up yourself — something you saw, heard, smelled, or otherwise noticed just now or very recently. ようだ is a conclusion drawn on the spot from something real in front of you, not a hunch pulled out of thin air and not a guess about something you have no way of checking.

That requirement also means ようだ sits oddly on facts you already know for certain about yourself. A sentence like 私は学生のようだ is strange to a native ear — you don't need to read evidence that you're a student, you already know it without looking for clues. ようだ does show up talking about yourself, but mainly when you're noticing something about yourself as if from the outside — catching your reflection in a window and realizing you look worn out, say — not for plain, settled facts you'd never need to infer. The same logic applies to anything you already know firsthand: if you watched your friend walk in the door completely fine, you wouldn't turn around and say 友達は元気なようだ about something you just witnessed directly with no room for doubt. Save ようだ for the gap between noticing something and being fully sure of it.

> ようだ describes what the evidence in front of you suggests, not what's already confirmed. The moment something becomes certain, the sentence usually drops ようだ entirely and just states it plainly.

## Casual speech

In relaxed, everyday speech, ようだ very often shrinks down to みたいだ, and you'll hear this constantly once you start listening for it. The meaning stays completely identical — same evidence, same conclusion, same level of confidence — but the attachment gets simpler across the board: no の, no な, みたいだ goes straight onto the noun or na-adjective with nothing in between.

```jp
友達は病気みたいだ。
```
*My friend seems to be sick.*

> With みたいだ, skip の and な entirely — don't carry them over out of habit. `病気のみたいだ` and `暇なみたいだ` are both wrong; it's 病気みたいだ and 暇みたいだ, full stop.

## Contrast: ようだ vs らしい

You've already met らしい, which also translates as seems, and the overlap is real enough that it trips people up for months. Here's the line between them: ようだ is your own impression, built from something you directly noticed — sight, sound, a scene playing out right in front of you. らしい leans on evidence that came from somewhere else entirely — something you heard secondhand, read, or were told by someone who saw it before you did.

Picture an empty platform with no train in sight for ten straight minutes, and you're the one standing there watching it not arrive:

```jp
電車が遅れたようだ。
```
*Looks like the train is delayed.*

Now picture hearing the exact same fact from a station announcement, or a text from a friend already waiting somewhere else, instead of watching it unfold with your own eyes:

```jp
電車が遅れたらしい。
```
*I heard the train is delayed.*

はず sits on a completely different axis from either of these. It's about what logically should be true given something you already trust as fact, not a fresh impression at all — closer to given what I already know, this has to be the case than here's what I'm noticing right now. ようだ never reaches that level of certainty; it's always sitting a half-step short of being sure, which is exactly why it's useful — it lets you say something without overcommitting to it. Mixing these three up mostly just sounds a little off rather than truly wrong, so don't panic if you blur them at first; the difference gets easier to feel the more often you notice, yourself, which kind of knowledge a sentence is actually resting on.

## Closing thought

Reading a situation and saying so, without overclaiming it, is most of what makes ようだ worth having in the first place — you'll reach for it constantly, the moment something looks, sounds, or feels a certain way and you want to say that out loud honestly instead of stating it as settled fact. みたいだ gets its own full lesson soon, with its own range of tone and use; for now, just recognize it as this exact same instinct, said a little more casually among friends.
