Picture your older brother waking up with a fever. He should stay in bed today — that's the obvious move. Instead he gets dressed and walks out the door to work like nothing happened. English has a whole family of words for that shrug — still, anyway, regardless — but Japanese has one word built for exactly this move: それでも.

```jp
兄は熱があった。それでも仕事に行った。
```

*My older brother had a fever. Even so, he went to work.*

That's the whole shape of it. State what happened. Put a period down. Drop それでも in as its own little sentence-opener. State what happened next — something the first sentence should have stopped, and didn't.

That gap between what should happen and what actually does is the whole point of the word. それでも doesn't soften the obstacle, apologize for it, or explain it away — it just names the obstacle, closes the sentence on it, and lets the next sentence walk straight through it anyway.

## How it works

それでも always sits between two separate ideas: **Sentence. + それでも + Sentence.** The first sentence lays out a fact that creates resistance — bad weather, an injury, a lack of sleep, anything that would normally get in the way of what comes next. The second sentence reports that the resistance didn't work.

```jp
雨が降っていた。それでも兄は散歩した。
```

*It was raining. Even so, my brother went for a walk.*

Notice that それでも doesn't attach to anything — it isn't glued onto a verb stem the way a conjugated ending would be, so there's nothing here to conjugate. Past tense, present tense, casual or polite, it stays それでも no matter what came before it. Its only job is standing at the front of the second sentence and pointing back at the first one.

That's also why it never changes shape for politeness. The sentences around it might be polite or plain, but それでも itself is neither — it's just a connector, not a verb or an adjective, so there's no です or ます form of it to reach for.

It also doesn't shrink in casual speech the way some polite words do — わからない becoming わかんない, or では becoming じゃ. それでも already sounds plain and complete on its own, so there's nothing left to trim, and you'll hear it exactly this way whether the conversation is formal or between old friends.

You'll also see it show up mid-sentence, right after a clause joined with が, instead of after a fresh period. It's still doing the same job — the が just means the writer chose to keep both halves in one breath instead of splitting them into two sentences.

```jp
兄は薬を飲んだが、それでも熱は下がらなかった。
```

*My brother took his medicine, but even then his fever didn't go down.*

Either way — full stop or が — the test is the same: can you point at the sentence right before それでも and name the thing it should have blocked? If yes, you're using it correctly.

Both versions show up constantly in ordinary storytelling — someone recapping their weekend, a coworker explaining why they're late, a doctor summarizing a patient's stubborn symptoms. Anywhere someone reports "this happened, and then, against expectation, this also happened," それでも is doing quiet work in the background.

## Rules and exceptions

それでも needs the first sentence to carry real weight. It has to describe an obstacle a listener would nod along to — "yes, that should have been a problem." Drop in something trivial and それでも starts to sound like you're announcing a fight that never happened.

```jp
先生は説明を三回した。それでも私は意味が分からなかった。
```

*The teacher explained it three times. Even so, I didn't understand the meaning.*

Three explanations is genuine effort — the kind of thing that should have produced understanding. それでも earns its keep here because the outcome really does fight against the setup.

Compare that to a setup with barely any weight — one quick reminder, say, instead of three full explanations. それでも would feel like overkill there, as if you were bracing the listener for a much bigger fight than the sentence actually describes. The word carries its own sense of scale, so save it for obstacles that are genuinely heavy.

> それでも always looks backward at something already stated. It can't reach forward into a hypothetical — a sentence like "if the exam turns out to be hard, I'll study anyway" isn't a job for それでも. It wants a sentence that already happened or is already true, not a guess about a future that hasn't landed yet.

The obstacle also doesn't have to be one blunt fact — a whole earlier situation can serve as the setup, as long as a reader can point back and name what it was.

```jp
説明は難しかった。それでも学生は頑張って勉強した。
```

*The explanation was difficult. Even so, the student kept at their studying.*

This is also why それでも rarely opens a conversation cold. Someone hearing it for the first time needs the earlier sentence to be sitting right there — spoken out loud a moment ago, or written just above. Start a chat with それでも and nobody knows what "even so" is supposed to be answering.

## Contrast

Here's a piece of trivia that makes それでも easier to hold onto: it's literally それ (that) plus でも (even) — "even that" worn down into "even so." Which means you already knew half of this word. でも on its own means but/however, and それでも just points that でも squarely at the sentence sitting right in front of it.

Swap それでも for a bare でも and you lose that pointing-back feeling. でも can open a sentence out of nowhere, contrasting two things that were never really in tension to begin with.

```jp
天気は悪かった。でも、公園はきれいだった。
天気は悪かった。それでも私たちは公園を歩いた。
```

*The weather was bad. But the park was pretty.*
*The weather was bad. Even so, we walked through the park.*

The first pair is just two facts sitting side by side — bad weather, pretty park, no real friction between them. The second pair says the bad weather should have kept everyone indoors, and it didn't; that resistance is the part only それでも carries.

ところが is the other neighbor worth separating out, and it's an easy pair to mix up because both sit after a full stop and both introduce something a little unwelcome. The difference is in what kind of "unwelcome": ところが reports a genuine twist, a result nobody was steering toward. それでも reports a course that held steady on purpose, against pressure everyone could already see coming.

```jp
薬を飲んだ。ところが熱は上がった。
薬を飲んだ。それでも熱は下がらなかった。
```

*I took the medicine. However, my fever went up unexpectedly.*
*I took the medicine. Even so, my fever didn't go down.*

The fever spiking is the twist ところが is built for — nobody expected the medicine to backfire. The fever holding steady is the opposite of a twist: everyone half-expected the obstacle to win, and それでも is just confirming that it did. If you can rewrite a それでも sentence as "and, unsurprisingly, the obstacle won anyway," you've got the right word.

反面 is worth one more line, mostly so you don't confuse the shapes. 反面 welds directly onto a plain-form clause inside a single sentence, describing two sides of the same subject at once — it never needs a full stop before it the way それでも does. Once you're watching for that period, the two stop looking anything alike.

That's the whole pattern: name the obstacle, then let それでも announce that it lost. It's a small word to carry that much weight — one clause of setup, one word of pivot, one clause of payoff — but that's exactly why it reads as calm instead of dramatic. It isn't fighting the obstacle; it's just reporting, flatly, that the obstacle didn't get its way.

Hold onto the ところが comparison — once そこで shows up, you'll see it pick up right where それでも leaves off, moving the story from "and yet, nothing changed" to "so here's what I did about it."
