You're at the library with a friend's paperback and a pen in hand, ready to underline a line you love. Before you touch the page: 図書館の本にペンで書いてはいけない。The book isn't yours to mark up — てはいけない is how Japanese slams a rule shut, no negotiation.

## How it works

てはいけない bolts onto the te-form of a verb — the same te-form you already use for てある, ておく, and the rest of that family — and adds はいけない, literally "as for [doing that], it's not good." Together the two pieces read as a flat prohibition: *must not*.

```jp
書く → 書いて
図書館の本にペンで書いてはいけない。
```

*Write → to write.*

*You must not write in library books with a pen.*

The same shape works for any verb once you have its te-form. Loud talking in a library gets the same treatment.

```jp
図書館で大きい声で話してはいけない。
```

*Don't talk in a loud voice in the library.*

Notice the structure never changes shape for who's being told off — no conjugation for "you" versus "everyone." The rule sits on the verb, not the person.

## Rules and exceptions

てはいけない only attaches to the **affirmative** te-form. There's no such thing as stacking a negative onto it to soften the meaning — `書かないてはいけない` isn't a real sentence, it's just two grammar pieces mashed together that don't combine that way.

> てはいけない doesn't get politer or ruder by putting です/ます in front of it — the bluntness is baked into the pattern itself, which is why it shows up on signs and warnings more than in relaxed conversation between friends.

Some things are worth stating flatly enough that softening them would be missing the point.

```jp
財布を忘れてはいけない。
```

*You must not forget your wallet.*

There's also てはだめだ, a slightly more conversational cousin that swaps いけない for だめ (だめ meaning roughly "no good"). The meaning is identical — pick whichever one your ear likes better.

```jp
ここに座ってはだめだ。
```

*You mustn't sit here.*

## Casual speech

In fast, casual speech the ては contracts down to ちゃ, and いけない or だめだ often drops off entirely, leaving just ちゃだめ hanging at the end. It's the same "no" as てはいけない, just worn smooth by everyday use.

```jp
話しちゃだめ。
忘れちゃだめ。
```

*Don't talk.*

*Don't forget it.*

You'll hear this constantly from parents to kids and friends to each other — it's the version people actually say, while てはいけない is the version printed on the sign.

## Contrast

You already know てもいい for "it's fine if you do this" — permission granted. てはいけない is its mirror image: permission denied. Swap one for the other in the same sentence and you flip the entire meaning of what's allowed.

```jp
ここに座ってもいいですか。
```

*Is it okay if I sit here?*

Ask that question and get 座ってはいけない back, and you know exactly where you stand — nowhere near that chair.

Rules like this only cover what you can't do right now. Later you'll meet なければならない for what you *have* to do — a different kind of pressure, obligation instead of prohibition, but it rhymes with everything you just learned about how these blunt little patterns work.
