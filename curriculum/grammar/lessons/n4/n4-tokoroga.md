You bought a new pair of shoes. They were on sale, the color was exactly what you wanted, and you walked out of the store feeling like you'd won. Less than a week later, the sole peels off the left one while you're crossing the street.

```jp
新しい靴を買った。とても安かった。
ところが、すぐに壊れた。
```
*I bought new shoes. They were very cheap.*
*However, they broke right away.*

That third line is where ところが earns its keep. The first two sentences built up an expectation — new shoes, a good price, nothing to complain about — and then ところが yanks the story somewhere you didn't see coming. That's the whole job of this word: it marks the exact point where a situation you thought you understood turns out to have a catch.

It's tempting to translate ところが as a plain "but," and most dictionaries will hand you exactly that gloss. It isn't wrong, but it undersells what's happening. "But" can join two facts that were never expected to line up in the first place — "the shoes were cheap, but I don't really like the color" is a mild, low-stakes "but." ところが is reserved for the moment the story actively double-crosses you. If nobody would raise an eyebrow at your sentence, ところが is probably too strong a word for it.

## How it works

ところが sits at the front of its own sentence, right after the full stop of the sentence before it. Nothing about that first sentence changes shape to make room for it — no special ending, no attaching to a verb stem, no plain-form requirement the way something like かどうか demands. You finish your first sentence normally, in whatever form you'd already use — plain or polite, past or present — then start a fresh sentence with ところが, drop a comma, and say what actually happened.

```jp
新しいレストランに行った。
ところが、値段がとても高かった。
```
*I went to a new restaurant.*
*However, the price was very expensive.*

The shape is always: Sentence. + ところが、+ Sentence. Because ところが is doing all the connecting work by itself, it never needs help from another word — you won't see it stacked onto something else in the same slot. It stands alone, and it always opens the sentence that carries the surprise, never the one that sets it up.

It also doesn't care what kind of sentence it's attaching to. The first half can end in a verb, an adjective, or a noun-plus-copula, in plain speech or polite speech, and ところが sits down after it exactly the same way every time. Compare that to something like かどうか, which only accepts a plain-form verb or adjective right before it and rejects です outright — ところが has no such restriction, because it isn't attaching to anything at all. It's standing at the start of a brand-new sentence, looking back at the one that just ended.

## Rules and exceptions

ところが wants something that actually happened — a real event you're reporting on, usually a first half in the past tense that turned out to be a setup for a second half nobody predicted. It isn't built for hedging about the future or floating a plan that might fall through; it's for telling someone what did happen, and how it didn't go the way the first sentence implied.

```jp
友達を駅で待った。
ところが、友達は来なかった。
```
*I waited for my friend at the station.*
*However, my friend didn't come.*

> Use ところが on two facts that were never actually in tension with each other and it reads like a plot twist that never arrives — the listener braces for a reversal and gets a shrug instead. Save it for a moment where something genuinely goes sideways; if nothing sideways happened, a plain connecting word does the job better.

Real speech is a little messier than that rule makes it sound. You'll hear ところが used in narration, news reports, and casual storytelling even when the "surprise" is fairly mild — more of a scene-turn than a shock. Don't fight that texture too hard while you're still getting a feel for it; leaning toward genuine reversal is the safer habit to build first, and the softer, everyday uses will come naturally once the core sense is solid.

This one is worth sitting with, because you already have the instinct for it in English — you say "but then" or "except" in exactly this spot without thinking. The only new muscle is remembering that in Japanese, that pivot word starts its own sentence instead of getting stitched onto the end of the one before it.

There's also a habit worth building around who ところが is allowed to surprise. It almost always describes something happening to the speaker, or something the speaker is reporting on as an outside observer — not a plan the speaker themselves is still deciding on. If you haven't lived through the twist yet, or you're only guessing that something might go wrong, ところが is the wrong tool; you'd want a different, more tentative way of talking about it. ところが speaks with the confidence of someone describing something that's already over and done.

## Contrast: ところが vs. 反面

You've already met 反面 for holding two sides of the same thing up against each other — a "while at the same time" kind of contrast. It's built for describing standing qualities, not for narrating an event that overturned what you expected.

```jp
兄は厳しい反面、優しい。
```
*My older brother is strict, while also being kind.*

That sentence is a portrait: two traits that sit side by side in the same person, all the time, with nothing surprising about either one. ところが needs something different — a timeline, a before and an after, something that was true and then, unexpectedly, stopped being true.

It helps to notice that 反面 sentences usually still make sense if you flip the order — "strict while also kind" and "kind while also strict" describe the same person either way. Try that with ところが and it breaks immediately: "always strict, however today kind" tells a specific story in a specific direction, and reversing it — "kind today, however always strict" — describes someone completely different. That directionality is the real fingerprint of ところが. It isn't just joining two ideas; it's insisting that the second one landed as a surprise given the first.

```jp
兄はいつも厳しい。
ところが、今日は優しかった。
```
*My older brother is always strict.*
*However, today he was kind.*

Same brother, the same two qualities even, but now there's a story: always strict, until today, when he wasn't. Try swapping in 反面 here and the sentence falls apart — 反面 doesn't know what to do with "today" breaking a pattern; all it knows how to do is hold two sides of one unchanging picture at once.

That's the test you can run in your head. If the honest next question is "and then what happened?" and the answer overturns the first half, reach for ところが. If you're just describing two sides of the same coin with no event involved, 反面 is the better tool.

Neither of them is right or wrong in some absolute sense — they're answering different questions. 反面 answers "what is this thing like?" ところが answers "what happened, and how did it not go the way it looked like it would?" Once you can hear that difference, mixing them up gets a lot harder to do by accident.

Hold onto that distinction — それでも lives in the same neighborhood, for the moments later on when the surprise doesn't end the story, it just makes whatever comes next harder to pull off.
