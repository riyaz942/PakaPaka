Picture this: you're texting a friend about tomorrow's hike, but the forecast is shaky. You don't want to promise it'll rain — you just want to cover what happens if it does. That's the shape 場合 is built for. It doesn't predict anything. It labels a possible scenario and lets you say what you'll do if that one turns out to be the one that happens.

Japanese has plenty of ways to talk about conditions, and you'll meet more of them soon. 場合 is the one for laying out a specific case — this situation, as opposed to some other one — and then responding to it directly.

## How it works

場合 (ばあい) is itself a noun meaning "case" or "instance." You build the scenario in front of it, then treat the whole thing as a launching point for what comes next. With a verb, the clause in front just stays in plain form — the same shape you'd use to end a sentence on its own.

```jp
電車が遅れる場合は、連絡してください。
```

*If the train is late, please contact me.*

Notice 遅れる sits there completely unchanged, present-tense and plain, the way it would if you were just saying "the train is late" to a friend. 場合は then turns that into "in the case where that happens."

With a noun in front, you can't jump straight to 場合 — you need の bridging the two nouns first, the same の that links 友達の家 or 私の傘.

```jp
雨 → 雨の場合
```

*rain → in the case of rain*

```jp
雨の場合、傘を持って行きます。
```

*In case of rain, I'll bring an umbrella.*

Past and negative clauses slot in exactly the same way, because they're still plain forms — 場合 doesn't care about timing, only about the shape of what's in front of it.

```jp
分からない場合は、先生に聞いてください。
```

*If you don't understand, please ask the teacher.*

```jp
財布を無くした場合、警官に言います。
```

*If I lose my wallet, I'll tell a police officer.*

That second one is worth sitting with — 無くした is past tense, describing a scenario that hasn't happened yet. That feels backwards in English, but in Japanese the past form there just marks the loss as a completed event *within* the hypothetical, the way "if I've lost my wallet" reads more naturally than "if I lose my wallet" once you notice it.

## Rules and exceptions

The の between a noun and 場合 isn't optional style — it's structural.

> `病気場合` isn't a sentence a Japanese speaker would produce. It has to be `病気の場合` — の is doing real grammatical work, not sitting there for decoration.

は after 場合 is common but not required. Reach for it when you're contrasting this case against some other one you're aware of; skip it when you're simply laying out one scenario on its own.

```jp
病気の場合、学校を休みます。
```

*If I'm sick, I take the day off school.*

```jp
忙しい場合は、手伝ってください。
```

*If you're busy, please help me.*

One more trap worth naming early: even when the scenario you're describing is about something that might happen later, the clause in front of 場合 stays plain present or plain past — never a guessing form like `遅れるだろう場合`. 場合 wants a fact-shaped clause sitting in front of it; the hedging about whether it'll actually happen lives outside the grammar, in your tone and context, not baked into the sentence itself.

## 場合 vs たびに

You've already met たびに for "every time X happens, Y happens" — a pattern that repeats on schedule with its trigger. On the page, たびに and 場合 look like cousins: both sit after a plain-form clause or noun+の. But they're answering different questions. たびに says *this happens every single time, without fail*. 場合 says *if this particular thing comes up, here's my plan* — with no claim at all about how often, or whether, it actually will.

```jp
雨が降るたびに、傘を忘れます。
```

*Every time it rains, I forget my umbrella.*

Set that next to the 雨の場合 line from earlier. 雨が降るたびに is basically a confession about a pattern in your life — it keeps happening, and the same thing follows every time. 雨の場合 makes no claim about how often it rains at all; it just says, should that be the case today, here's the plan. Swap one for the other and you've swapped a running joke about your own forgetfulness for a calm, one-off contingency plan.

Hold onto that distinction — 場合 sets up a scenario without committing to it coming true, and that's exactly the territory と will move into soon, for situations where one thing leads to another automatically, no maybe about it.
