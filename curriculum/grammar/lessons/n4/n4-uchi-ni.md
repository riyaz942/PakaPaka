Your friend just poured you tea, and it's exactly the kind you love — hot enough to fog the cup, poured straight from the kettle. You get pulled into conversation, thirty seconds pass, and now it's lukewarm and a little sad. The move isn't "drink your tea" — it's drink it *now*, because this particular window is closing whether you notice or not.

```jp
お茶が熱いうちに飲んだ方がいいですよ。
```
*You should drink the tea while it's still hot.*

That's うちに — a pattern for describing a state that's true right now but won't stay true, paired with something you need to squeeze in before it changes. The textbook gloss is "while" or "during," but that undersells it. The real feeling is closer to: there's a clock running, and everyone in the sentence knows it.

## How it works

うちに attaches to a plain-form clause — the same shape you already use in front of ことにする or ところ (the plain form is just the casual, unconjugated shape of a verb or adjective: 食べる, not 食べます). い-adjectives take it directly:

```jp
熱い → 熱いうちに
```

The window here is "however long the tea stays hot." Once that's over, so is your chance.

Verbs usually show up in their ている shape in front of うちに, because the window this pattern points at is almost always an ongoing state, not a single finished action:

```jp
子供が寝ているうちに掃除しましょう。
```
*Let's clean while the child is sleeping.*

The child being asleep is the window. It's open right now, it's temporary, and the sentence tells you exactly what to do with it before it shuts.

The other shape you'll meet constantly is negative: ない form + うちに, meaning "before [something that hasn't happened yet happens]." This one might actually be more common in daily speech than the plain affirmative version, because "do this before that" is a shape people reach for all the time.

```jp
忘れないうちに名前を書いた。
```
*I wrote down the name before I could forget it.*

```jp
忘れる → 忘れないうちに
```

The logic flips slightly here: the window is "the stretch where you still remember," and it slams shut the instant you forget. 忘れないうちに is close to a set phrase in Japanese — people reach for it constantly, well beyond any one grammar lesson.

うちに also attaches to na-adjectives with な (元気なうちに, "while you're still healthy") and to nouns with の, which turns a whole stretch of time into the window itself:

```jp
若いうちにたくさん旅行した方がいい。
```
*You should travel a lot while you're young.*

"Young" isn't one instant — it's a stretch of years quietly running out the whole time you have it, which is exactly the shape of thing うちに was built for.

## Rules and exceptions

The state inside the うちに clause has to still be open. That means the nonpast plain form, or ている for something ongoing — never the た-form. `寝たうちに` isn't something a native speaker would say: た marks something already finished, and うちに needs a window that hasn't shut yet. Once the state is over, うちに has nothing left to point at.

> うちに usually assumes the window is closing on its own — daylight fading, tea cooling, someone falling asleep — not because of anything the main-clause action does. If the action you're describing is what actually ends the state (you wake the child up by cleaning loudly, say), the sentence starts working against itself. Pick a different action, or a different pattern.

It also doesn't fit a state with no end in sight. You wouldn't use it for something permanent, because there's no clock to race — and racing a clock is the entire reason this pattern exists.

## Contrast

You already know 間・間に for "during" — noun+の or verb+ている間(に). Run the same scenario through both, and the difference becomes obvious:

```jp
週末の間に宿題をした。
```
*I did my homework during the weekend.*

```jp
週末のうちに宿題をした。
```
*I got my homework done while it was still the weekend.*

Same two nouns, same event, very different feeling. 間に is just reporting a timeframe — no urgency, no sense that anything was about to slip away. うちに frames the weekend as a window with an expiration date, and the sentence carries a quiet "I made it in time." Whenever the scenario has that flavor of racing a deadline — real or implied — うちに is the one doing the work; 間に would flatten the sentence into a plain fact.

Next time something around you is fading — the tea, the daylight, someone's patience — you've got the phrase for racing it. That same instinct, "this window won't stay open forever," is exactly what you'll need for たびに later, except たびに handles windows that reopen again and again instead of closing for good.
