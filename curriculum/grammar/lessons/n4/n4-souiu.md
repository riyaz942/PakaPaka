Your friend is describing someone from work, and by the end of it you're just shaking your head: "そういう話は聞きたくない。" You didn't ask for every detail, and you don't need it — the moment you heard the shape of the story, you already knew you didn't want to hear the rest. そういう does exactly that job for you: it points at a whole category of thing — talk, people, weather, food, whatever — without naming a single specific example.

```jp
そういう話は聞きたくない。
```

*I don't want to hear that kind of talk.*

そういう means roughly "that kind of," and it comes with two close cousins that split the same job by distance. こういう means "this kind of" — something right in front of you, or something you're about to describe yourself. ああいう means "that kind of" too, but for something far from both you and the person you're talking to, the same distant feeling あれ carries when you point at something across the room instead of something in your hand. So this isn't three unrelated words to memorize; it's one job (naming a category instead of a specific thing) done at three distances, the same way これ・それ・あれ split "this/that/that over there."

Why bother with a category word at all, instead of just describing the thing directly? Because most of the time you don't actually want to relitigate the details. Your friend's story wasn't really about one coworker — it was about a whole familiar type of story, the kind you've heard a dozen times with different names swapped in. そういう lets you skip straight to that judgment without pretending you need to hear it again.

## How it works

All three attach directly in front of a noun — no gap, no particle, nothing between them and the word they're modifying. Whatever sits right after the blank is the thing being sorted into a category, and そういう itself tells you nothing on its own; it's entirely about what follows it.

```jp
こういう天気は苦手だ。
```

*I'm no good with this kind of weather.*

```jp
ああいう人には会いたくない。
```

*I don't want to meet that kind of person.*

Notice the shape of the word itself, because it explains a lot of what comes next: そういう ends in う, not い. That matters more than it looks like it should. Words that end in い — 高い, 面白い — are i-adjectives, adjectives that inflect on their own the way an English verb does, and plenty of them can end a sentence all by themselves (this is delicious → おいしい。). そういう never does that. It has exactly one form, forever, and that one form exists only to sit in front of a noun.

```jp
彼はそういう人だ。
```

*He's that kind of person.*

Look closely at what's actually being described in that sentence: it isn't そういう, it's 彼, described through 人. そういう just supplies the category — it tells you what kind of 人 you're dealing with, then gets out of the way. Try to drop the noun and let そういう carry the sentence by itself, and it stops meaning anything at all.

> `そういうだ` isn't a sentence on its own. そういう has no meaning without a noun sitting directly after it — you need something like そういう人だ or そういうものだ, never そういう standing alone.

## Rules and exceptions

The noun after そういう・こういう・ああいう doesn't have to be a concrete word like 話 or 人. Very often it's a placeholder noun instead — こと (a matter, an abstract thing that happened or was said) or もの (a thing, used more for the nature or essence of something). You reach for these when you're categorizing an idea rather than an object you could point at.

```jp
あの人はそういうことを言う人ではない。
```

*He's not the kind of person who says that sort of thing.*

Here そういうこと isn't naming a specific sentence someone said — it's naming the *category* of thing that gets said, the same way "that kind of talk" works in English without quoting anyone directly. This is one of the most common jobs そういう does in real conversation: summing up an entire remark, complaint, or situation as "that sort of thing" instead of repeating it.

You'll also run into そういうの constantly in casual speech — の here is the nominalizer, a little word that turns a whole description into a thing you can talk about, so そういうの means something like "that kind of thing/one" used loosely for anything from an object to a whole situation. It's shorter and more casual than そういうもの, and you'll hear it far more often in conversation than in writing.

One more sharp edge, and it's an easy one to trip on because it looks so close: こんな, そんな, and あんな — words you already know as "this/that kind of" — cover similar ground, but they don't behave the same way grammatically. They're shaped like na-adjectives, so they can end a sentence on their own (そんなだ works as a loose comment, "it's just like that") and they can modify a verb directly (そんなに食べないで, "don't eat that much"). そういう can't do either of those things — it only ever sits directly in front of a noun. When you're not sure which to reach for and a noun follows right away, そういう is the safer, more neutral-sounding choice; こんな/そんな/あんな tend to carry more of the speaker's feeling about the thing.

This trips learners twice, usually in opposite directions. Some people, once they learn そんな, try to stretch it in front of every noun the way they'd use そういう — that mostly works, but it quietly adds an emotional color (surprise, complaint, admiration) that plain そういう doesn't carry. Others learn そういう first and then try to use it to end a sentence the way そんなだ does, which simply doesn't work; there's no version of そういう that stands alone. Keep the two families separate in your head by function rather than by translation: そういう describes and categorizes, そんな reacts.

## Contrast

You already have に関して for bringing up a topic — その書類に関して, "regarding that document." On the surface it looks like it's doing something similar to そういう, since both can follow a demonstrative and both can precede or set up a noun phrase. They aren't interchangeable, though, and the difference matters.

```jp
そういう書類 → その書類に関して
```

*that kind of document → regarding that document*

そういう labels the noun as belonging to a category — a type of document, not one specific piece of paper. に関して doesn't sort anything into a category at all; it just marks a topic, "on the subject of this." Swap one for the other and the sentence stops meaning what you wanted. そういう書類にはよく問題がある is a general comment about documents of that type — this kind of paperwork tends to have problems. その書類に関して質問がある is about one particular document already sitting in front of you — you have a question about this exact piece of paper, not about its category.

```jp
こういう話は前からあった。
```

*This kind of story has been around for a while.*

That last one is a small, useful habit: こういう話は前からあった doesn't claim anything about one specific story you just heard — it's a comment on the whole category, the observation that stories shaped like this one keep coming up. That's the real payoff of そういう・こういう・ああいう: they let you talk about a pattern you've noticed without having to pin down or repeat a single example of it.

That's the whole shape of it: そういう・こういう・ああいう sort things into a category by distance, they never stand alone, and they only ever point forward at a noun. Hold onto that habit of pointing at something without naming it — it's exactly the instinct って will ask of you soon, when you start quoting other people's words the same loose way.
