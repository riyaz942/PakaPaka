You're at a friend's place and there's food on the table that looks too good to leave alone. You don't just reach for it — you check first: これ、食べてもいいですか。 That's the whole shape of てもいい: te-form plus いい, and it turns any verb into "is it okay if I—".

## How it works

てもいい is built from the te-form you already know, plus いい (good). Literally it reads as "even doing X is good" — the て part sets up the action, いい signs off on it. Once you can make a te-form, you already have this pattern.

```jp
食べる → 食べても → 食べてもいい
```

*to eat → even if (I) eat → it's fine if (I) eat / may I eat*

Ask it as a question with か and you get permission-seeking; say it flat and you're granting permission to someone else.

```jp
ここに座ってもいいですか。
```

*May I sit here?*

```jp
窓を開けてもいいですか。
```

*May I open the window?*

Answering someone else's request uses the same form, just without the question:

```jp
もう帰ってもいいですよ。
```

*You may go home now.*

The よ at the end isn't part of the grammar point — it's just the ordinary "letting you know" particle, doing the same job it always does.

## Rules and exceptions

てもいい attaches to the te-form of any verb — there's no group it refuses to work with. The politeness lives entirely in です: drop it and you're at plain-speech level, keep it and you're neutral-polite.

There's a second accepted shape, てもかまわない ("even if X, it doesn't matter" — かまう is "to mind, to be bothered"). It answers the same question but with a shrug instead of a nod: less "sure, go ahead," more "I genuinely don't care either way."

```jp
電話を使ってもいいですか。
```

*May I use the phone?*

```jp
もう帰ってもかまわない。
```

*It's fine if you go home already.*

> Don't try to build the negative by just adding a negative verb inside the pattern — `帰ってもいいじゃない` reads as a totally different thing (a rhetorical "isn't it fine if...?"), not "you may not go home." Permission-denial is its own pattern, coming up separately.

One more trap: てもいい answers "is this allowed," not "is this necessary." If someone asks whether they *have* to do something, this pattern can't answer that question either way — that's a different piece of grammar you'll meet soon.

## Casual speech

Drop です and the question softens into something you'd say to a friend rather than a stranger:

- てもいいですか → てもいい？ (rising intonation carries the question mark)
- てもいいですよ → てもいいよ

Nothing about the te-form itself changes — only the politeness layer peels off, the same way it does everywhere else.

## Contrast: てもいい vs ても

You already know ても for "even if / even though" — it sets up a condition that doesn't change the outcome. てもいい borrows that exact same て...も shape, then bolts on いい to turn "even if" into "and that's fine." Drop いい and you're back to plain ても, which just describes a fact regardless of permission:

```jp
雨が降っても、公園で遊びます。
```

*Even if it rains, (we're) playing at the park.*

Compare that to 遊んでもいいですか — one is reporting what happens no matter what, the other is asking whether something is allowed to happen at all. Same skeleton, completely different job once いい shows up.

That's the whole pattern: te-form, いい, done. Hold onto the shape — you'll meet its two mirror images soon, one for "not allowed" and one for "don't have to."
