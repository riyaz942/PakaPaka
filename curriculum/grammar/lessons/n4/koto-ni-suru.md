Your doctor just told you your blood pressure is a little high, and you're sitting in the waiting room deciding what to actually do about it. Not "I should probably think about maybe cutting back" — an actual decision, made right now, that you're committing to. That's the job ことにする does: it turns a choice you've just made into a sentence.

```jp
明日から毎朝早く起きることにする。
```
*I've decided to start getting up early every morning from tomorrow.*

The moment you say that sentence, the decision exists. Nobody made it for you, nothing forced it — you weighed it and chose it, and ことにする is the grammar that records the choice.

## How it works

こと is a **nominalizer** — a little word that turns a whole verb phrase into a thing you can talk about, the way "going" turns "go" into something you can point at. ことにする then works with に + する (to do) — literally something like "make it the thing of going," which is a clunky translation but explains why the whole phrase reads as *deciding on* an action.

The attachment point is the plain dictionary form of the verb, exactly as it appears in a dictionary, right before こと.

```jp
今年は日本語を習うことにしました。
```
*I've decided to learn Japanese this year.*

When the choice is to stop or avoid something instead, the ない form goes in that same slot.

```jp
甘い物を食べないことにする。
```
*I've decided not to eat sweets.*

する verbs behave exactly the same way — the する in 食事をする is just doing its normal job as "to do," and the whole phrase still slots in front of ことにする like any other dictionary form.

```jp
週末はレストランで食事をすることにしました。
```
*I've decided to have a meal at a restaurant this weekend.*

Both tenses matter here, and they carry a real difference. ことにする, left in the present, sounds like the decision is happening as you speak — you're announcing it. ことにしました, in the past, reports a decision you already made and are now living by. Most of the time you'll reach for ことにしました, because you're usually telling someone about a choice you settled on earlier, not narrating it live.

## Rules and exceptions

> ことにする is strictly about **your own** decision. If the outcome was decided by someone else, by circumstances, or by a rule, this pattern is the wrong tool — that job belongs to ことになる, which you'll meet soon.

A common wrong guess is reaching for the て-form or the ます-stem before こと. Neither works. It has to be the plain dictionary form for "I will do this" or the plain ない form for "I won't do this" — nothing in between.

```jp
飲む → 飲まないことにする
```

That arrow is the whole rule in miniature: take the dictionary form, negate it if the decision is to stop doing something, then attach ことにする. Nothing else changes.

## Contrast

You already have つもり for stating an intention — "I plan to." つもり can be looser than ことにする; it can describe something you're leaning toward without having fully committed.

```jp
来月旅行するつもりです。
```
*I plan to travel next month.*

Swap that つもり for ことにする and the sentence tightens: it stops sounding like a plan you're still holding loosely and starts sounding like a decision you've closed the door on.

よう にする lives in a nearby but different space too — it's about the ongoing effort to make something a habit, not the single moment of deciding.

```jp
毎日野菜を食べるようにする。
```
*I'm making it a habit to eat vegetables every day.*

ことにする is the decision itself, made once. ようにする is the standing effort you keep applying afterward — you might say ことにする the day you decide to change your diet, then describe the follow-through with ようにする for months after.

```jp
お酒を飲まないことにしました。
```
*I've decided not to drink alcohol.*

That's the shape you'll use constantly once you start noticing it — someone announcing a resolution, big or small, about their own behavior.

Hold onto this one: ことになる looks almost identical on the page but flips who's behind the wheel — it shows up next, for decisions and outcomes that land on you rather than ones you make.
