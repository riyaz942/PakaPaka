Imagine you try on a jacket at a shop, glance at the price tag, and quietly put it back on the rack without a word. Your friend asks what happened. You don't need a whole sentence explaining that the price is "high" — you need one word that says the price crossed a line you weren't willing to cross. That word is すぎる: it takes almost any adjective or verb you already know and turns it into "too much of that." You didn't say the jacket was ugly or the wrong color — the whole complaint was about degree, about how far past reasonable the number on the tag had gone. すぎる is built exactly for that shape of complaint, and once you have it, you'll reach for it constantly: for prices, for weather, for how spicy something turned out to be.

```jp
このシャツは高すぎる。
```
*This shirt is too expensive.*

## How it works

すぎる is, underneath everything, a completely ordinary verb — it's built on the same ichidan pattern as 食べる or 見る. Once you attach it to something, the whole phrase conjugates as if すぎる were the only verb in the sentence: すぎます, すぎました, すぎない. That's actually good news: instead of memorizing a separate ending for adjectives and a separate one for verbs, you only ever memorize one ending, and then you memorize one small rule about what to feed it. What actually changes from case to case is what you glue it onto, and that depends on whether you're starting from an adjective or a verb.

For an い-adjective, you drop the final い and add すぎる directly onto what's left. 高い loses its い, leaving 高, and 高すぎる is what you get — the sentence you just read. For a verb, you reach for the stem you already use before ます: drop ます, and whatever's left is what すぎる attaches to. If you can already put a verb into its polite ます-form, you already know the exact piece すぎる needs — there's no separate stem to learn.

```jp
飲む → 飲みすぎる
```
*to drink → to drink too much*

な-adjectives are the simplest of the three: you attach すぎる straight onto the adjective with nothing in between — no な, no い, nothing to drop first.

```jp
この部屋は静かすぎる。
```
*This room is too quiet.*

A silent room doesn't sound like a complaint in English, but picture walking into one expecting normal background noise and getting nothing — that's exactly the kind of mild unease すぎる is built to report.

Because すぎる takes over as the main verb once it's attached, tense and politeness live on it, not on the original word. A polite past sentence just adds ました the same way any other ichidan verb would.

```jp
昨日、働きすぎました。
```
*I worked too much yesterday.*

Notice that the original verb never changes past its stem here — all the grammatical weight of "yesterday," "finished," and "politely" sits on すぎました, while the stem just tells you what kind of excess happened.

## Rules and exceptions

すぎる has to land on a stem — never on the full dictionary form of a verb, and never on an い-adjective that's still wearing its い. `食べるすぎる` and `高いすぎる` are both wrong for the same reason: the stem has already done the job of marking "this is a verb" or "this is an adjective," so すぎる doesn't need that ending spelled out a second time before it can attach. Think of the stem as すぎる's way of asking "how much of *what*?" — it needs the bare core of the word, not the finished, already-conjugated version of it, or the question doesn't parse.

> いい is the one word that refuses to follow its own rule. It doesn't become `いすぎる` — instead it quietly borrows the stem of its older, more formal twin 良い, so what you actually get is よすぎる.

```jp
いい → よすぎる
```
*good → too good (to be true)*

This one trips people up the first time they meet it, mostly because nothing about いい looks like it's hiding a second word inside it. It's worth just memorizing よすぎる as its own small fact rather than trying to derive it from a rule.

Suru-verbs follow the ordinary verb logic, not some special exception: strip する down to the pre-し stem, the same stem you'd use before other suffixes, and attach すぎる there.

```jp
彼は勉強しすぎる。
```
*He studies too much.*

すぎる leans negative most of the time — nobody reaches for "too much" to describe something they're happy about — but it isn't grammatically required to be a complaint. よすぎる, from just a moment ago, is the clearest proof of that: something can be so good it starts to feel suspicious, and すぎる is exactly the tool for saying so without a separate word for "suspiciously."

## Contrast

You've already learned にくい and やすい, and they attach to a verb's stem exactly the way すぎる does — which means, on paper, three totally different sentences can look almost identical until you read past the ending. It helps to be precise about what each one is actually reporting. にくい and やすい describe how *difficult* or *easy* an action is to carry out. すぎる doesn't care about difficulty at all — it's reporting *how much* of something happened, full stop.

```jp
肉を切りすぎる。
```
*You're cutting the meat too much.*

```jp
この肉は切りにくい。
```
*This meat is hard to cut.*

Same verb, same stem, and yet those two sentences describe completely different problems. The first one is about quantity — too many cuts, pieces sliced smaller than they needed to be. The second is about resistance — the knife is struggling, maybe the meat is too tough, and no amount of stopping earlier would have fixed that. Swap one ending for the other and you've accidentally complained about the wrong thing entirely, which is a very normal mistake the first few times you use both patterns in the same week.

One quick way to keep them apart in the moment: にくい and やすい only ever make sense as a description of the action itself, independent of how many times anyone's actually done it — a rusty lock is 開けにくい whether you've turned the key once or fifty times. すぎる, on the other hand, is always keeping a running total. It only shows up once that total has gotten out of hand, which is why it so often lands on a completed action, like 昨日、働きすぎました, rather than a general property of the world.

Once you can reliably spot "an amount that crossed a line," you're set up nicely for あげく later on — the pattern for describing what finally happens *after* you've kept doing too much of something for too long. すぎる tells you the moment it became too much; あげく tells you what that excess eventually cost you.
