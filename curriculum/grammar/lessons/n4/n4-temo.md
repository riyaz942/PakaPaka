You're supposed to meet a friend at the park at six. At five-thirty it starts raining, hard. You text them anyway: 雨が降っても、公園を歩く。 Rain or no rain, you're going. That flip — "no matter what happens, the plan holds" — is what ても does. It bolts onto the te-form you already know and turns any verb or adjective into "even if / even though X."

## How it works

The base is the same te-form you've been using for ている, てある, ておく, and the rest — you're just handing it to a new job. Take the te-form, add も (the word that works like *even / also*), and you get "even if / even though."

```jp
忙しくても、毎日勉強する。
```

*Even if I'm busy, I study every day.*

With verbs, the te-form does all the work — も just rides on the end. With い-adjectives, drop the い, add くて, then も:

```jp
忙しい → 忙しくて → 忙しくても
```

For な-adjectives and nouns, the connector is で, and ても becomes でも (the same shape as the word for "but" — more on that below):

```jp
元気でも、今日は休む。
```

*Even though I'm fine, I'm resting today.*

```jp
元気 → 元気で → 元気でも
```

One more shape worth knowing early: pair ても with a question word like 何回 (how many times) and you get "no matter how many times."

```jp
何回聞いても、分からない。
```

*No matter how many times I ask, I don't understand.*

That's the whole mechanic. One connector, four flavors depending on what it's attached to — verb, い-adjective, な-adjective/noun, or a question word.

## Rules and exceptions

ても doesn't care whether the outcome is good or bad, expected or not — it just says "this condition doesn't change the result." Compare that to から (for reasons) or ので (softer reasons), which you'll meet elsewhere: those explain *why* something happens. ても explains why something *doesn't stop* happening.

```jp
高くても、あの時計を買う。
```

*Even if it's expensive, I'll buy that watch.*

Nobody's giving a reason here — 高い isn't the cause of buying the watch, it's the obstacle that fails to change the decision. That's the whole flavor of ても: expect it wherever a sentence in English has "even if," "no matter," or "however X is."

> A common first-week slip is dropping the も and just leaving the te-form alone: `忙しくて、毎日勉強する` reads as "I'm busy, and so I study every day" — a plain reason, the opposite of what you meant. The も is not optional decoration; it's the whole concession.

でも after a noun or な-adjective looks exactly like でも the conjunction meaning "but" (the one sitting in your vocabulary list already). They really are the same word wearing two hats: sentence-initial でも contrasts two whole sentences, while attached でも at the end of a noun or な-adjective contrasts a single condition against its result. Context tells them apart fast — one starts a sentence, the other ends a clause.

## Contrast

You already know a handful of jobs the te-form can do: ている marks something in progress, てしまう marks something finished (often with a little regret), ていく and てくる track a change moving away from or toward you. ても is another one of those jobs, and it's worth keeping straight from the others precisely because the *shape* is so similar — te-form plus one more piece.

The difference is what that piece asks. ている, てしまう, ていく, and てくる all describe what's happening *to the action itself* — is it ongoing, complete, moving. ても doesn't touch the action's own status at all; it's entirely about the relationship between a condition and a result that refuses to budge. 忙しくても勉強する isn't telling you anything about the studying being finished or ongoing — it's telling you the studying happens *regardless* of the business.

## Closing thought

Every time you reach for ても, you're saying "here's the obstacle, and here's the outcome that ignores it." Hold onto that shape — you'll want it again soon, when てもいい turns the same connector into asking permission instead of describing defiance.
