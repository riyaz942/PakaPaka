It's late, you're at the desk with your textbook open, and your eyelids have that heavy pull that means you're one blink away from losing the fight. You're not asleep yet — you catch yourself right on the edge of it. Japanese has one small pattern built exactly for that edge, the instant before something happens: そうになる.

```jp
寝る → 寝そうになる
```

*to sleep → to be right on the edge of falling asleep*

## How it works

そうになる attaches to the stem of a verb — the same stem you already strip off before adding other endings. For an ichidan verb like 寝る, drop the る to get 寝そうになる. For a godan verb, swap the final u-sound for its i-row partner first: 忘れる is actually ichidan too (忘れそうになる, not 忘れるそうになる), but a true godan verb like 泣く becomes 泣きそうになる instead of 泣くそうになる. Whatever the verb, the meaning is the same shape: the subject was right at the threshold of that action or state, close enough to feel it, before it either happened or was caught in time.

```jp
授業で寝そうになりました。
```

*In class, I nearly fell asleep.*

Most of the time you'll meet this pattern in the past tense, そうになった, because you're reporting a close call after the fact — the moment has already passed by the time you're telling someone about it.

```jp
約束を忘れそうになりました。
```

*I nearly forgot the appointment.*

## Rules and exceptions

そうになる almost always describes something the subject didn't actually want — falling, crying, forgetting, being late. Nothing in the grammar forces that, but in real use it's overwhelmingly the pattern for near misses you're glad didn't fully land.

> Don't confuse そうになる with the appearance pattern そうだ ("it looks like it's about to rain," 雨が降りそうだ). そうだ is a guess about something you're watching from outside; そうになる is the subject itself standing right at the edge of an event. The stem attachment looks similar, but the meaning is not interchangeable.

The other trap is attaching it to the wrong form. The stem loses its dictionary-form る or u-ending before そうになる goes on — `食べるそうになる` is wrong; it has to be 食べそうになる. If you can say the plain stem on its own and it sounds incomplete, that's the piece そうになる wants.

```jp
電車に遅れそうになりました。
```

*I nearly missed the train.*

```jp
階段で落ちそうになりました。
```

*I nearly fell on the stairs.*

```jp
悲しい映画を見て、泣きそうになりました。
```

*I watched a sad movie and nearly cried.*

## Contrast: そうになる vs ていく・てくる

You already know ていく and てくる for change that unfolds over a stretch of time — something drifting steadily in one direction. そうになる is the opposite shape: not a stretch, a single moment right at the tipping point. Put them side by side and the difference is obvious.

```jp
荷物がだんだん重くなっていく。
```

*The luggage keeps getting heavier and heavier.*

```jp
でも、今日は重くて、道で落ちそうになった。
```

*But today it was so heavy that I nearly dropped it in the street.*

The first sentence describes a slow slide that's still going; the second is one sharp instant inside that slide, the point where it almost got away from you.

## Closing thought

そうになる is for the instant something almost happens to you — the near miss, caught right before it lands. Later on you'll meet ようにする, which is the deliberate opposite: not something that almost happens to you, but something you push yourself to make happen on purpose.
