Your friend catches you leaving the classroom and asks how the test went. You don't want to say it was impossible — that's dramatic, and probably not even true. You also don't want to shrug it off, because it genuinely wasn't easy. What you actually mean sits in between: solidly, noticeably hard — more than "a little," short of "the worst thing that has ever happened to you." That middle ground, the one English speakers reach for with "pretty" or "fairly," is exactly what かなり covers.

```jp
今日の試験はかなり難しかった。
```
*Today's exam was quite difficult.*

かなり sits right in front of whatever you're describing and tells the listener "more than you'd expect — and I'm not exaggerating." It's the workhorse word for reporting things honestly: how a test went, what the weather's doing, how well someone cooks, how far away somewhere is. Once you have it, you stop reaching for the strongest word every time something is more than a little, and your Japanese starts sounding calibrated instead of breathless.

It also does something useful socially. If you tell your friend the test was 難しすぎる ("too difficult"), that sounds like a complaint about the test itself — maybe even the teacher. Say it was かなり難しかった instead, and you're just reporting a fact: it was hard, you noticed, no grudge attached. That's a distinction worth having before you need it.

You'll reach for かなり constantly once you notice how many everyday topics need it: weather (今日はかなり暑い, "it's quite hot today"), prices (このレストランはかなり高い, "this restaurant is quite expensive"), distance (駅はかなり遠い, "the station is quite far"). None of these are emergencies. They're just facts worth flagging clearly, and かなり is built exactly for that job.

## How it works

かなり never changes shape. It isn't an adjective, so there's no past tense or negative form built into it; it isn't a な-adjective either, so it never takes な. You place it directly in front of whatever you're describing, and it just sits there doing its job — no conjugation to worry about, ever.

In front of an i-adjective, it works exactly the way you'd guess:

```jp
彼はかなり足が速い。
```
*He's quite a fast runner.*

In front of a な-adjective, かなり attaches straight onto the adjective's base — the だ or です that ends the sentence stays exactly where it always is, untouched by かなり being there:

```jp
彼女はかなり親切だ。
```
*She's quite kind.*

In front of a verb, かなり describes the extent of the action itself — how much happened, not how well it happened. That distinction matters: かなり食べた tells you about quantity, not skill.

```jp
彼はご飯をかなり食べた。
```
*He ate quite a lot of rice.*

```jp
彼女はかなりお金を持っている。
```
*She has quite a lot of money.*

It also sits comfortably inside a reason clause, describing the adjective that's driving the whole sentence rather than the main verb:

```jp
彼はかなり忙しいから、あまり寝ない。
```
*He's quite busy, so he doesn't sleep much.*

One placement habit worth locking in early: かなり always stays glued to the word it modifies. English "quite" sometimes drifts toward the front of a sentence ("quite a lot happened today"), but かなり doesn't wander — it lives right before the adjective or verb it's attached to, every single time, no matter how long or short the rest of the sentence is.

It also helps to know where かなり sits on the scale of degree words you already have. ちょっと難しい undersells the situation — barely worth mentioning, something you'd say to brush a worry aside. とても難しい oversells it — edging toward dramatic, the kind of thing you'd say if you wanted sympathy. かなり難しい lands in the middle: solidly, unmistakably hard, and whoever's listening takes you at your word without assuming you're exaggerating in either direction. That's why it's the one native speakers reach for constantly in ordinary conversation — it's honest without being flat.

## Rules and exceptions

> かなり never takes な, even though it's sitting in the exact slot a な-adjective usually occupies. `かなりな人` isn't a real phrase, and if you say it, people will just be confused about what you meant. If you want to describe a person directly with this word, you still need an adjective to attach it to — かなり優しい人, "a quite kind person" — never かなり parked alone in front of a noun.

This trips people up specifically because かなり *looks* like it should behave the way 有名な人 ("a famous person") or 親切な人 ("a kind person") behave — an adjective plus な plus a noun. But かなり isn't an adjective at all; it's an adverb standing in front of one, and adverbs in Japanese never take な. The confusion is understandable, but the fix is simple once you see it: always check whether there's an actual adjective sitting between かなり and the noun.

かなり also reports a real, fairly high amount without passing judgment on it. Said about a test score or someone's cooking, it can even land as a mild compliment — 彼女はかなり料理が上手だ ("she's quite good at cooking") is a compliment, not a complaint. That's the part that trips people up once they've also learned すぎる: if what you actually mean is that the amount crossed a line into being a problem, かなり is the wrong tool for the job, because it simply doesn't carry that judgment.

かなり doesn't shift register either. It shows up equally in casual chat between friends and in careful です/ます speech without changing shape, which is unusual for a word this common — most everyday intensifiers pick a lane and stay in it. かなり just works everywhere, which is part of why it's worth internalizing early: you will never have to think twice about whether it fits the situation.

It also doesn't stack naturally with other degree words the way you might expect from English, where you can pile on "quite really very" for emphasis. Saying `とてもかなり難しい` to mean "extremely quite difficult" just sounds like you couldn't decide which word you wanted. Pick one degree word per adjective and let it do the work; かなり on its own is already doing plenty.

## Contrast: かなり vs すぎる

You've already met すぎる for "too much." The two words describe similar territory — both signal a noticeably large amount — but they disagree about judgment: かなり just states the size of something, while すぎる always flags that the size became a problem worth mentioning.

```jp
この問題は難しすぎる。
```
*This problem is too difficult.*

Swap in かなり and the complaint disappears. What's left is a plain, almost admiring description of how hard the problem is — no implication that something is wrong with the test, the teacher, or the situation:

```jp
この問題はかなり難しい。
```
*This problem is quite difficult.*

Same adjective, roughly the same amount of difficulty — but one sentence is a complaint and the other is just an observation. When you're deciding which one to reach for, ask yourself whether you're reporting a fact or lodging one. If a friend asks how your day went and you genuinely just want to describe it, かなり keeps things neutral; save すぎる for when you actually want the listener to hear that something went too far.

Hold onto that habit of measuring amounts without judging them. It comes back once くらい enters the picture, where "to what extent" starts getting measured against something else entirely — a person, a number, another situation — instead of just being stated on its own the way かなり states it here. For now, though, かなり alone will carry you through most of the honest, unremarkable reporting that ordinary conversation is actually made of.
