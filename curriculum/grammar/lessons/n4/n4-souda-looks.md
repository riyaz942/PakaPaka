You are at the window of a shop near the station, looking at a tray of bread that came out of the oven ten minutes ago. You want to say something about it, and you reach for このパンはおいしいです — which is a small lie. おいしい is a verdict from your tongue, and your tongue has been nowhere near that tray. What you actually have is your eyes.

```jp
このパンはおいしそうですね。
```

*That bread looks delicious, doesn't it.*

That is the whole job of this そう. It takes a word about how something **is** and turns it into a word about how something **strikes you right now, from what is in front of you**. It is a guess made on the spot, from visible evidence, and Japanese would rather you marked it as a guess than smuggled it in as fact.

You will need it constantly. Luggage that looks heavy, a friend who looks tired, a sky that is obviously about to do something. All of that runs through そう.

## How it works

One move: **cut the word back to its stem, then put そう on the end.** The stem is the word with its ending taken off — the part that carries the meaning, before the grammar starts.

- い-adjectives drop their い.
- な-adjectives are already stems, so nothing goes between them and そう.
- Verbs go to the ます-stem, the shape you are left holding when you pull ます off the polite form.

```jp
高い → 高そうです
元気 → 元気そうです
降る → 降りそうです
```

*expensive → looks expensive*

*well → looks well*

*to fall (of rain) → looks about to fall*

Why a stem and not a whole form? Because そう is not standing outside a finished sentence and commenting on it. It fuses onto the word and builds a **new adjective** out of it. 高そう is a single word meaning "expensive-looking", exactly the way 高い is a single word meaning "expensive". Hold onto that idea — half of this lesson falls out of it.

な-adjectives are where learners over-engineer things. There is no な, no だ, nothing at all in the gap. 元気 walks straight into そう.

```jp
兄は今日とても元気そうです。
```

*My older brother looks really well today.*

He has not told you how he feels. You are working from the fact that he is up early and talking. `元気なそうです` is the slip almost everyone makes once, borrowed out of ので habits — but there is nothing to borrow here, because そう attaches to the bare word and to nothing else.

Verbs shift the meaning slightly, and this part catches people. With an adjective, そう tells you how something appears. With a verb, it tells you the action is **on the verge of happening** — you are watching the run-up to it, not the thing itself. 降りそう does not mean "rain is a thing that occasionally happens here"; it means "that rain is coming, any minute now". A cat inching toward the edge of a desk is 落ちそう. A train sitting three minutes too long at the platform is 遅れそう.

```jp
雨が降りそうなので、傘を持ちます。
```

*It looks like it's going to rain, so I'll take an umbrella.*

Notice the そうな in there. That is the second half of the pattern, and you get it for free: **そう behaves exactly like a な-adjective**. Once you have built 高そう or 降りそう, you can do to it everything you already do to 元気 or 静か. Before a noun it takes な. Before a verb it takes に. In a polite sentence it takes です, and in the past, でした.

```jp
おいしそうなパンを三つ買いました。
```

*I bought three delicious-looking rolls.*

```jp
子供は楽しそうに歌います。
```

*The child sings as though she's having a wonderful time.*

That そうに is the workhorse for describing **how** somebody does something: 楽しそうに, 悲しそうに, 忙しそうに. You are not claiming to know what is going on inside another person — you are reporting the shape of it from the outside, which is precisely what そう is for.

Tense goes on そう and nowhere else, because そう is the adjective now: 高そうでした, "it looked expensive". The word in front never moves. 高い stays 高, whenever the guess was made.

Negatives take a little care, and they split in two. To say something does not look a certain way, you make そう itself negative: 高そうじゃないです. To say an action does not look about to happen, the ない fuses in front instead, growing a さ on the way: 電車は遅れなさそうです.

## Rules and exceptions

Two words are irregular, and both are common enough that you will meet them this week. いい and ない are too short to survive the chop, so they grow a さ first.

> いい becomes よさそう and ない becomes なさそう. `いそう` and `なそう` are wrong. And yes, いい switches to its 良い root on the way, exactly as it does everywhere else it conjugates.

Then the trap that every learner walks into once.

> `かわいそう` is a real word, and it does **not** mean "cute-looking". It means "poor thing" — pity, not charm. To say a cat looks cute, say かわいい猫ですね and keep そう out of it entirely.

Nouns are simply out of scope. そう builds adjectives out of adjectives and verbs, and a noun has no ending to chop. `学生そうです` is not a sentence in any register. If you want to guess someone's job from their bag, this pattern cannot carry you there — you will need a different tool, and you will get one later.

The last rule is honesty running the other way. そう is for a judgement built from partial evidence. When the fact is just plainly there in front of you, do not hedge it. A room you are standing in the middle of is 静かです, not `静かそうです`, and something plainly pretty is きれいです, not `きれいそうです`. きれい and かわいい are already verdicts delivered by the eyes, so stacking そう on top of them either sounds strange or, as you just saw, means something else altogether.

## Casual speech

In casual talk the です or だ falls straight off and そう ends the sentence by itself: 高そう, おいしそう, 安そう. This is one of the most common things you will hear friends say to each other over a menu. Add a final particle and you have the rest of it — 雨が降りそうだね, おいしそうだよ. The negative shortens the same way: 高くなさそう.

Because そう is doing the guessing for you, this ending is safe with strangers as well as with friends. おいしそうですね said to the person who cooked is a compliment, not a hedge — you are telling them their food is winning before you have even started. Nothing needs softening; you only swap です in or out.

## Contrast: the そうだ you were told against the そうだ you can see

You have met そうだ once already, for hearsay, and from the outside the two are identical. They are unrelated patterns, they never mix, and the thing that tells them apart sits **in front** of そう, never behind it.

Hearsay takes a **complete plain form** — something that could stand alone as a sentence. Appearance takes a **bare stem** — a word with its ending cut off. So the minimal pair splits on exactly one い.

```jp
来週の試験は難しいそうです。
```

*I hear next week's exam is hard.*

```jp
来週の試験は難しそうです。
```

*Next week's exam looks hard.*

The first sentence is a message from someone who sat that exam last year, and you have no opinion of your own in it. The second is you, holding the study sheet, doing the arithmetic on how much of it you actually know. Same exam, completely different source, one syllable of difference.

な-adjectives split even more visibly, and this is where all three patterns you now know pull apart. ので and のに take な: 静かなので, 静かなのに. Hearsay そうだ takes だ: 静かだそうです. Appearance そう takes nothing whatsoever: 静かそうです. Three endings, three joints, one stem, and the syllable sitting in the gap tells you which one you are reading. This trips up nearly everyone for a fortnight, and then one day it stops.

There is a deeper difference under the syllable. Hearsay そうだ is a lid: it shuts the sentence and never conjugates. Appearance そう is a word: it takes な, it takes に, it goes past, it goes negative, it happily lives inside a longer sentence. That is why 雨が降りそうなので works and why the hearsay one could never be bent into that shape.

So read backwards, not forwards. A full form in front means somebody told you. A bare stem in front means you are the one looking.

Later, when you meet たら, this ending becomes one of the most useful things you own — "if it looks like rain, ..." is a sentence people say most weeks of their lives, and you are one small step away from it.
