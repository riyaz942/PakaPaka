You're at your desk and notice your coworker's seat is empty, jacket gone from the hook. Someone mentions it over coffee, not with any real certainty — just passing along what they picked up secondhand. That's the exact gap らしい fills: a way to state something without claiming you personally confirmed it.

```jp
彼は今日休むらしい。
```

*I heard he's taking today off.*

You didn't check his calendar and you didn't call him. Someone told you, or you noticed something and pieced it together, and now you're reporting the conclusion rather than swearing to it. らしい carries that distance built right into the sentence — it's the difference between "he's off today" and "word is, he's off today."

This comes up constantly once you notice it. Weather, gossip, news you skimmed rather than read closely, a friend's plans you only heard about thirdhand — all of it wants らしい, because in all of it, you are not the source. English does the same job with "apparently," "supposedly," or "word is" — a small tag that quietly tells the listener where the information came from, without you having to spell it out in a whole extra sentence.

That's really the whole job of らしい: it lets one word carry a disclaimer that would otherwise take a full clause to say out loud ("I'm not totally sure, but from what I gather..."). Once you have it, you'll reach for it constantly, because most of what you know about other people's lives is secondhand anyway.

## How it works

らしい attaches after the **plain form** — the dictionary-style version of a word, the one you'd use with a close friend, not the polite -です/-ます version. With verbs, the plain dictionary form or the plain past slots straight in, no extra changes:

```jp
友達は先週旅行に行ったらしい。
```

*It seems my friend went on a trip last week.*

行った already carries its own past tense on its back; らしい just tacks onto the end and adds "this is what I picked up, not what I watched happen."

い-adjectives work the same way — the plain adjective goes in exactly as it is:

```jp
あの店の料理はおいしいらしい。
```

*That restaurant's food is apparently delicious.*

You haven't eaten there. Somebody who has told you, or you saw the line out the door and drew your own conclusion — either way, おいしい goes in untouched and らしい does the reporting.

な-adjectives and nouns are where people trip up, because these are exactly the words that normally lean on だ to finish a plain sentence. In front of らしい, that だ **disappears**:

```jp
新しいレストランは有名らしい。
```

*The new restaurant is apparently famous.*

```jp
彼は医者らしい。
```

*He's apparently a doctor.*

有名だ becomes 有名らしい, not `有名だらしい`. 医者だ becomes 医者らしい, not `医者だらしい`. If your instinct is to slot だ in front of every だ-type word out of habit, this is the one place that habit has to switch off — らしい already behaves like an い-adjective itself, and い-adjectives never sit next to だ.

Negation works exactly the same way: whatever the plain negative form looks like — 忙しくない, 休まない — らしい just attaches after it with no extra steps. Nothing about らしい itself changes; only the word underneath it does.

Put those pieces together and the rule is genuinely one rule, not four separate ones to memorize: find the plain form of whatever you're saying — verb, い-adjective, な-adjective, or noun — and drop らしい on the end. The only word class that needs a small adjustment is だ-type words, because だ itself has no place sitting in front of らしい.

## Rules and exceptions

> だ never survives in front of らしい, full stop. 静かだ → 静からしい, 学生だ → 学生らしい. Say `静かだらしい` out loud and it will sound exactly as wrong to a native speaker as it looks on the page.

らしい can stack です on the end for extra politeness, and this is completely normal: 忙しいらしいです softens the claim while keeping the sentence polite. But the order only runs one way — らしい comes first, です after. `忙しいですらしい` is not something anyone says, in any register.

In casual speech among friends, らしい often gets a よ or a ね tacked onto the very end — 彼は今日休むらしいよ, 彼は今日休むらしいね — the way you'd add "you know" or "right?" in English. Neither particle changes what らしい itself is doing; they just color how the news lands, more of a nudge than a fact dump.

> There's a second, older job for this exact same word, and it's worth knowing before it confuses you. Noun + らしい can mean "typical of," not "seems to be." 子供らしい doesn't report a rumor about someone being a child — it means "childlike," acting the way a child would be expected to. 医者らしい, said with nothing else around it, could mean "seems to be a doctor" or "acts like a proper doctor should." Whatever came right before it in the conversation is usually what tells you which one you're hearing.

Because らしい signals distance from the source, using it about something you witnessed with your own eyes tends to sound off — like you're hedging on a fact you're actually sure of. Save it for what you genuinely picked up secondhand, read somewhere, or pieced together from a clue, not for what you personally confirmed yourself.

That distinction matters more in Japanese than it might feel like it should in English, where "apparently" gets used loosely all the time, even about things we saw ourselves. A Japanese listener hearing らしい will assume, correctly, that you're not the source — so if you did see it happen, drop らしい entirely and just state the plain sentence. The absence of らしい is itself information.

## Contrast — らしい vs はず

You already have はず for "should be" — a conclusion you reasoned out yourself from something you already know. らしい covers the opposite direction: a conclusion that came from outside you, something you heard or noticed rather than worked out on your own.

```jp
空が暗いから、雨が降るはずだ。
```

*The sky is dark, so it should rain.*

```jp
友達が言ったから、今日の午後は雨が降るらしい。
```

*My friend told me, so it seems it'll rain this afternoon.*

Same weather, same conclusion, two completely different reasons for believing it. はず says "I looked at the evidence in front of me and worked this out myself." らしい says "someone else handed me this conclusion, and I'm passing it along." Mix the two up and you end up either claiming ownership of a guess that was never yours, or disowning one you actually reasoned through — both read strangely to a native listener, even if the sentence is grammatical.

らしい and はず don't stack together on the same claim; pick whichever one matches where the information actually came from, and let that choice do the talking.

A useful test when you're not sure which one to reach for: ask yourself whether you could explain *why* you believe the thing, using only your own knowledge, with no outside source involved. If yes, that's はず territory. If the honest answer is "someone told me" or "I noticed something and guessed," that's らしい, every time. The two patterns aren't really competing for the same sentences — they're sorting your beliefs by where they came from, and once you start asking that question automatically, the choice mostly makes itself.

Hold onto that distinction — a close cousin, ようだ, shows up soon, and it leans on your own read of a scene in front of you rather than something you were told about it.
