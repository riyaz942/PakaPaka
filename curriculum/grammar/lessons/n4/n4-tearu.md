You get home, and the bicycle that was leaning against the fence this morning is standing upright against the wall, chained in place. You didn't do that. Your roommate did, earlier, and now the state of it — parked, secured — is just sitting there as a fact about the world. That's the feeling てある captures: not someone doing something, but something someone did, on display.

## How it works

てある attaches to the te-form of a **transitive** verb (one that takes a direct object — the person doing the action acts *on* something) and adds ある, the verb for existence of things. Together they describe a state that exists *because* someone deliberately set it up that way.

```jp
窓が開けてある。
```

*The window has been opened (and left that way).*

Notice the marker: it's が, not を, even though 開ける is a transitive verb and 窓 is what got opened. Once てある turns the sentence into a description of a state, が marks what that state belongs to — the same shift you'd expect from any sentence about how something *is*.

```jp
名前が書いてある。
```

*A name is written (there).*

```jp
机の上に手紙が置いてある。
```

*There's a letter placed on the desk.*

The verb conjugates exactly like it does before いる in ている — drop る, add て — then ある just tacks on.

```jp
止める → 止めてある
```

## Rules and exceptions

The whole pattern depends on the verb being transitive. 止める (to stop something — a car, a machine) works because there's an agent doing the stopping. Its intransitive twin, 止まる (to stop, on its own), can't take てある at all — there's no one whose deliberate setup you'd be describing.

> てある almost always implies a person set the state up on purpose. If nothing planted an intentional state, てある sounds wrong to a native ear — plain ている is what you want for something that just happened on its own.

```jp
自転車が止めてある。
```

*The bicycle has been parked.*

Because the emphasis is on the *result being ready for someone*, てある often shows up when the state matters to the speaker right now — a door left open for a guest, a seat saved, food set out. Simply stating that a transitive action happened isn't enough; you need the outcome to still be sitting there, relevant.

## Casual speech

In casual explanations, てある often picks up んだ instead of the more formal んです — you're not changing the pattern itself, just how you frame it as an aside. 電気がつけてあるんだ ("the light's on, see" — someone left it on) reads far more natural in conversation than the plain sentence alone.

```jp
電気がつけてある。
```

*The light has been left on.*

## Contrast

You already have ている doing two jobs: progressive ("in the middle of") and resultant state for **intransitive** verbs. てある is the transitive half of that second job — same idea, "something is in a state," but now there's a person behind it.

```jp
自転車が止まっている。
```

*The bicycle is stopped (it's just sitting there, stopped).*

Line that up against 自転車が止めてある above: both describe a parked bike, but 止まっている only tells you the bike isn't moving — it says nothing about who parked it or why. 止めてある insists someone put it there on purpose, and that fact is still true.

```jp
ドアが閉めてある。
```

*The door has been shut.*

Swap in ドアが閉まっている and you'd only be reporting that the door is closed right now, no hint of intent behind it — useful if you don't know or don't care who closed it.

That distinction — a bare fact about how something is, versus a fact plus someone's fingerprints on it — is what you're listening for every time you meet these two side by side. Hold onto てある; you'll want it again soon when you learn ておく, which is the *moment before* this state gets set up on purpose.
