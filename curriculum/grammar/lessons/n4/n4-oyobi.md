You are at a counter filling out a form, and the clerk taps two empty boxes: one for your name, one for your address. In conversation you would say 名前と住所 and be done. But the printed label on the sheet says 名前および住所. Same meaning — "name and address" — just wearing a suit.

および is the word formal written Japanese uses to join two nouns into a pair. Think of it as the と of contracts, public notices, instruction sheets, and announcements: the register where every word is chosen with care.

```jp
名前および住所を、ここに書いてください。
```

*Please write your name and address here.*

Drop that line onto a government form and nobody would blink.

## How it works

The shape is exactly what it looks like: **Noun + および + Noun**. A noun on the left, a noun on the right, and および welds them into a single unit that the rest of the sentence then handles as one thing.

The conjugation information you were handed is only `{"formal_conjunction": true}`. That is the system's way of saying there is nothing to conjugate. および has one form, および, forever — no past, no negative, no politeness levels. The formality is baked in and never moves.

One consequence of "the pair acts as one unit" is worth slowing down for. The particle that says what the pair is doing in the sentence — は for the topic, が for the subject, を for the object, に for the target — lands **after the second noun**, never between the two. The nouns join first; the particle comes second.

```jp
会議の場所および時間は、まだ決まっていません。
```

*The meeting's location and time have not been decided yet.*

場所 and 時間 clip together, and then a single は makes the whole pair the topic. Writing `場所は および 時間は` would be like saying "the location is and the time is" — the particle has been let loose too early.

For a longer formal list, および usually links just the final two items while 、 separates the ones before it — the same slot English keeps for its last "and".

```jp
試験の結果および説明は、金曜日に伝えます。
```

*The exam results and the explanation will be given on Friday.*

The same structure carries notices that address people rather than things:

```jp
学生および先生は、この入口から入ってください。
```

*Students and teachers, please enter through this entrance.*

```jp
書類および荷物は、受付に出してください。
```

*Please hand in your documents and luggage at the reception desk.*

Read those two aloud and notice how official they sound. That tone is not an accident — it is the whole reason および exists as a separate word from と.

## Rules and exceptions

**および joins nouns, full stop.** It will not connect two verbs, two adjectives, or two complete sentences. Formal Japanese links actions and clauses with other tools — the て-form, し, and more you have yet to meet — but never with および. Trying to bolt it onto a verb is the mistake almost everyone makes first.

> `食べるおよび飲む` for "eat and drink" is not a thing — および cannot touch a verb. You join the nouns instead: 食べ物および飲み物.

**This is a register choice, not a grammar rule.** および is not *incorrect* in speech, but out loud it sounds like you are reading from a printed manual. When you are actually talking, と joins a closed pair ("exactly these two") and や joins an open-ended set ("these among others"). および steps in only when the sentence is written and wants to be taken seriously.

**Personal relationships sit awkwardly with it.** Because および is so stiff, using it for your own family can sound like a legal filing:

```jp
父および母も、この意見に反対です。
```

*My father and my mother are both against this idea, too.*

That sentence is fine in a written report or a formal statement. Said to a friend over lunch, 父と母 is what you would use — および there would make your parents sound like line items on a form.

## のみ versus および

You have already met のみ, the formal word for "only". のみ and および feel like siblings: both are bookish, both belong to the same careful written register, both would look at home on the same official page. But they move in opposite directions. および **adds** a second item to what you are talking about; のみ **strips everything away** except one.

```jp
学生および先生が来ます。
```

*Students and teachers are coming.*

```jp
学生のみ来ます。
```

*Only students are coming.*

One word changed, and the room either doubles or empties out. When you are drafting something formal and reach for one of these, say the sentence back to yourself and check which you meant: "these two groups together" points to one word, "this group and no one else" to the other.

## Closing

および is how a form or a contract says two nouns belong together: **Noun + および + Noun**, no conjugation ever, and the marking particle waits until after the second noun. In speech, と does the same job without the suit.

Coming up is に対して, another formal piece that attaches straight to a noun — but rather than tying two nouns together as equals, it stands one thing opposite another. Keep this "careful written register" feeling nearby; に対して lives in the same neighbourhood.
