You're back in your hometown after a year away, and your friend meets you at the station. 「大きくなったね」isn't the sentence you reach for — what you actually notice is *change still happening*, something that was one way and is sliding toward another. Japanese has a pair of endings built exactly for that: **ていく** and **てくる**. They turn any verb into a story about direction — away from now, or toward now.

```jp
天気がだんだん暖かくなってくる。
```

*The weather is gradually getting warmer (and it's happening right now, moving toward us).*

## How it works

Both endings attach to the **te-form** of a verb, then add 行く (いく, to go) or 来る (くる, to come). That's the whole structure: te-form + いく, or te-form + くる. The "go / come" meaning is mostly bleached out — what's left is a sense of *direction*, either away from the present moment (**ていく**) or toward it (**てくる**).

Think of it on a timeline with "now" as a fixed point. **てくる** describes something arriving at now, coming closer, building up to this point. **ていく** describes something leaving now, heading off, continuing on from here.

```jp
子供はだんだん大きくなっていく。
```

*The child keeps growing bigger and bigger (moving forward from now).*

```jp
彼は毎晩遅く帰ってくる。
```

*He comes home late every night (repeatedly arriving back, toward here).*

This works with plain motion verbs too, and there the "go/come" sense is much more literal — it just adds the direction relative to the speaker.

```jp
友達がこちらへ歩いてくる。
```

*My friend is walking this way (toward me).*

```jp
私は駅まで歩いていく。
```

*I'll walk to the station (away from here).*

The same logic covers actions that aren't physical movement at all — a habit, a change, a process that keeps going. 仕事を続ける (to continue work) plus ていく says the continuing is aimed at the future.

```jp
彼女は仕事を続けていく。
```

*She's going to keep going with her work (carrying it forward from here).*

## Rules and exceptions

Both endings need the verb's te-form first — there's no shortcut version that skips it. `大きいてくる` is not a real word; you need `大きくなってくる`, because it's なる (to become) that's carrying the ていく/てくる, not the adjective itself.

> 来る itself can take てくる: 来てくる. It looks redundant — "come and come" — but it's genuinely rare and > mostly shows up with other movement verbs stacked on top, so don't reach for it as your default てくる example.

The direction reading (away vs. toward) is the default with change-of-state verbs like なる, but with plain motion verbs like 歩く or 走る, ていく/てくる simply mark which way the person is heading relative to whoever's talking. Context tells you which flavor you're getting — there's no separate grammar for it.

```jp
犬が公園から走ってくる。
```

*The dog comes running out of the park (heading toward the speaker).*

## Casual speech

In relaxed conversation, ていく often shrinks to てく — the い drops out, the same way ている shrinks to てる. てくる doesn't contract the same way; it usually stays whole even in casual speech.

- Neutral: 私は駅まで歩いていく。
- Casual: 私は駅まで歩いてく。

## Contrast

You already know ている for an action in progress or a state that's settled in — it freezes a moment. ていく/てくる instead point at *movement between* moments: where something is coming from, or where it's headed. 大きくなっている says a child is currently in a bigger state; 大きくなっていく says the growing is still going to keep happening from here on.

You also know てしまう for finishing something completely, often with a note of regret. てしまう closes a door; ていく/てくる describe someone walking through one, mid-step, either arriving or leaving.

Hold onto that "toward now / away from now" picture — it comes back once you meet ても and てはいけない, where the same te-form is doing a completely different job, but the habit of reading a te-form ending as a small grammatical hinge will already feel familiar.
