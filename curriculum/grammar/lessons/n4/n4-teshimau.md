You're describing your day to a friend, but every sentence carries a little sigh — you forgot the homework, you ate all the snacks you were supposed to save, you lost your wallet. In English you'd just say "I forgot" or "I ate it all," but Japanese has a whole grammar pattern built for that sigh: てしまう.

```jp
宿題を忘れてしまう。
```
*I end up forgetting the homework.*

That しまう on the end isn't decoration — it's doing real work. It's telling the listener: this happened in full, and the speaker wishes it hadn't.

## How it works

てしまう attaches to the te-form of any verb, exactly like ている or ておく do. Take the verb, drop it into its te-form, and add しまう.

```jp
食べる → 食べてしまう
```
*to eat → to eat up / end up eating*

Once you've got te-form + しまう, that whole chunk conjugates like any regular verb: past, non-past, and so on, just like a normal verb.

```jp
お菓子を全部食べてしまう。
```
*I end up eating all the snacks.*

Notice what しまう adds that 食べた alone wouldn't: it wasn't supposed to happen, or it happened faster or more thoroughly than intended. 食べた is neutral. 食べてしまう has a story behind it.

```jp
財布を無くしてしまう。
```
*I end up losing my wallet.*

## Rules and exceptions

てしまう actually carries two related meanings, and context decides which one you're hearing:

1. **An unwanted result** — something went wrong, and you (or someone) is stuck with it. That's the wallet, the snacks, the homework you forgot.
2. **Emphatic completion** — the action ran all the way through, with no bad feeling at all, just emphasis that nothing was left undone.

```jp
宿題を全部してしまう。
```
*I get all the homework done.*

Same grammar, opposite mood — here it just means "done, every last bit of it," maybe even with relief. Which reading you get depends entirely on the situation and the speaker's tone, not on the grammar itself.

> てしまう attaches only to verbs — you can't put it on an adjective or a noun directly. `暑いてしまう` is not a sentence; there's no equivalent shortcut for adjectives. Keep it to the te-form of verbs.

A common beginner habit is reaching for てしまう every time something is simply "done," the way learners overuse plain past tense on its own. Save it for moments where thoroughness or an unwanted outcome is actually the point — otherwise plain past tense does the job.

## Casual speech

In fast, casual speech, てしまう almost always contracts. てしまう becomes ちゃう, and でしまう (after ん-stem verbs like 遊ぶ) becomes じゃう.

```jp
忘れちゃう。
```
*I totally forget.*

```jp
遊んじゃう。
```
*I go and play (when I shouldn't).*

You'll hear ちゃう constantly between friends and almost never in polite writing — treat it like any other casual contraction: fine to recognize everywhere, risky to use with your teacher.

## Contrast

You've already met ておく — te-form + おく — for doing something *in advance*, so it's ready later. てしまう points the opposite direction: it looks *back* at something already fully done, often with a reaction attached.

```jp
明日の準備をしておきました。
```
*I got tomorrow's preparations done in advance.*

```jp
明日の準備をしてしまう。
```
*I go and do tomorrow's preparations (before I meant to).*

Swap おく for しまう and the meaning shifts from "I set this up ahead of time, on purpose" to a completed action with a reaction attached — the forward-planning feel disappears entirely.

ている, by contrast, marks something still in progress or a state that has settled in. てしまう marks a point where the action stops because there's nothing left to do.

Homework you've already turned in and can't get back, snacks you can't unsnack — that's the shape of てしまう. Hold onto that finality; you'll want the opposite feeling again once ていく and てくる show up, since those describe change that's still underway.
