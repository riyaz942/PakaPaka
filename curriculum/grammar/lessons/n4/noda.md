Your study partner didn't show up yesterday. No text, no warning, just an empty seat where they usually sit. Today they slide in next to you looking a little sheepish, and before "hey" is even out of your mouth you're already asking:

```jp
昨日、どうしたんですか。
```

*What happened yesterday?*

Notice this isn't a cold, out-of-nowhere question — the empty seat already told you something was off, and your question is built to lean on that. んですか is the polite question form of the pattern you're learning today: んだ in its plain, unpolished form, のです when it's dressed up further. Both spellings do the same job — they frame whatever comes before them as *the explanation*, not just a fact floating in space. Ask a bare どうしましたか instead and you sound like you're asking a stranger for directions, no shared context assumed.

English doesn't really force you to choose here — "I was sick" works whether you're announcing it cold or answering a direct question. Japanese does force the choice, because a plain です/だ sentence reads as a freestanding fact, dropped into the room with no strings attached. んです exists specifically to tie a sentence back to something already sitting between you and the listener — a question, a weird situation, an obvious gap that needs filling. It isn't extra politeness and it isn't decoration; it's a signal that says "this is the reason for what we were just talking about."

Your friend answers:

```jp
病気だったんです。
```

*I was sick.*

That んです at the end isn't decoration. It's doing one specific job: framing 病気だった (I was sick) as the answer to the question hanging in the air, not as a random fact your friend decided to volunteer. Drop it — a plain 病気でした — and the sentence stops sounding like an answer and starts sounding like small talk about the weather.

## How it works

The mechanics are almost insultingly simple once you see them: take a sentence in its plain form — the form you'd use talking to yourself, or scribbling in a diary, not the polite form you'd use with your teacher — and tack んです (or the fuller のです) onto the end. Verbs are the simplest case of all: dictionary form and all, nothing shifts, nothing gets dropped.

```jp
友達が来るんです。
```

*A friend is coming over.*

That's what you'd say if someone found you scrubbing the kitchen floor at nine at night and wanted to know why. 来る never bends to fit んです — んです just rides on top of whatever the plain form of the verb already was, past, present, or negative alike.

い-adjectives work exactly the same way.

```jp
忙しいんです。
```

*I'm busy.*

忙しい just sits there exactly as it always does, and んです rides on top of it. Compare that to the noun and な-adjective case, which is the one spot in this whole pattern that actually trips people up, because their plain form doesn't naturally end in something んです can attach to.

```jp
元気 → 元気なんです
```

*healthy/fine → It's that I'm doing fine*

元気 on its own isn't a full sentence — you'd normally need だ or です to finish it off, and it's that だ that vanishes and gets replaced with な the moment んです shows up. Say `元気んです` and it just sounds broken, like a word with a piece missing, because there's no plain form ending in ん for んです to land on. You'll meet this same な wedge again outside of んです entirely, wherever a plain-form pattern attaches to a noun or な-adjective — this is just the first time it's forcing you to notice it.

```jp
今日は元気なんです。
```

*I'm doing fine today.*

Picture someone checking in on you after yesterday, worried you might still be feeling rough, and this is your reply. 元気 alone would read as a flat status update; 元気なんです answers the actual worry sitting behind their question.

## Rules and exceptions

Past tense skips the な problem entirely, because だった (the plain past of だ) already ends in a shape んです can attach to directly — exactly what you saw with 病気だったんです a moment ago. The な wedge only shows up in the plain present affirmative, the one tense where だ itself would otherwise disappear without a trace.

> Writing `学生んです` for "I'm a student" is one of the most common slips at this stage. The correct form is 学生なんです — the な isn't optional decoration, it's standing in for the だ that would be there if んです weren't attaching to it.

んです also isn't something you sprinkle onto every sentence you happen to say. Announcing something like 今日は水曜日なんです out of absolutely nowhere, with nobody asking and nothing unusual going on, comes across as if you're bracing the listener for bad news that never shows up. Save it for the moments where something genuinely needs explaining, or where somebody just asked.

> Don't stack it on top of です the way you might guess from the spelling. です already is the polite copula doing its own job — んです replaces the plain だ underneath it, it doesn't sit next to it. `だんです` isn't a form anyone uses.

The question version carries its own particular flavor, too. んですか reads as "something's going on here — tell me about it," which is exactly why it fits your friend showing up sheepish after a missed day, but would feel oddly probing coming from someone who has no reason to suspect anything's up in the first place.

## Casual speech

Among friends, んです contracts down to んだ — same job, same framing, just with the politeness layer stripped off.

```jp
忙しいんです → 忙しいんだ
```

*I'm busy (polite) → I'm busy (casual, to a friend)*

```jp
今日は忙しいんだ。
```

*I'm busy today.*

That's the line you'd actually text a friend who's asking why you're bailing on plans — んです there would sound stiff, almost like you're reading from a script. Formal writing swings the opposite direction: instead of contracting anything, it usually keeps the fuller のです, which reads as more deliberate and considered than everyday んです ever does.

```jp
これは大変な問題なのです。
```

*This is a serious problem.*

You'll run into なのです like this in notices, articles, anything meant to sound like it was composed rather than said out loud on the spot. Same mechanics as everything above — just a more formal register wrapped around them.

## Contrast

You've already met ため for stating a formal cause — dry, explicit, "due to X." んです is doing something different: it isn't naming a cause so much as framing an entire fact as the explanation the listener needed. Put near-identical sentences side by side and the difference in tone becomes obvious.

```jp
病気のため、休みました。
```

*Due to illness, I took the day off.*

That's the sentence you'd write on a form or say to your boss — factual, a touch formal, cause stated up front and then dropped. Compare it to 病気だったんです from the hook: same illness, same missed day, but one reads like a report and the other reads like you're actually talking to a friend who wants to know what happened to you.

っけ, which you've met for jogging a fuzzy memory, isn't really competing with んです at all — it's reaching for something you might have forgotten, not framing something as an explanation for someone else. The two rarely show up in the same sentence, because they're answering different questions. っけ asks "wait, what was that again?" んです answers "here's what's actually going on."

It helps to keep both of those next to んです whenever a sentence feels like it needs *something* extra at the end — ため tells you the cause is the point, っけ tells you memory is the point, and んです tells you the whole fact is standing in as the answer to something already in the air. Mixing them up doesn't produce broken Japanese so much as Japanese that answers the wrong question.

## Closing

んです turns a plain statement into the answer to a question that didn't necessarily need to be spoken aloud — that's the entire trick, once the な stops being scary. It's also one of those pieces of grammar that native speakers use constantly without ever thinking about it, which is exactly why it's worth this much attention now: you're building an instinct they never had to build consciously. Hold onto that framing instinct; you'll want it again soon, when でしょう starts letting you soften a guess the same way んです frames a fact.
