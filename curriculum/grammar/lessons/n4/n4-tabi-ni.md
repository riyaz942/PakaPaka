Your little sister visits for an hour, and by the time she leaves, the cat has vanished under the bed again. Not because of today's visit specifically — this happens every single time she comes over. You want a sentence that captures the whole pattern, not just today's incident, and Japanese has a tidy tool built exactly for that job.

```jp
妹が来るたびに、猫が逃げる。
```

*Every time my little sister comes over, the cat runs away.*

たびに is doing the heavy lifting in that sentence. It takes what sounds like a one-time news report — she came, the cat ran — and turns it into a standing fact about how these two things relate: every single time A happens, B follows, no exceptions worth mentioning.

## How it works

たびに attaches straight onto the dictionary form (the plain, unconjugated form you would look up in a dictionary) of a verb. No extra conjugation, no special stem, nothing to remember beyond taking the verb as it already is and adding たびに.

```jp
来る → 来るたびに
```

*to come → every time (someone) comes*

That's the entire mechanic for verbs. Whatever the dictionary form already looks like — 買う, 見る, 話す — that's exactly the shape that sits in front of たびに.

Nouns work differently, because a bare noun can't glue onto たびに on its own. You need の in between — the same connecting の that links any two nouns together, the way it does in 私の本, meaning my book. Think of の as the piece that turns a noun into something たびに can attach to.

```jp
旅行のたびに、新しい靴を買う。
```

*Every trip, I buy new shoes.*

The same shape covers events you attend, not just trips you take:

```jp
会議のたびに、彼は遅れる。
```

*Every meeting, he's late.*

If you already know 度 as the counter for counting how many times something happened, たびに is written with that same kanji, just read differently. That's not a coincidence — both readings point at the same idea: counting occasions one by one, and saying something holds true of every single one you'd count.

Notice what the two halves of these sentences are doing. 来るたびに and 旅行のたびに just name the recurring trigger — the thing that keeps happening. The rest of the sentence is whatever reliably tags along whenever that trigger fires. The subject of each half doesn't even have to match: in 会議のたびに、彼は遅れる, the meeting itself doesn't have a particular doer, but 彼 is the one arriving late every time it rolls around.

## Rules and exceptions

The clause right before たびに — the trigger — stays in dictionary form no matter what tense the rest of the sentence lands on. Even when you're describing a pattern that played out entirely in the past, resist pulling that first verb into the past tense along with it.

```jp
彼が来るたびに、猫が逃げた。
```

*Every time he came, the cat ran away.*

> The sentence is unmistakably about the past — 逃げた closes it out — but 来る stays in dictionary form. Writing `来たたびに` feels like it should match, and that instinct is exactly what trips people up; たびに simply never follows a past-tense verb.

The mirror-image mistake shows up with nouns: dropping の because the sentence already feels long enough. `旅行たびに` looks like a shortcut, but it isn't one — a noun always needs の to lead into たびに, the same way it needs の in front of any other noun phrase.

たびに also only earns its keep when something genuinely keeps happening. It's for the pattern you'd notice after the third or fourth time, not a single moment where this happened and then that happened. If you're only reporting one specific occasion, this word is off the table.

One thing you don't need to worry about: たびに doesn't shrink or contract in casual speech the way です or the negative forms do. You'll hear it exactly like this whether you're talking to a friend or writing something formal.

## Contrast: たびに vs 間に

You've already met 間に for while, during the interval — one particular stretch of time, with something happening once inside it. たびに is built for the opposite shape: not one window, but every window, each one counted on its own.

```jp
彼が話している間に、私は宿題をした。
```

*While he was talking, I did my homework.*

```jp
彼が話すたびに、私は笑う。
```

*Every time he talks, I laugh.*

The first sentence is about one occasion: he talked, and sometime during that stretch, the homework got done. Swap in たびに there and you'd suddenly be claiming this happens every time he talks — a far bigger claim than just this once. The second sentence is the opposite: a standing rule about every occasion. Swap in 間に there and the sentence collapses into describing a single conversation, which misses the entire point — the reason it's worth saying is that it happens over and over.

That distinction — one window versus every window — is worth sitting with, because it's easy to reach for whichever pattern you learned first out of habit. Ask yourself whether you're describing something that happened once during a stretch of time, or something that happens reliably, every single time. That question alone tells you which of the two you need.

Hold onto that repeating-pattern feeling. You'll meet it again from a different angle once と comes up for automatic, general results — when X happens, Y always follows, built into the grammar of a single clause rather than named from the outside. たびに says here's a pattern I've noticed; と will let you build that same inevitability directly into one sentence. For now, whenever you catch yourself thinking this always happens when, that's your cue to reach for たびに.
