Your doctor takes one look at your blood sugar numbers and tells you plainly: cut back on sweets. You don't need a magic diet, you need a habit — a small deliberate effort, repeated. That's exactly the shape ようにする was built for.

```jp
毎朝、野菜を食べるようにしている。
```
*I make a point of eating vegetables every morning.*

That sentence isn't reporting what you did once. It's reporting a standing effort — something you've set up as a rule for yourself and keep chasing, even on the days you fall short.

## How it works

ようにする attaches straight onto the dictionary form of a verb for a habit you're aiming for, or the ない form for one you're trying to avoid. Nothing else changes — no te-form, no stem-shaving, just the plain form plus ようにする.

```jp
食べる → 食べるようにする
```
*to eat → to make it a habit to eat*

```jp
食べない → 食べないようにする
```
*to not eat → to make it a habit not to eat*

する here is doing its ordinary job — the word for "to do" — so it conjugates exactly like every other する verb you already know: past ようにした, negative ようにしない, and so on.

The form you'll meet constantly is ようにしている — the て-form of する plus いる, the same "ongoing state" marker you already use for continuous actions. ようにする by itself is closer to a decision you're making right now; ようにしている is the report that the effort has been running for a while.

```jp
甘い物を食べないようにしている。
```
*I try not to eat sweets.*

```jp
毎晩早く寝るようにする。
```
*I'm going to make a habit of going to bed early.*

Notice the difference: the sweets sentence is a lifestyle you've already built (しています), while the early-bedtime one is you announcing the plan today, before the habit exists yet.

## Rules and exceptions

ようにする is for effort toward a repeated behavior, not for a single one-off action. "I made sure to lock the door this one time" isn't this pattern — that's just a plain past-tense sentence. ようにする wants something you're trying to keep doing, day after day, whether or not you always succeed.

> The "try" is doing real work here. ようにする never promises the outcome — it promises the attempt. Someone can say 野菜を食べるようにしている and still skip vegetables some days; the sentence is honest about that built-in slack.

A common wrong guess is treating ようにする like a flat statement of fact, the way you'd use する alone. `野菜を食べるにする` drops the necessary よう and just sounds broken — the よう isn't optional decoration, it's the part that turns "do" into "orient yourself toward doing."

## Contrast: ようにする vs つもり

You already have つもり for stating a plan — 野菜を食べるつもりだ, "I intend to eat vegetables." That's a single decision, and it says nothing about follow-through or repetition; you could mean tonight, once.

```jp
野菜を食べるつもりだ。
```
*I intend to eat vegetables.*

```jp
野菜をもっと食べるようにしている。
```
*I make a point of eating more vegetables.*

Swap the two and the sentence changes shape, not just tone: つもり is a plan you're holding in your head; ようにしている is a habit you can already point to evidence for, because you've been doing the work.

```jp
毎日散歩するようにしている。
```
*I make a point of taking a walk every day.*

## Closing thought

ようにする is the grammar of trying on purpose, day after day, not the grammar of a single decision — hold onto that distinction, because ことにする, when you meet it, looks deceptively similar but marks a one-time choice instead of a standing effort.
