You're standing in a shop, staring at a price tag, trying to decide whether to buy something. You already know how to say 高いです — it's expensive — but what you actually want is a personal rule, something that applies not just today but every time you're in this situation: expensive means no. That's the job of ば. It builds a general-truth "if," the kind that states a standing condition rather than describing one specific moment.

```jp
高ければ、買いません。
```

*If it's expensive, I won't buy it.*

Think of ば as the Japanese version of the "if X, then Y" you'd write in a rulebook or a recipe, not the "if" you'd use to describe one particular afternoon. Nobody's talking about this one item on this one day — the sentence is a policy you carry around with you, ready for the next price tag and the one after that.

It attaches to the end of whatever sets up the condition, and the shape it takes depends on what kind of word that is — a verb, an adjective, or a noun / な-adjective. Four families, four small changes. None of them are hard once you see the pattern underneath, and you already know every verb and adjective involved; this lesson is really just teaching you one new ending to bolt on.

That's also why ば is worth learning properly instead of skimming: it's the ending you reach for constantly once you start noticing how much of daily talk is really a string of small standing rules — what you do when it's late, what you buy when it's cheap, what happens when a friend calls. English handles all of that with one word, "if." Japanese wants you to first decide whether you're stating a rule or narrating an event, and only then pick the right ending.

## How it works

**Group 1 verbs** — the ones ending in う, く, す, つ, ぬ, ぶ, む, or a る that doesn't behave like an い/え-stem verb — shift their final sound from the u-row to the e-row, then add ば. 話す (to speak) becomes 話せば. This is the same row-shift you'll eventually recognize all over the language; here it's just doing conditional duty instead of something else.

```jp
ゆっくり話せば、分かります。
```

*If you speak slowly, I'll understand.*

**Group 2 verbs** — ones like 見る or 食べる whose stem already ends in an い or え sound before る — just drop the る and add れば. The irregular 来る follows the same shape, which is one less thing to memorize separately.

```jp
彼が来れば、嬉しいです。
```

*If he comes, I'll be happy.*

**する**, the other irregular, becomes すれば. Every verb built on する — 勉強する, 練習する, and every other noun-plus-する verb you've picked up — inherits this same change automatically, so you never have to look it up twice.

```jp
毎日練習すれば、上手になります。
```

*If you practice every day, you'll get good at it.*

**い-adjectives** drop the final い and add ければ. The plain negative ない rides along on this same rule, since grammatically it behaves exactly like an い-adjective — it ends in い, so it conjugates like one.

```jp
忙しくなければ、手伝います。
```

*If I'm not busy, I'll help.*

**な-adjectives and nouns** don't take ば directly — there's no verb-like stem for it to grab onto, since these words lean on the copula だ instead of conjugating on their own. Instead, that だ turns into なら, which is casual and by far the most common choice, or into the more formal であれば, which you'll see more in writing and polite speech than in conversation.

```jp
静かなら、勉強できます。
```

*If it's quiet, I can study.*

```jp
学生であれば、安いです。
```

*If you're a student, it's cheap.*

Look back at those examples and you'll notice ば never actually shows up by itself except right after a verb — せば、れば、すれば、ければ、なら、であれば are all the same idea, just wearing whatever shape the word in front of it demands. Once you can spot the base word hiding underneath the ending, none of these are a surprise anymore; you're not learning six patterns, you're learning one pattern with five outfits.

## Rules and exceptions

Here's the edge that catches almost everyone the first time through: when the ば clause describes an action rather than a state, the sentence can't end in a command, an invitation, or a "let's."

> `図書館に行けば、本を借りましょう` sounds broken to a native ear even though every piece is conjugated correctly. Swap the front clause to たら instead, and it becomes completely natural: 図書館に行ったら、本を借りましょう。 ("Once you get to the library, let's borrow a book.")

It helps to have a reason, not just a rule to memorize: ば is stating something like a law of the world — a standing fact about how things work — and a law doesn't turn around and hand out orders in the same breath. たら, by contrast, is just narrating one step and then the next, so a command or invitation fits naturally after it.

That restriction lifts as soon as the front clause is a state instead of an action — an adjective, a negative, or a stative verb like 分かる. That's exactly why 忙しくなければ、手伝います works without a hitch: 忙しい isn't an action, it's a description of how things are, and a description doesn't carry the same weight of "law" that an action does.

There's a second trap worth flagging: ければ only ever attaches to the plain form of an adjective. There's no polite version of the ending itself — you build it off 高い, never off a -です or -ます shape of anything.

A third thing worth knowing: because ば describes a standing rule rather than one moment, it also does the job of a hypothetical — the "if I were, if I had" kind of if, about something that isn't true right now. The mechanics don't change at all; only the situation you're picturing does, and context usually makes clear which one you mean.

## Casual speech

In relaxed speech, ければ often shrinks further, to けりゃ or even きゃ. 高ければ can turn into 高けりゃ, and 忙しければ into 忙しけりゃ. It reads as blunt and a little rough around the edges, the kind of thing you'll hear from an older relative or in a casual drama more than from a coworker — worth recognizing when you hear it, not something you need to produce yet.

## Contrast: ば vs. たら

You already know たら for conditionals — Ta-form + ら. The two overlap enough on the surface to blur together, and honestly, in a lot of everyday sentences either one gets the point across and a native speaker won't blink. But they're answering different questions underneath. ば states a standing rule, something generally or always true; たら describes one specific event, usually something you're picturing as a single occurrence, often already decided by the time you're saying it.

```jp
雨が降れば、家にいます。
```

*If it rains, I stay home.*

```jp
雨が降ったら、家にいます。
```

*If it rains, I'll stay home.*

The English barely shifts, but the Japanese is telling two different stories. The first is a habit — rain, any rain, any day, and you're staying in, full stop, as a rule about yourself. The second is about one forecast, one day: if it rains today, that's what you'll do today, and tomorrow is a separate question. When you're talking about a single, particular event, たら is almost always the safer and more natural pick; save ば for the rule.

ので and のに aren't in this competition at all — they answer "why" and "even though," not "if" — so they don't overlap with ば the way たら does. It's easy to lump every because-shaped or if-shaped word together in your head early on, especially while all of them are still new; this is a good moment to let them separate back out into their own lanes.

You've actually already used なら twice above, quietly doing ば's job for nouns and な-adjectives. That's not the whole of what なら can do, though. On its own as a grammar point, なら can react to something someone just said, or raise a topic before you've even confirmed it's true — a job ば can't touch, since ば always states a rule rather than responding to the moment. Keep today's なら as one shape ば wears; the fuller version of it is still ahead of you.
