Your friend tops up your glass. You already said you were done, you meant it, and the glass is full again anyway. The next morning someone asks how the night went, and none of the sentences you own quite fit. 私は飲む is true, but it makes the whole evening sound like your project. A sentence with your friend as the subject puts the spotlight on them, when the story is really about what happened to you.

This is the sentence you want:

```jp
私は友達にお酒を飲まされる。
```

*My friend makes me drink.*

飲まされる is two forms you already have, welded together. You know 飲ませる, the causative — the make-or-let form. You know 飲まれる, the passive — the form where the action lands on you rather than starting from you. Stack the passive on top of the causative and you get the form for sitting on the wrong end of somebody else's decision: they decided, your body carried it out, and you would rather it hadn't.

That last part is not decoration. This pattern is not neutral, the way English "I was asked to" is neutral. Built into it is the fact that you didn't want to. A speaker who chooses this form is complaining — mildly, maybe fondly, but complaining.

## How it works

Two moves, always in this order: build the causative first, then make that causative passive.

Start with a る-verb (ichidan — the ones that simply drop る). 食べる goes to causative 食べさせる, and then the せる ending swaps to せられる.

```jp
食べる → 食べさせられる
```

*to eat → to be made to eat*

Put it to work on the child who has strong opinions about dinner:

```jp
弟は毎晩野菜を食べさせられる。
```

*Every night my little brother is made to eat his vegetables.*

う-verbs (godan) take the same two steps, plus a shortcut the language worked out for you a long time ago. 飲む goes to 飲ませる, and that goes to 飲ませられる. Four syllables of ending is a lot to carry, so せられ got squeezed down to され.

```jp
飲む → 飲ませられる → 飲まされる
```

*to drink → to be made to drink (long form) → to be made to drink (short form)*

Both are correct Japanese. The long 飲ませられる is what the textbook builds and what you meet in careful writing; the short 飲まされる is what people actually say, and it is the one that will come flying at you in conversation. Recognize both. Produce whichever one you can assemble faster under pressure — nobody will mark you down.

The two irregular verbs behave themselves. する becomes させられる and 来る becomes 来させられる. Every する-verb inherits this automatically, so 掃除する gives 掃除させられる, 勉強する gives 勉強させられる, and so on down the list.

```jp
私は日曜日に掃除させられる。
```

*On Sundays I get put to work cleaning.*

Now the particles, which you can borrow wholesale from the passive. The person who made it happen takes に — the friend with the bottle, the teacher with the worksheet. The thing the action lands on keeps を. And you, the one whose hands and mouth actually did the unwanted thing, are the topic or subject: は or が.

The shape, then, is: victim は instigator に object を verb.

## Rules and exceptions

The short form has one hard limit: it belongs to う-verbs only, and only to those that don't end in す. 話す builds its causative as 話させる, so the causative-passive is 話させられる. Contracting that would hand you `話さされる`, with さ crashing into さ, and Japanese simply refuses.

```jp
私は会議で英語を話させられる。
```

*At meetings I'm the one who gets made to speak English.*

> Never contract an ichidan verb either. `食べさされる` is not a form. る-verbs and す-verbs use the long ending, full stop.

待つ deserves its own line, because 待たされる is one of the highest-frequency members of this entire family. Waiting is the classic thing that gets done to a person.

```jp
私は駅で一時間待たされる。
```

*I get left waiting at the station for an hour.*

Notice how the English drifts there. "Made to wait" is stiff; "kept waiting" is what an English speaker actually says. Expect that. The Japanese form is one consistent thing, but the natural English for it changes verb by verb, and chasing a single English formula will make your translations worse, not better.

> される on its own is also the plain passive of する. So 掃除される means something got cleaned — nobody was forced into anything. If you mean a person was put to work, you need the extra syllable: 掃除させられる.

One more edge, and this one is social rather than mechanical. Because the pattern carries a complaint, you don't use it for things you chose, even unpleasant ones. Stayed late for 残業 because you decided the work needed finishing? Plain sentence. Stayed late because the office kept you there? The causative-passive is exactly right, and everyone hearing it will pick up the resentment without you adding a single extra word.

## Casual speech

The contraction above *is* casual speech doing its work, and in conversation it goes further: the plain forms crowd out the polite ones. Among friends you'll hear 飲まされる and 飲まされた far more often than 飲ませられます, and the long 飲ませられた can land as slightly formal, or as someone deliberately slowing down to make a point of it.

For ichidan and す-verbs there's no shortcut available, so casual speech just strips the politeness instead: 食べさせられた, 話させられた, 掃除させられた. The ending stays long because it has nowhere shorter to go.

## Contrast

Here is the minimal triple. Same two people, same beer, three grammars, three completely different stories.

First, the causative you already know. Your older brother is the one making decisions:

```jp
兄は私にビールを飲ませる。
```

*My older brother makes me drink beer.*

Now the plain passive. Same brother, but nobody is forcing anybody — the beer was yours, he drank it, and the sentence exists because you're annoyed:

```jp
私は兄にビールを飲まれる。
```

*My brother goes and drinks my beer on me.*

And the causative-passive, where you are the subject just like in the passive, but this time the beer went down your throat:

```jp
私は兄にビールを飲まされる。
```

*My brother makes me drink beer.*

Hold the first and the third side by side. They report one single event from two different seats. 兄 has the plan in both of them; what changes is who the sentence is about and whose experience we're being told. Swap them by accident and you've just claimed to be the one pouring.

There's a bonus here, and it's rare enough to be worth pointing out. 食べられる has to work three jobs at once — passive, potential, and polite — and you disambiguate it from context. 食べさせられる has no such problem. However long it is, it means exactly one thing, every time. The length is the price of the clarity.

## A closing thought

Causative plus passive, in that order, with に on whoever is responsible and you as the topic — and a built-in shrug of complaint that you can't switch off. Keep half an eye on the fact that being made to do something for long enough tends to end somewhere else entirely: there's a pattern coming, ようになる, for the moment a thing you couldn't do becomes a thing you just do.
