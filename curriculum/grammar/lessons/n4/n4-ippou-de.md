Your older brother works constantly — up before six, never turns down overtime, the kind of person who answers work calls on Sunday. Your younger brother works when the mood strikes him, and it shows: shorter hours, more naps, a much lighter load on his shoulders. You want to say both of those things in the same breath, not as two flat, disconnected statements sitting next to each other with a period between them. You want the sentence itself to carry the little tug between the two brothers. That's the job of 一方で — on the other hand.

```jp
兄はよく働く一方で、弟はあまり働かない。
```

*My older brother works a lot; my younger brother, on the other hand, doesn't work much.*

You already have でも for "but," and だけでなく for "not only... but also." Neither quite fits here. でも would split this into two separate sentences and lose the sense that you're weighing one fact against the other inside a single thought. だけでなく would suggest the two facts pile up in the same direction, which is exactly backwards — these two facts pull apart, not together. 一方で sits in the middle of one sentence and points at that gap directly.

It helps to notice what 一方で is literally built from. 方 — the same 方 you already know as "direction" or "way" — turns 一方 into something like "one side" or "one direction." で then marks that as the ground you're standing on while you say the first half. So the phrase is quietly telling you: here's one side of this, and once you're through with it, expect the other side to follow. That mental picture — one side, then the other — is worth keeping close, because it's exactly what stops the pattern from feeling like a random grammar rule.

## How it works

一方で attaches straight onto the **plain form** — the casual, dictionary-style version of a word, the one you'd use talking to a friend rather than the polite です/ます one — of whatever comes right before it. For verbs and い-adjectives, nothing changes at the seam; you just tack 一方で on where a period would otherwise go.

```jp
忙しい → 忙しい一方で
```

*busy → busy, but on the other hand...*

You already saw the verb version above with 働く. Here's the い-adjective version in a full sentence, with a room that's great in one way and inconvenient in another:

```jp
部屋は広い一方で、駅から遠いです。
```

*The room is spacious, but on the other hand it's far from the station.*

Notice that nothing had to change about 広い itself — the plain form of an い-adjective already ends the way 一方で needs it to, so the two just sit next to each other.

な-adjectives — the describing words that need a な glued on right before a noun, like 上手な人 (a skilled person) — need that same な glued on right before 一方で too. Think of 一方で as behaving like a noun for grammar purposes here: whatever would normally need な before a noun needs な before this.

```jp
上手だ → 上手な一方で
```

*good at it → good at it, but on the other hand...*

```jp
兄は料理が上手な一方で、妹は下手です。
```

*My older brother is good at cooking, but on the other hand my younger sister is bad at it.*

Nouns follow the same な-adjective logic, except the connecting piece is usually である rather than a bare だ. This is one of the few spots where 一方で leans a little more formal and essay-like than most of what you've studied so far — which fits its job. You reach for 一方で when you're weighing two sides of something carefully, the way a newspaper article or a written report might, not when you're just chatting about your day.

```jp
学生である一方で
```

*being a student — but on the other hand...*

This is also why 一方で shows up so often around opinions and comparisons: describing a plan's upside against its downside, a person's strength against their weakness, a place's convenience against its cost. Anywhere you'd naturally hold two sides of something up next to each other, 一方で is doing quiet, structural work.

## Rules and exceptions

一方で only works glued into a single sentence, joining what came before it to what comes after. It can't stand at the front of a fresh sentence on its own the way でも can. Picture it as a hinge fixed in the middle of one sentence rather than a door you can open onto a new one — if you find yourself wanting to start a new sentence with it, you actually want でも instead.

The plain-form rule is strict, and this trips people up early on because everything else around a sentence like this can still be polite. Something like `忙しいです一方で` isn't a form anyone writes — keep the piece right before 一方で plain, and let the very end of the whole sentence carry whatever politeness level you need.

```jp
その店は安い一方で、あまりきれいじゃない。
```

*That shop is cheap, but on the other hand it's not very clean.*

Notice あまりきれいじゃない there — the plain negative of the な-adjective きれい, not a です form. That's the shape 一方で expects immediately on either side of it.

For な-adjectives and nouns, skip straight to な or である; a bare だ sitting right in front of 一方で, like `上手だ一方で`, reads as a mistake rather than a shortcut, even though だ is the plain form you'd use almost everywhere else.

There's also a quieter rule about meaning, not grammar: the two halves need some real tension between them for 一方で to earn its keep. Swapping in two facts that don't actually pull against each other — being busy, say, and liking cats — just reads oddly, because there's no gap left for 一方で to point at. The pattern needs friction to work with, the same way a pair of scales needs something on both sides before it means anything. If two clauses don't have that friction, plain て or し usually does a better, plainer job of just listing facts side by side.

> One more trap worth naming: 一方だ — the same word, minus で — means something completely different. It attaches to a verb of change and says that thing just keeps happening on its own, no second clause needed, as in `忙しくなる一方だ` ("it just keeps getting busier and busier"). Same three kanji, unrelated job. If there's no second half being weighed against the first, you're looking at that other pattern, not this one.

## Contrast

You've already met だけでなく — not only... but also — for stacking two facts that agree with each other and point the same way. 一方で is what you reach for the moment the two facts stop agreeing.

```jp
姉は真面目なだけでなく、優しいです。
```

*My older sister isn't just serious — she's kind too.*

```jp
姉は真面目な一方で、厳しいです。
```

*My older sister is serious, but on the other hand she's strict.*

Swap one connector for the other in that pair and the whole relationship between the two clauses flips. だけでなく tells you the second fact piles onto the first in the same direction, rounding out a positive picture. 一方で tells you it pulls the other way, adding an edge to the picture instead. Same two adjectives about the same person, opposite shape of sentence — and opposite feeling by the end of it.

Both connectors share that same slightly formal, considered feel compared to something like でも, which is worth remembering as a pair: reach for だけでなく when you're building a case that stacks up, and reach for 一方で the moment that case needs a "but, at the same time" running through the middle of it.

Hold onto this instinct for weighing two sides of something against each other. It's exactly what you'll need soon, when にとって lets you weigh a fact against someone's particular point of view rather than against another fact, and one side of a comparison starts depending on who's doing the looking. For now, though, the two-sided sentence itself is the whole trick — get comfortable weighing one plain-form clause against another, and the rest of this family of connectors will feel a lot less like separate rules and more like variations on the same move.
