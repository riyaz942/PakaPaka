Your older brother used to have coffee in his hand from morning until night. Lately, every time you see him, it is water. You want to say this in Japanese, and the first thing that comes to mind is 飲みません — but that just says he does not drink coffee, flat and timeless, as if the man had never touched the stuff in his life.

What you actually mean has a shape to it. There was a before, there is an after, and somewhere in between the coffee quietly stopped. Japanese has a pattern for exactly that shape.

```jp
兄はコーヒーを飲まなくなりました。
```

*My older brother stopped drinking coffee.*

Two moving parts: a negative, and a becoming. Someone became a person who does not do the thing. That is the entire idea, and once you see it that way the form almost builds itself.

## How it works

Start from the verb's ない form — the plain negative you already make every day. Then remember something you may not have been told out loud: ない behaves like an い-adjective (a describing word ending in い, like 高い or 寒い). And you already know what happens to an い-adjective in front of なる — the い becomes く.

So 飲まない loses its い, becomes 飲まなく, and takes なる on the end.

```jp
飲む → 飲まない → 飲まなくなる
する → しない → しなくなる
来る → 来ない → 来なくなる
```

*drink → does not drink → comes to no longer drink*
*do → does not do → comes to no longer do*
*come → does not come → comes to no longer come*

The two irregular verbs behave themselves here, which is a small mercy. する and 来る just hand over their normal negative stems and the rest is mechanical.

Notice where the politeness sits. The first verb stays plain forever — it is buried inside the pattern, not at the end of the sentence — and all the tense and politeness work happens on なる: なる, なります, なりました, なった. If you want the polite past, you get 〜なくなりました, and nothing else in the sentence changes.

The pattern is about change, so it loves subjects that drift. A cat, for example, who has decided that the food it demanded for three years is now beneath it.

```jp
猫が魚を食べなくなりました。
```

*The cat stopped eating fish.*

It also works beautifully for things that fade out of your reach rather than things you decide about. Rain comes down, the air goes white, and the mountain that was there a minute ago is simply not available to your eyes anymore.

```jp
雨で山が見えなくなりました。
```

*Because of the rain, the mountain became impossible to see.*

That sentence is worth sitting with, because English wants two different words here — *stopped* for the cat, *became impossible* for the mountain — and Japanese uses one. The pattern does not care whether a person chose the change. It only reports that the state flipped from doing to not doing.

And it flips for the inside of your head too. Names, meanings, whole trains of thought can go the same way as the mountain.

```jp
彼の名前が分からなくなりました。
```

*I no longer know his name.*

One more piece of range: because ない is an い-adjective, the pattern is not limited to verbs. Any negative that ends in ない can take the same treatment, so 寒くない becomes 寒くなくなる — the nights stopped being cold. You will meet this less often than the verb version, but when you meet it, it is the same machine.

## Rules and exceptions

The first trap is a spelling one, and it catches almost everyone once. The ない must lose its い. Writing `飲まないなる` or `飲まないになる` feels reasonable — なる takes に after nouns, after all — but ない is not a noun here, it is a describing word, so it goes to 飲まなく and takes なる bare.

The second trap is politeness creeping to the wrong end of the sentence. `飲みませんなる` is not a thing. Politeness is a coat you put on last, and here the last word is なる.

> ある has no negative form of its own — its negative simply *is* ない. So ある plus this pattern collapses into なくなる with nothing in front of it, which is spelled exactly like the ordinary verb 無くなる (to run out, to disappear). お金がなくなりました is the ordinary verb, and everyone reads it that way. That overlap is not a bug you can fix; it is just how the two words landed on top of each other, and context does the sorting.

The third thing is less a rule than a warning about meaning. This pattern describes a change that has settled in, not a single refusal at a single moment. If your brother turns down one cup of coffee on one Tuesday, that is not 飲まなくなりました — that is just him saying no today. The pattern claims that the world is different now than it was.

> Because it reports a change rather than a decision, なくなる carries no intention at all. 学校に来なくなりました says a person stopped showing up; it says nothing whatsoever about whether they meant to, and a listener will not assume they did.

The pattern is also perfectly happy in the plain non-past, 〜なくなる, when you are talking about something that has not happened yet — the coffee will stop, the mountain will disappear. Learners tend to lock onto 〜なくなりました because that is where they first met it, but the future half of the pattern is just as real.

## Casual speech

In conversation the ending drops its politeness and you get 〜なくなった, which is by far the form you will actually hear. Beyond that, the contractions you already know keep working inside the pattern, because the first half is just a normal negative doing normal negative things.

```jp
分からなくなった → わかんなくなった
```

*stopped understanding → stopped getting it (casual)*

That わかんない for 分からない is the same casual squeeze you have met before, and it survives the trip into this pattern intact. It is friends-and-family speech only — say it to a teacher and it will land oddly.

## Contrast

You already know ようになる, which is this pattern's mirror twin. ようになる is the door opening: someone reaches the point of doing a thing, or becomes able to. なくなる is the door closing.

```jp
兄はコーヒーを飲むようになりました。
```

*My older brother started drinking coffee.*

Put that beside the sentence you opened this lesson with and the only difference is the direction of travel. Same brother, same coffee, same なる underneath, opposite arrows. If you can build one, you can build the other, and it is worth deliberately practising them as a pair.

The sharper contrast is with the potential form れる・られる, because that is where a real meaning difference hides. Compare 漢字を読まなくなりました with this:

```jp
漢字が読めなくなりました。
```

*I became unable to read kanji.*

The first one — 読まなくなりました — says you can still read kanji perfectly well, you just do not anymore. The books are on the shelf and you walk past them. The second one, with the potential 読める inside, says the ability itself went away: you sit down with the page and nothing comes. Same pattern, and the entire difference in your life is whether れる・られる is in there.

This is one of the places where Japanese is more precise than English, which cheerfully uses "stopped reading" for both. When you build a sentence, ask yourself which one you mean, because your listener will hear the answer.

One last neighbour worth naming: 見える and 聞こえる are already about things arriving at your senses on their own, so 見えなくなる is the natural way to say something slipped out of sight, and you do not need the potential form on top of it.

## A closing thought

Negative, then becoming: the state flipped from doing to not doing, and the sentence reports the flip without judging it. Hold onto how neatly this pattern pairs with ようになる — you are about to meet はず, and the moment you can say what someone has stopped doing, you will want to say what they were supposed to be doing instead.
