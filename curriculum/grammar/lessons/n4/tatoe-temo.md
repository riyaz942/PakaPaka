You've been planning a Saturday hike with two friends for weeks. Thursday night the forecast flips: rain, all day, start to finish. Your phone buzzes. 'So we're canceling, right?' You are absolutely not canceling. What you need is a sentence that admits the rain is possible — and then walks straight past it without breaking stride.

Japanese builds that sentence with たとえ〜ても. Textbooks hand it to you at exactly this level for a reason: it is the first pattern whose whole job is to say, out loud, I have imagined the worst — and it changes nothing.

```jp
たとえ雨が降っても、私たちは山に登ります。
```

*Even if it rains, we will climb the mountain.*

Read it as two moving parts. たとえ, up front, means 'supposing' — a small flag that announces *what comes next is a what-if, not a report*. The 〜ても half is the 'even,' the part that does the actual conceding. Neither piece does the job alone; together they land as 'even if.'

## How it works

The structure is たとえ + te-form + も. You already own the te-form, so this is one more place it pays rent. And the も is the same も you use for 'me too.' Under the hood, 雨が降っても is almost 'it rains *too*' — the rain shows up, joins the day, and the plan carries on regardless. That little 'also' is doing all the heavy lifting, which is why the pattern feels so calm: nothing is being fought, just absorbed and stepped over.

Verbs are the easy case. Take the te-form and add も:

```jp
食べる → 食べても
```

*to eat → even if you eat*

So 降る becomes 降っても, 行く becomes 行っても, する becomes しても. If you can build the te-form, you can build this pattern — there are no new endings to memorize, only a new job for an old one.

I-adjectives trade their final い for くても:

```jp
高い → 高くても
```

*expensive → even if it is expensive*

```jp
たとえこの本が高くても、買います。
```

*Even if this book is expensive, I will buy it.*

Picture yourself in the bookshop, holding the one dictionary you actually came for, and the price tag is genuinely rude. 高くても — fine. You are buying it anyway. Notice that たとえ does not touch the price itself; it touches your attitude toward the price.

Na-adjectives and nouns have no te-form, so they take でも directly — one shape for both word types, a rare free lunch:

```jp
雨 → 雨でも
```

*rain → even if it rains*

```jp
たとえ明日雪でも、学校はあります。
```

*Even if it snows tomorrow, there will still be school.*

静かでも, 元気でも, 雪でも — the same move every time, no exceptions hiding in the corners.

Negatives behave too. いる becomes いなくても, and 行かない becomes 行かなくても. たとえ先生がいなくても — even if the teacher is not there — and you can already finish that thought yourself.

You might wonder whether you can drop たとえ and just say 雨が降っても on its own. You can, and people do, all the time. But without the flag up front, ても can also concede plain facts, so the sentence loses some of its what-if flavor. たとえ is you announcing, before anything else, that you are about to bend reality on purpose. Keep it in while you are learning — it makes your intention unmistakable.

One thing to feel in your bones: this is a **strong concession**. The second half of the sentence is not a wish or a maybe. It states what stands *no matter what* — a decision, a fact, a refusal to budge. The worse the condition in the first half, the more stubborn the second half sounds. That stubbornness is the whole personality of the pattern.

## Rules and exceptions

たとえ is a supposition flag, so it only flies over what-ifs. If the thing actually happened, たとえ is the wrong tool. About last Saturday's very real rain, `たとえ昨日雨が降っても、家にいました` sounds like you have forgotten whether it rained or not. For facts, drop the flag: plain ても concedes reality just fine, and ため names the cause outright — more on that one below.

The te-form itself is not optional. Learners regularly produce `たとえ雨が降るても` — dictionary form plus も — and it simply is not a shape Japanese has. It must be 降っても. The same trap waits with i-adjectives: `高いても` is broken, because the final い has to become く first, giving 高くても.

Word order, happily, is fixed: たとえ always opens its clause. You cannot tuck it in after the subject or let it drift to the end of the sentence. Front of the clause, every time — which also makes it wonderfully easy to catch when you are listening to someone else use it.

> Watch out for a look-alike. Japanese has a word that opens with the same three kana as たとえ and means 'for example.' Same face, different job: one sets up a supposition, the other sets up an illustration. Mixing them turns 'even if it rains' into 'for example, it rains' — a very strange thing to tell a friend on the phone.

> And do not read たとえ〜ても as 'even though.' 'Even though' concedes a fact — it really is raining, and still. This pattern concedes a possibility: it might rain, it might not, and the second half stands either way. If you already know it rained, you have outgrown this sentence.

The second half carries your real stance, so put something there that can bear the weight:

```jp
たとえ試験が難しくても、私は頑張ります。
```

*Even if the exam is difficult, I will do my best.*

Nobody knows yet whether the exam really is hard — that is exactly why たとえ is standing at the front. The supposition is uncertain. The 頑張ります is not.

## Casual speech

In relaxed speech the ても half contracts, and it contracts hard. Verb te-forms become 〜ったって: 降っても turns into 降ったって. I-adjectives follow the same current: 高くても becomes 高くたって. And でも after nouns and na-adjectives shrinks to だって: 雨でも becomes 雨だって.

Through all of this, たとえ itself never changes. It just sits at the front of the clause, watching its partner get shorter.

```jp
たとえ暇だって、あの店には行かない。
```

*Even if I am free, I will not go to that shop.*

暇だって is the casual twin of 暇でも. You will hear 〜たって and 〜だって constantly in conversation, and they carry a relaxed, slightly blunt feel. Try listening for it the next time a character in a show refuses to back down — the たとえ at the front is the tell. In writing and in polite speech, stay with 〜ても and 〜でも — the contracted versions can sound rough in the wrong room.

## Contrast

You already know ため, the formal 'because of,' and it lives in the same neighborhood — both patterns can hang on a word like 雨. The difference is reality. ため reports a cause that actually happened:

```jp
雨のため、私は家にいました。
```

*Because of the rain, I stayed home.*

It rained. Fact. The rain is the reason, full stop. Now set たとえ雨でも beside it: the rain is only a possibility, and the whole point of the sentence is that the possibility changes nothing. ため explains the world as it is; たとえ〜ても overrules the world as it might be.

限り — 'as long as' — is the mirror image, and the cleanest way to feel what たとえ〜ても is really doing:

```jp
暇な限り、毎朝公園を走ります。
```

*As long as I am free, I run in the park every morning.*

限り makes the condition everything: free mornings mean running, busy mornings mean no running, and the sentence lives or dies by that line. Flip it to たとえ暇でも、走りません and the condition stops mattering at all — free or busy, the answer is no. One pattern chains the result to the condition. The other cuts the chain and walks away.

So: たとえ flags the what-if, ても concedes it, and the second half says what stands anyway. Keep this one close — soon you will meet でしょう, the 'probably' word, and it loves parking itself in the second half of these sentences, softening 'I will, no matter what' into 'I probably will, no matter what.'
