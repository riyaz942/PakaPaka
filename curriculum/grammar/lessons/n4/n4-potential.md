Your friend wants to go to the beach this weekend, and already has the whole day sketched out — towels, a cooler, maybe a nap on the sand afterward. Before anyone agrees to any of it, there's one practical question standing between you and actually getting in the water.

```jp
泳げる？
```

*Can you swim?*

That ending isn't a new verb — it's 泳ぐ (to swim) wearing a different outfit. Japanese has a whole conjugation dedicated to exactly this idea: not "I swim" but "I'm capable of swimming, if it comes to that." It's called the potential form, and once you can build it, you can ask and answer this kind of question about almost anything you already know how to do — not just what you're doing, but what's within your reach to do.

泳ぐ on its own just states an action: someone swims, or is swimming, or will swim. 泳げる doesn't state an action at all — it states a standing fact about you, true whether or not you ever actually get in the water this weekend. That's the whole difference between the plain form and the potential form: one reports what happens, the other reports what's possible.

## How it works

The potential form replaces the ending of a verb with れる or られる, attached to the stem — everything before that final る. Which ending you use, and how much of the verb survives the swap, depends on which of three families the verb belongs to. You've already been sorting verbs into these families without necessarily naming them; this is just the first time the split actually changes which ending you reach for.

Ichidan verbs — the ones whose dictionary form always ends in an e-sound or i-sound plus る, like 食べる or 見る — are the easy case. Drop that final る and add られる, and you're done. 見る becomes 見られる, 起きる becomes 起きられる, and the pattern never wobbles.

```jp
食べる → 食べられる
```

*to eat -> can eat*

So if the food at your friend's place turns out fierier than expected, you can still hold your own, and say so without changing anything else about the sentence.

```jp
辛い料理も食べられる。
```

*I can even eat spicy food.*

Godan verbs — the ones like 買う or 話す whose ending actually shifts shape depending on what follows it — do this differently, and it's worth slowing down for. The final u-sound moves back to an e-sound, and る attaches directly, with no られる involved at all. 書く becomes 書ける, 飲む becomes 飲める, and 買う (to buy) becomes 買える.

```jp
買う → 買える
```

*to buy -> can buy*

Same shift, any object you can name.

```jp
傘が買える。
```

*I can buy an umbrella.*

This is also why 話せる already sits in your vocabulary as "can speak" — it was never a separate word to memorize in the first place. It's 話す run through this exact same shift, and once you see it that way, a handful of other "-eru" words you already know will start looking less like arbitrary vocabulary and more like verbs you'd already half-learned.

The two irregular verbs go their own way, and neither borrows the regular ichidan or godan pattern. する becomes できる, not `すれる` — an irregular jump you've probably already met as its own vocabulary word, long before you needed the grammar behind it. 来る becomes 来られる, borrowing the ichidan ending even though 来る itself isn't ichidan.

```jp
来る → 来られる
```

*to come -> can come*

Which means when your friend circles back with the actual invitation, the grammar is already sitting in your hands, fully built.

```jp
週末、来られる？
```

*Can you come this weekend?*

## Rules and exceptions

One habit worth building early: potential-form sentences usually mark what you're able to do with が, not を. 日本語が話せる, not `日本語を話せる` — you'll hear the second version in relaxed speech, and older textbooks sometimes treat it as acceptable too, but が is the one to default to until you have a feel for when the looser version fits. Some speakers reach for を when the sentence already has a strong sense of "doing something to this specific thing," but that's a nuance to notice later, not a reason to second-guess が now.

> Two words you already know look like they might be potential forms of verbs you've met, and neither one is. 見える ("to be visible") is not the potential of 見る — that would be 見られる. 聞こえる ("to be audible") is not the potential of 聞く — that would be 聞ける. Both describe something reaching you on its own, not your ability to perceive it, and mixing them up is one of the most common slips at this level, precisely because the two pairs look like they should line up and don't.

できる is worth one more note. It started life as する's potential form, but it's drifted into being the everyday word for "possible" or "can," used on its own the way English uses *can* — you'll see it standing alone in sentences that have nothing to do with する at all. That's a normal fate for a potential form popular enough to become a vocabulary word in its own right — it happened to a handful of others too, which is exactly why 話せる was already sitting in your list before this lesson.

One more thing worth knowing before you go further: once you've built a potential form, it behaves exactly like a brand-new ichidan verb from that point on. 食べられる conjugates like 食べる's cousin, not like the original 食べる — so every negative, past tense, or conditional you build on top of it follows ichidan rules, regardless of which family the original verb came from. That detail is what makes the contrast section below work at all. It also means the negative follows the same ichidan pattern: 食べられる's negative is 食べられない, built the same way you'd negate 見られる or any other ichidan verb, with nothing extra to learn.

## Casual speech

In relaxed conversation, ichidan potential forms routinely drop a syllable in the middle. 食べられる becomes 食べれる — the ら quietly disappears, and nobody blinks.

```jp
食べられる → 食べれる
```

*can eat -> can eat (casual)*

> This dropping only touches ichidan potential forms — the ones built with られる in the first place. Godan forms like 買える or 泳げる never had a ら to lose, so there's no shortcut to take there. Formal writing and most tests still expect the full られる, and some listeners quietly judge the short version, so treat it as something to recognize on sight before you lean on it yourself.

## Contrast: なら and たら with a potential verb

Once a verb sits in potential form, you'll find it slotting into conditionals you already know — and which one you pick still changes the sentence, even though the potential half stays identical. なら reacts to something already on the table: someone hands you a fact, and なら responds to it directly, almost like the second half of a conversation someone else started.

```jp
日本語が話せるなら、便利だ。
```

*If you can speak Japanese, that's convenient.*

Swap in たら, and the sentence stops reacting to a given fact and starts describing a future checkpoint instead — first this becomes true, then that follows, in that order.

```jp
日本語が話せたら、旅行が楽しい。
```

*Once you can speak Japanese, travel gets fun.*

Say the なら version and you sound like you're responding to someone who just told you they can speak Japanese. Say the たら version and you sound like you're picturing a future where that's true and imagining what comes after. Same potential verb, same conditional-sounding grammar, genuinely different scene in the listener's head.

The shape doesn't change here — れる and られる will look exactly the same whether the topic is ability or, soon, something being done to you instead of by you. There's no shortcut for telling them apart except context: who's doing what to whom, and whether the sentence is describing a capability or an event landing on someone. Hold onto that; passive voice borrows this exact conjugation next, and sorting the two apart is the hurdle waiting right after this one.
