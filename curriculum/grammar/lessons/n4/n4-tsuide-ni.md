You're lacing up your shoes to run to the convenience store before it closes. Your sister leans out of the kitchen: the fridge is empty, can you grab milk? You're already going out the door. You don't want to make the trip twice, and Japanese has one word for exactly this 'since I'm heading there anyway' move: ついでに.

```jp
コンビニに行くついでに、牛乳を買ってくる。
```

*While I'm going to the convenience store, I'll pick up some milk.*

The main mission is the convenience store. The milk is a rider — something small you fold into a trip you were making regardless. That is the whole feel of ついでに: one primary action, and a second, lighter action that gets a free ride on it. The two are not equal partners, and that imbalance is the point.

## How it works

ついでに attaches in two ways, depending on what sits in front of it.

**Dictionary-form verb + ついでに.** The verb names the trip or task you're already committed to. Attach ついでに straight onto the plain dictionary form — nothing comes between them.

```jp
駅まで歩くついでに、手紙を出す。
```

*While I'm walking to the station, I'll mail the letter.*

Walking to the station is happening no matter what. Mailing the letter is the passenger. Watch the order: the action you were already doing comes first, then ついでに, then the add-on. A comma after ついでに is normal and helps the reader feel the seam.

**Noun + の + ついでに.** When the occasion is naturally a noun — shopping, a walk, a work trip — join it with の.

```jp
買い物のついでに、図書館で本を返す。
```

*While I'm out shopping, I'll return the books at the library.*

Here 買い物 is the trip and returning the library books is the extra stop. する-verbs give you a choice: you can treat 掃除 as a bare noun (掃除のついでに) or use the full verb (掃除するついでに). Both are fine and mean the same thing.

```jp
台所を掃除するついでに、冷蔵庫も洗う。
```

*While I'm cleaning the kitchen, I'll wash out the fridge too.*

The も on 冷蔵庫 is doing quiet work — it says 'this one as well, on top of the kitchen.' ついでに and も are natural partners, because the pattern is fundamentally about adding one more thing.

## Rules and exceptions

**The add-on has to be the smaller thing.** ついでに only sounds right when the second action is minor next to the first. If the second action is really your main goal, a listener will be puzzled.

```jp
会社から帰るついでに、スーパーで野菜を買う。
```

*On my way home from work, I'll buy vegetables at the supermarket.*

Going home is the big fixed event; buying vegetables is slipped inside it. Flip the weights — if the shopping were the real reason you left — and ついでに becomes the wrong tool.

**Same person on both halves.** ついでに is not 'two people doing things at the same moment.' Whoever makes the trip is the one who does the extra task.

**Both actions share one occasion.** You can't use ついでに to staple together two errands from different days or separate trips. It is one outing, one stretch of being out, with an opportunity taken inside it.

> After a verb, ついでに clamps directly onto the dictionary form. Learners who just met the noun pattern sometimes carry the の across and write `行くのついでに` — that extra の is wrong. Only nouns take の.

One honest note: in relaxed speech you'll sometimes hear the の dropped after a noun as well, as in `買い物ついでに`. It is casual and common, but の is the standard choice and the one to write.

## Contrast

Your recent particles — こそ, さえ, のみ — all *narrow* or *spotlight*. のみ in particular means 'only this, nothing else.'

```jp
今日は郵便局のみ行く。
```

*Today I'll go only to the post office.*

ついでに pulls the opposite way. It doesn't trim the list down to one; it adds a second thing onto the single trip you were already taking.

```jp
郵便局に行くついでに、銀行にもお金を出す。
```

*While I'm at the post office, I'll also take out money at the bank.*

Same starting point — a trip to the post office — but のみ shuts the door on anything else, while ついでに props it open for one more stop. If you catch yourself reaching for 'only,' ついでに is not what you want.

```jp
病院に行くついでに、薬局で薬をもらう。
```

*While I'm going to the hospital, I'll get medicine at the pharmacy.*

The hospital visit is settled; the pharmacy is a few steps out of the way and costs you nothing extra. That is the situation ついでに is built for.

## Closing

ついでに = one trip you were making anyway, plus a small extra thing you fold into it. Attach it to a dictionary-form verb directly, or to a noun with の.

Hold onto this 'in the middle of an action' feeling. Soon you'll meet 最中に, which sounds close in English but runs the other way — it's about being caught right in the thick of something, not about picking up a bonus errand on the side.
