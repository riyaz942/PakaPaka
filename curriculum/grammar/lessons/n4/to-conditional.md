You're staying at a friend's place, and their phone starts buzzing on the table. Before anyone reaches for it, their cat leaps a foot in the air and knocks a cup off the shelf. Every single time, without fail, that's how it goes — the phone rings, the cat panics. There's no discussion involved, no choice, no "maybe" about it. That's exactly the shape of と: it connects two things where the first one triggers the second automatically, the way a light switch is wired straight to a bulb.

```jp
電話が鳴ると、猫が驚く。
```

*The phone rings, and the cat gets startled.*

That's と doing its real job — not describing a one-off event, but describing how things just go, every time, without anyone stepping in to decide anything. English blurs "if" and "when" together in a dozen different ways, but と narrows the range on purpose: reach for it only when the second half isn't up for debate.

Think of と as a wire, not a suggestion. Once you connect the two ends, current flows whether anyone's watching or not. A friend's cat isn't consulted before it flinches, and neither is the weather, the season, or your own face after a drink — those are exactly the situations と was built for, and the rest of this lesson is mostly about learning to spot them.

## How it works

と attaches directly to the plain, present-tense form of whatever leads into it — the same casual form you'd use to end a sentence on its own. と itself never conjugates, and it doesn't care about politeness levels; whatever comes before it just needs to already be in plain present shape, and everything else builds off that one clause.

```jp
窓を開けると、風が入る。
```

*Open the window, and wind comes in.*

窓を開ける is already plain present ("open"), so と simply hooks onto the end of it. Whatever follows と has to be something that occurs as a direct, mechanical consequence of the first half — never something you're planning, arranging, or deciding to do. Open the window, wind arrives; there's no gap in between for anyone to weigh in.

い-adjectives slot in the same way — the plain present form of an い-adjective is just the adjective itself, no extra piece needed before と:

```jp
お酒を飲むと、顔が赤くなる。
```

*Drink this, and your face turns red.*

赤くなる is 赤い (red) reshaped into 赤く and paired with なる ("to become") — a bodily reaction nobody has to choose, it just happens. This くなる/になる + と combination shows up constantly with seasons and general changes in the world, because seasons and bodies don't ask permission either:

```jp
冬になると、雪が降る。
```

*When winter comes, snow falls.*

```jp
春になると、暖かくなる。
```

*When spring comes, it turns warm.*

Both sentences use になる or くなる as the "turns into" hinge, then chain the result on with と. Seasons arriving, temperatures shifting, routines kicking in — と is the natural home for all of it, because none of it is a decision anyone makes. Worth pausing on one small trap here: 鳴る (to ring, from the phone example) and なる (to become) sound identical but are completely different words written with different kanji. Context sorts them out fast once you've seen a few of each, but the first week they can blur together.

と has a second job worth knowing: reporting something you found out the moment the first action wrapped up.

```jp
家に帰ると、雨だった。
```

*I got home, and it was raining.*

Here the second half sits in the past (雨だった), because you're reporting a discovery, not stating a standing rule — you walked in the door and, huh, it turns out it's raining. The half in front of と still has to stay present plain regardless of when the whole sentence is set — 帰る, never 帰った, even though the story itself already happened.

## Rules and exceptions

Here's where と gets strict: the second half has to be something that just happens on its own — never something anyone requests, invites, orders, or plans. Requests, invitations, suggestions, and stated intentions are all off-limits after と, no matter how naturally they'd translate into English with "when" or "if."

> Something like `忙しいと、手伝ってください` doesn't work. Being busy doesn't automatically produce a request for help the way a phone ringing automatically startles a cat — a request is a choice someone makes, not a reflex, so と can't carry it.

For that kind of sentence, 場合 does the job instead — it was built to hold plans, requests, and advice, which is exactly what と refuses to hold:

```jp
忙しい場合、手伝ってください。
```

*If you're busy, please help.*

場合 marks a particular situation and leaves plenty of room for a decision, a request, or a plan tied to it. と refuses that room on purpose — it's reserved for the automatic and the inevitable, the sentences where nobody is actually choosing anything.

## Contrast: と vs 場合

You already know 場合 for flagging "in the case of X" before advice or a plan. と looks similar on the surface — both sit after a plain clause and set up a second half — but they carry very different weight. 場合 describes a scenario someone is reasoning about ahead of time; と describes something that simply keeps happening, rain or shine, decision or no decision.

```jp
雨の場合、公園に行かない。
```

*If it rains, we won't go to the park.*

```jp
雨が降ると、公園に行かない。
```

*When it rains, we don't go to the park.*

Both sentences land on the same outcome, but they sound different. The 場合 version reads like a plan somebody worked out in advance — "in the case that it rains, here's what we've decided." The と version reads like a fact about how things go — rain shows up, the park trip just doesn't happen, every time, no planning involved, almost like a rule of the household rather than a choice anyone is making today. Swap them and you swap the flavor: と turns a decision into a law of nature, 場合 turns a law of nature back into a choice.

This is also why と sentences tend to sound more confident, almost blunt, next to their 場合 counterparts. Saying 雨の場合、公園に行かない leaves a little space — maybe circumstances change, maybe someone reconsiders. Saying 雨が降ると、公園に行かない closes that space entirely. If you've ever wondered why a textbook example with と can sound oddly matter-of-fact in translation, this is usually why: the grammar itself is doing the work of removing doubt, not just describing a sequence of events.

One more small thing worth knowing: unlike some connectors you've seen, と doesn't shrink or shift in casual speech. Friends talking quickly still say と exactly as written — there's no shortened version to learn here, so once the plain-present-plus-と shape is solid, you already have the whole pattern in every register.

Reach for と when you're stating something automatic, habitual, or freshly discovered. Reach for 場合 the moment a request, a suggestion, or a real decision needs to sit in the second half. Keeping that line clear is most of what と actually asks of you — and it's the same instinct you'll lean on again once から shows up for reasons that are simply true, rather than reasons someone is asking you to accept.

One last thing worth carrying forward: と rewards paying attention to what kind of sentence you're actually building before you reach for it. If you catch yourself about to type a request, an invitation, or a plan right after と, that's the signal to stop and swap in 場合 instead — not a sign that と is broken, just a sign it's the wrong tool for that particular sentence. Get comfortable with that swap and the rest of the pattern mostly takes care of itself.
