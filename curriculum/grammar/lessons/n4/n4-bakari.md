Your host family's little brother has a bowl of お菓子 in front of him every single time you walk into the kitchen — breakfast, lunch, it doesn't matter. Vegetables sit on the plate untouched, day after day, and eventually you want to say it out loud, the way you'd say "he only eats sweets" in English. That's the job ばかり does: it plants itself right after a noun and announces "nothing else, just this — and it's been going on."

```jp
弟はお菓子ばかり食べている。
```

*My younger brother eats nothing but sweets.*

Notice what's missing from that sentence: no negative verb, no extra word bolted on to spell out the exclusion. ばかり does the whole job by itself, and it leans a little judgmental while it does it — you're not neutrally reporting a diet, you're complaining about one, and native listeners will hear that complaint whether you meant to put it there or not.

That single word is doing two jobs at once, and it's worth separating them before you go any further, because mixing them up is the single most common mistake with this pattern. One job is about *what*: out of everything that could be true, only this one thing is. The other job, which you'll meet a little further down, is about *when*: something happened, and it happened so recently that it still feels like news. Both jobs share the same name and the same little word, but they attach to different pieces of grammar, and only context tells you which one a given sentence means.

## How it works

ばかり attaches directly to a noun, no particle needed in between most of the time — though a topic marker like は often rides along outside it, the way it did in that first sentence, marking who the whole comment is about. The meaning underneath is always "nothing but X": whatever else could reasonably be happening instead is conspicuously absent, and that absence is the point.

Your own week can look the same some months. Think back to a stretch where every evening after class disappeared into one activity and nothing else got touched — that's exactly the shape ばかり is built for.

```jp
私は宿題ばかりしている。
```

*I'm doing nothing but homework.*

That sentence works because ばかり sits directly on 宿題, the thing being done exclusively. It doesn't matter whether that's actually true down to the minute — you slept, you ate, you did other things too — ばかり is about what stands out when you look back on the stretch as a whole. That's the flavor: not a literal 100%, but "this is what defined the period."

ばかり has a second job that looks completely different on the surface: bolt it onto the Ta-form — the plain past form you already know from -た, like 起きた for "woke up" — and it stops meaning "nothing but" and starts meaning "just did." The trigger switches from exclusivity to timing: this thing finished so recently that it's still fresh, close enough that mentioning it feels current rather than historical.

```jp
起きる → 起きた → 起きたばかり
```

*wake up → woke up → just woke up*

Same verb, three stages: dictionary form, plain past, and plain past plus ばかり for "moments ago." Once ばかり lands on the Ta-form, it almost always shows up with です or だ closing the sentence out, because the whole point is usually a comment about right now — someone asking what's going on, and you answering with what just happened.

```jp
今、起きたばかりです。
```

*I just woke up.*

The same shape works for anything that recently wrapped up, not just waking up. Picture texting a friend who's clearly stressed and finding out why: it turns out they only clocked in for the first time yesterday, so of course everything still feels overwhelming.

```jp
友達は仕事を始めたばかりだ。
```

*My friend just started the job.*

## Rules and exceptions

The Ta-form requirement for the "just did" reading is not optional, and this is where the two jobs of ばかり can genuinely trip you up. Attach ばかり to a dictionary-form verb hoping for "just do" and you don't get that meaning at all — you get something that doesn't parse the way you intended, because ばかり on its own carries no sense of completion. The completion has to already be sitting in the Ta-form before ばかり arrives to comment on how recent it was.

> Don't reach for `起きるばかり` when you mean "just woke up" — the Ta-form (起きた) is what carries the "this already happened" meaning; ばかり only adds "and it happened recently." Drop the Ta-form and there's nothing left for ばかり to attach its timing to.

There's also a nuance trap on the noun side, and it trips up learners in the opposite direction — not a grammar mistake, but a tone one. Noun + ばかり is technically neutral; it just states an exclusive fact about what's happening. But in real conversation it almost always carries a complaint, the same way English "all he does is..." sounds annoyed even though it's, strictly speaking, just a true statement. There's no way to keep the same word and strip the attitude back out — the raised eyebrow comes bundled in, every time.

The "just did" reading has one more sharp edge worth naming: "recently" is a feeling, not a stopwatch. Whether たばかり sounds natural depends on how the speaker sees the gap, not on an actual number of minutes. Someone who moved cities a month ago might still say 引っ越したばかりだ if the boxes are still unpacked and the new routine hasn't settled in — the sentence is really about how fresh the change still feels to the person saying it, not a strict cutoff you can look up.

## Casual speech

In relaxed conversation, ばかり often stretches into ばっかり — same meaning, heavier emphasis, the kind of sound you'd expect from someone who's genuinely fed up rather than just reporting a fact. 弟はお菓子ばっかり食べてるよ carries the exact same complaint as the very first example in this lesson, just with the volume turned up: ばかり lengthens into ばっかり, and ている contracts down to てる the way it usually does when people are talking quickly. Save ばっかり for friends and family. Written Japanese, and anything even slightly formal, wants the fuller ばかり.

## Contrast

You already have ことがある for talking about experience — something you've done at some undefined point in your life, with no particular claim about when. Ta-form plus ばかり looks almost identical on the page, since both sit on the exact same Ta-form starting point, but the two constructions are answering completely different questions: not "has this ever happened to you," but "did this happen just moments ago."

```jp
日本に行ったことがある。
```

*I have been to Japan before.*

```jp
私は日本に行ったばかりだ。
```

*I just went to Japan.*

Same verb, same destination, same Ta-form beginning — and the two endings send the sentence to opposite ends of a timeline. ことがある points backward with no particular date attached, a fact filed away in your personal history. たばかり points at right now, looking back over its shoulder at something that only just wrapped up. Swap one ending for the other and you've swapped "at some point in my life" for "as of a few minutes ago," which is exactly why this pair is worth slowing down on the first few times you meet it.

Hold onto that "just wrapped up" feeling — you're about to meet ところ, a cousin construction that covers the same general territory from a few different angles at once: about to happen, happening right now, or just finished, all wrapped into one word. ばかり only ever handles that last piece, the "just finished" edge, but it's good preparation for seeing how much more ground ところ ends up covering.
