Your friend sends you a photo of their kitchen window with a crack running straight across it. You want to describe what you're looking at right now, not the moment it happened.

```jp
窓が壊れている。
```

*The window is broken.*

That's the same て-form + いる you already used for progressive ている (something happening right now). Here it's doing a different job: it describes a resultant state — the lingering result of something that already happened, still true at this moment. The window broke at some point in the past; what you're reporting is the state it's in now.

## How it works

The shape is identical to progressive ている: take the て-form of the verb and add いる. What changes is the *kind* of verb you attach it to. With a verb that describes a one-time change — something flips from one state to another and stays there — ている points at the state left behind, not the flipping itself.

```jp
壊れる → 壊れて
```

壊れる (to break) is exactly this kind of verb: a window can't be "in the middle of" breaking for very long, it either hasn't broken or it has. So 壊れている can only mean *is broken*, never *is breaking*.

The same logic covers things left in a spot after arriving there.

```jp
本が机の下に落ちている。
```

*The book has fallen and is under the desk.*

落ちる (to fall) is another one-shot change — the falling itself takes a second, but 落ちている describes the much longer stretch afterward, where the book just sits there under the desk.

A handful of verbs use this same pattern to describe a state you're simply *in*, with no separate "event" moment you're picturing at all.

```jp
私は先生を知っている。
```

*I know the teacher.*

知る technically means "to come to know" — the moment information lands in your head. But 私は先生を知る with no いる would sound like you're about to learn who your teacher is, right now, live. 知っている is how you say you already have that knowledge and are holding onto it. 持つ (to hold) works the same way — 財布を持っている is simply "I have a wallet," not "I am in the act of grabbing one."

```jp
会議の時間はもう決まっている。
```

*The meeting time has already been decided.*

決まる (to be decided) flips once, from undecided to decided, and もう (already) leans into exactly that: the decision landed at some point, and ている reports the settled state it left behind.

## Rules and exceptions

The test isn't the verb's meaning in English — it's whether the action is a one-time flip or something you can watch unfold second by second. 始まる (to begin) sounds like an instant, but it's used the same way once the event is underway:

```jp
授業はもう始まっている。
```

*Class has already started.*

The class began at a specific moment; ている covers the whole stretch of time you're now sitting inside, after that moment.

> 死ぬ (to die) is the sharpest example of this. Death happens once and doesn't repeat, so 死んでいる can only mean *is dead* — never *is dying*. English speakers reach for "is dying" instinctively and it trips people up the first week; that's normal.

```jp
猫が死んでいる。
```

*The cat is dead.*

One thing this pattern does *not* do: it doesn't reach back and describe someone actively doing the breaking, deciding, or dropping. `窓を壊している` with a direct object reads as someone breaking the window *right now*, live, because 壊す (the transitive partner of 壊れる) is a normal ongoing action when you're the one doing it. Resultant ている belongs to the intransitive side — the thing that changed, not the person who changed it.

## Casual speech

In relaxed spoken Japanese, ている often shrinks to てる, and this pattern is no exception.

- 壊れている → `壊れてる`
- 知っている → `知ってる`

Same meaning, just faster to say. You'll hear the contracted form constantly; write the full ている in anything formal.

## Contrast: the same ている, two different jobs

This is where 窓が壊れている and the progressive ている you already know split apart, even though they're built identically. With an ordinary ongoing action, ている still means "happening right now":

```jp
彼は今友達と話している。
```

*He is talking with a friend right now.*

話す is a verb you can watch unfold — talking has duration, a beginning and an end you could interrupt. That's progressive ている, the one you already met. 決まる and 壊れる can't be interrupted mid-flip the same way, so the exact same grammar points at the aftermath instead.

A few verbs sit right on the border and genuinely go either way depending on context, which is honestly just messy — no clean rule saves you here, only the situation does.

```jp
彼はシャツを着ている。
```

*He is wearing a shirt.*

着る (to put on) is momentary — the act of pulling a shirt over your head takes a couple of seconds. So 着ている almost always describes the state afterward, *wearing* it, the way you'd use it to describe someone across the room. Only in a scene where you're watching someone get dressed in real time would the same sentence mean "is putting on" instead.

One more state ている covers, that you'll want soon: this lesson stuck to verbs that already describe their own change, like 壊れる. There's a separate pattern, てある, for describing a state that someone deliberately *set up* — a window someone left open on purpose, a table someone already set. Hold onto that distinction; it's the whole reason てある exists.

Same て-form + いる shape you already knew — the only new skill is checking whether the verb can be "in progress" at all before you decide what the sentence is reporting.
