Your friend is trying to decide whether to swing by tonight, and texts you: is she actually going to be home? You haven't called to double-check, and the last thing you want is to promise something and be wrong about it. What you can say, honestly, is what you currently know:

```jp
私の知る限り、彼女は今日、家にいる。
```

*As far as I know, she's home today.*

You've just hedged your answer instead of stating it as fact, and that difference matters — if it turns out she left an hour ago, you didn't lie, you just reported the edge of what you knew at the time. 限り comes from the verb `限る`, "to limit, to restrict" — as a noun it means "the extent, the boundary." Put a clause in front of it and you're saying "to the extent that this clause is true, here's my answer."

Depending on what's inside that clause, the same word reads as either "as far as ~" (the edge of what you personally can confirm — your knowledge, your eyes, your information) or "as long as ~" (the edge of how long some condition keeps holding true). It's the same underlying idea both times: 限り marks a boundary, and everything after it is only guaranteed to hold within that boundary. Whether the boundary is "what I happen to know" or "how long this situation lasts" just depends on the verb sitting in front of it.

## How it works

限り attaches to the end of a clause in its plain form — the same dictionary-form, plain-negative, or plain-adjective shape you already use in front of ため. Verbs work exactly as they are, no changes needed:

```jp
見る限り、今日の天気はいい。
```

*As far as I can see, today's weather is good.*

Past tense works exactly the same way, and honestly reads a little more naturally for a lot of "as far as I've seen/heard" statements, since you're reporting something you already checked rather than something you're checking right now — 見た限り, "as far as I've looked and seen," instead of 見る限り. Both show up constantly; pick whichever matches whether you're describing an ongoing habit of noticing something or one specific look you already took.

I-adjectives work as-is too — nothing gets inserted before 限り:

```jp
忙しい限り、彼は休まない。
```

*As long as he's busy, he doesn't rest.*

Na-adjectives need their な first, the same な you already use in front of a noun:

```jp
元気な限り、彼は毎朝走る。
```

*As long as he's healthy, he runs every morning.*

One shape shows up on its own constantly: できる限り, literally "to the extent that it's possible." In practice it's just "as much as I can":

```jp
できる限り、私は手伝う。
```

*I'll help as much as I possibly can.*

限り isn't stiff or bookish the way some "extent" vocabulary can feel — you'll hear 私の知る限り and できる限り in ordinary spoken conversation just as often as in writing. It carries a slightly careful, considered tone, the sound of someone being precise about what they can and can't back up, but it never sounds overly formal or out of place between friends.

Now look back at that very first example: 私の知る限り, not 私が知る限り. Inside the little clause that describes 限り — 「私の知る」, "the extent that I know" — the subject can take の instead of が. That's a small quirk of clauses that describe or modify something (this one is modifying 限り, the same way a clause can modify a noun): が still works fine too, and you'll hear both.

```jp
私が知っている限り、彼は約束を忘れない。
```

*As far as I know, he doesn't forget his promises.*

Same meaning as the opening example, just built with 知っている (the "currently in a state of knowing" form) and が instead of の. Neither is more correct than the other — の is simply the option that surfaces specifically inside clauses like this one, and 限り invites it constantly. You'll mostly see の with a short, single-word subject like 私 right before the verb; once the clause gets longer or more complicated, が tends to take over, which is exactly why the 知っている example above reaches for が instead.

## Rules and exceptions

限り only measures something the speaker can actually vouch for — their own knowledge, their own eyes, or a condition that's genuinely still true. That's why it reads as a hedge rather than a flat claim. Reach for it when you want to signal "this is the edge of what I can confirm," not when you're already certain — a plain sentence handles certainty just fine on its own.

The plain-form rule is strict about one thing: nothing gets inserted before 限り on a verb or an i-adjective. `忙しいだ限り` is wrong — the i-adjective already finishes the clause by itself, so that だ has nowhere to go. Only na-adjectives (and nouns) need something in that slot, and what they need is な, never だ: `元気だ限り` is wrong, 元気な限り is right.

> できる限り looks like it should build a fresh literal meaning every time — "the limit of what's possible" — but in practice it's frozen into a set phrase, the way "as far as possible" is frozen in English. Don't expect to take it apart and rebuild it; just learn it as a unit.

限り almost always describes something ongoing — a state of knowing, a state of health, a stretch of time — not a single finished event. That's part of why the "as long as" reading feels so natural with ある限り or 元気な限り: you're pointing at a condition that keeps being true, not something that happened once and ended. Try to build a sentence around a single completed action instead, and it stops making sense — there's no "edge" to measure, because the thing you'd be measuring already finished.

That's also why 限り doesn't do the job of a plain "if"-style hypothetical. Those ask what would follow from a possibility that might not hold at all. 限り instead leans on something the speaker is already treating as true right now — their current knowledge, a condition already in place — and measures how far that reaches. It's honest, mundane accounting of what you can vouch for, not speculation about what might happen.

## Contrast

限り and ため (cause) can end up sounding almost identical in English translation, which is exactly why they're worth setting side by side. Watch what happens when you swap one for the other:

```jp
時間がある限り、彼は本を読む。
```

*As long as he has time, he reads books.*

```jp
時間があるため、彼は本を読む。
```

*Because he has time, he reads books.*

Same two facts, different logic underneath. The ため version claims that having time is the *reason* he reads — take away the time and the reasoning collapses. The 限り version makes no claim about cause at all; it just says his reading is tied to the whole *stretch* during which he has time, holding only as long as that condition does. A quick test: if you can swap your "because" for "for as long as" and the sentence still lands the same point, you're in 限り territory, not ため's.

This is where 限り's honesty about mess actually helps you: plenty of real situations sound fine described either way, because "he has time, and that's why/while he reads" is genuinely ambiguous between cause and duration in everyday thought. Japanese speakers don't agonize over which one to pick any more than you'd agonize over "because" versus "while" in English — they reach for whichever framing fits what they mean to emphasize, cause or extent, and move on.

The other patterns you've already met aren't close enough to cause real confusion here. について marks what you're talking *about*, によると marks where information *came from*, として marks a role someone is acting *in* — none of them measure an extent the way 限り does, so you won't find yourself reaching for the wrong one by accident. ため is the only real neighbor, precisely because cause and extent so often describe the very same situation from two different angles.

Hold onto that "edge of a condition" feeling — you'll want it again soon, when たとえ〜ても hands you the flip side: not the boundary of what's true, but what stays true even past it.
