You're standing in the lobby of a friend's company, waiting while they finish a call. A small sign by the elevator catches your eye: 社員のみ使えます. You can guess most of it — 社員 is the staff, 使えます is "can use" — but that middle word, のみ, is doing something specific. It's not decoration. It's telling you, flatly, that this elevator has exactly one group of people who are allowed near it, and you are not one of them.

You could read the same sign a dozen different ways in casual speech — "just staff," "staff and nobody else," "sorry, employees only" — but whoever printed that sign didn't reach for any of those. They reached for one tight, formal word that does the whole job by itself.

## How it works

のみ means "only" or "nothing but," and it attaches straight onto a noun — no particle, no conjugation, nothing in between. Noun + のみ, and that noun becomes the one and only thing the sentence is willing to allow.

```jp
会社の入口は社員のみ使えます。
```

*Only employees can use the company entrance.*

Notice that のみ sits right where a particle like は or が might otherwise go, and the verb follows normally after it. You don't need to add anything to make the sentence work — のみ already carries the restriction on its own. Nothing before it, nothing after it, just the noun and the verb doing their normal jobs around it.

のみ also sits comfortably right before です, closing out a sentence the same way a regular noun would.

```jp
このレストランは現金のみです。
```

*This restaurant is cash only.*

Read that one out loud and you can hear why it works so well as signage: it's short, it's blunt, and there's no room to misread it. です is doing the same job it always does — wrapping up the sentence — and のみ has already done the work of narrowing "payment" down to one single option before です even gets there.

That's the shape you'll see most often: a rule, a policy, a sign — something written down, meant to be read rather than said out loud. A workplace posts a notice, a store prints a sign, an exam proctor hands out a printed instruction sheet, and every one of them reaches for のみ instead of anything looser.

```jp
試験の間は日本語のみ話せます。
```

*During the exam, only Japanese may be spoken.*

Same pattern each time: take the one noun that's allowed, drop のみ right after it, and let the rest of the sentence — a verb, です, whatever you need — carry on exactly as it would without のみ there at all. The grammar around it never has to change shape to make room.

## Rules and exceptions

のみ is formal, written-register Japanese — it belongs to the same world as printed rules and official announcements, not to a conversation between friends. You'll meet it constantly in signs, contracts, and business writing, and almost never hear it in someone's casual speech about their own day.

> Don't reach for のみ when you're just chatting. Saying `これのみ食べた` to a friend about your lunch sounds like you're reading from a memo, not describing a sandwich.

That stiffness is the whole point of the word, not a flaw in it. A company doesn't want its entrance policy to sound warm and conversational — it wants it to sound like a rule, and のみ carries exactly that weight. Once you can hear that formality, you'll start noticing のみ everywhere: on ticket machines, in contracts, on the fine print under a store's opening hours.

This lesson only covers のみ attached directly to a noun, which is by far the form you'll need most at this level. It can also attach after other things in more advanced writing, but treat that as a different problem for a different day — for now, noun first, のみ right after it, nothing in between. Resist the urge to bolt it onto a verb the way you might with some other patterns; at this stage, keep it a noun's word.

## Contrast

You already know 以外 for "except" — it pulls one thing *out* of a group and leaves the rest standing. のみ does the opposite: it throws everything out except the one thing it names.

```jp
日曜日以外は毎日仕事です。
```

*Every day except Sunday is work.*

```jp
日曜日のみ休みます。
```

*I only rest on Sunday.*

Same subject, same week, completely different shape of sentence. 以外 tells you what's left after removing one day from the group; のみ tells you that one day is all there is, full stop. Swap one for the other in either sentence and the meaning flips almost entirely — worth sitting with that pair until the difference feels automatic.

さえ makes a different kind of point. It picks an extreme example to prove how easy or how surprising something is — "even a beginner could do this." のみ never argues that way; it just draws a hard line around who's allowed in, with no interest in surprising you.

```jp
この仕事は大人のみできます。
```

*Only adults can do this job.*

```jp
この仕事は子供さえできます。
```

*Even children can do this job.*

Same job, same verb, and the two sentences say almost opposite things about how demanding the work is. 大人のみ makes it sound like it takes real skill, something only a narrow group can manage; 子供さえ makes it sound so simple that even the least likely candidate can pull it off.

Sign-language のみ and this さえ trick don't fight for the same space — you'll usually know which one you need from the tone of what you're writing, not from the vocabulary itself. If you're posting a rule, reach for のみ. If you're making a point about how easy or surprising something is, reach for さえ instead.

That elevator sign wasn't being rude. It was just doing what のみ always does — naming one group and closing the door on everyone else, briefly and without apology. Hold onto that flat, no-exceptions feel; you'll want it again once we get to ほかない, where "there's no choice but to" carries the same refusal to budge.
