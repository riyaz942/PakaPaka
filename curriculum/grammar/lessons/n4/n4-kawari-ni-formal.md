You are telling a friend about today's Japanese exam. 難しい, you start to say — difficult. Except that's not quite right. Every question was written in oddly indirect language, the time limit was tight, and by the end your hand hurt from writing — none of that is really what 難しい points at. What you actually mean is closer to annoying, and you want to say so without pretending you never said 難しい at all.

That is the exact job というより does. It does not erase the first word — it upgrades it. You put the first guess down, mark it as a rough draft with というより, and follow it with the sharper word.

```jp
この試験は難しいというより面倒くさい。
```

*This exam isn't so much difficult as it is annoying.*

You could of course just say 面倒くさい and stop there, dropping 難しい entirely. But that loses something real: the listener never finds out that 難しい was your first, natural reaction, and that you're now sharpening it on purpose. というより keeps both words in play and shows the correction happening in real time, which is exactly why it shows up so often in casual conversation — it's how people actually think out loud.

This is a small but useful trick for a learner, too. You don't always need the perfect word on the first try. Say the word you already know, then reach for というより and a second, closer word — the sentence still lands, and it sounds more natural than pausing mid-thought to hunt for the ideal adjective before you say anything at all.

## How it works

というより attaches to the plain form — the same shape you already use in front of から and と. If the word in front of it is an i-adjective or a verb, you don't touch it at all; you just tack というより straight onto the end.

It helps to see where the pattern comes from. という is built from と (a quoting particle — the little word that marks what someone said or thought) plus いう (to say), so というより literally reads something like more than saying that. より is the same より you'd use in a plain comparison. Put them together and you get exactly what the pattern does: more than saying the first thing, this is closer to the truth.

Nouns and na-adjectives work slightly differently, because their plain form usually carries だ (先生だ, 静かだ) — and というより doesn't want that だ sitting in front of it. Drop it, and put the bare noun or na-adjective directly before the pattern instead.

```jp
彼は先生というより友達だ。
```

*He's more a friend than a teacher.*

```jp
この部屋は静かというより暗い。
```

*This room isn't so much quiet as it is dark.*

Notice the two halves don't have to match in part of speech. 静か is a na-adjective, 暗い is an i-adjective, and というより sits between them without any trouble, because it isn't comparing grammar categories — it's comparing two descriptions of the same room.

The same freedom applies to verbs. Anything that ends in the plain dictionary form can sit in front of というより exactly as it is, with no extra piece bolted on. If you've been bracing for a special connecting form the way some patterns demand, you can relax here — the plain form you already default to is the whole story.

というより itself never changes shape. No past tense, no negative, no polite ます version living inside it. Whatever tense or politeness the sentence needs goes on the words sitting on either side of it, never on というより itself. That's one less thing to memorize than most of what you've met so far — the conjugation work all happens before というより, never to it.

## Rules and exceptions

というより always needs two descriptions of the same thing sitting in one sentence — a first guess and a better one. It has no life as a stand-alone reason the way から does; there's no reading of というより that works like because, even though both patterns attach to the same plain form.

That's also why というより never opens a conversation out of nowhere. There always has to be a first attempt sitting somewhere nearby for it to improve on — sometimes your own last sentence, sometimes something the other person just said.

This is also why というより tends to cluster with words that sit close together in meaning — 難しい and 面倒くさい, 忙しい and 大変, つまらない and 悲しい. Pick two words that have nothing to do with each other and the sentence stops making sense, because there's no shared thing left for the second word to describe more accurately than the first.

> The word *after* というより is the one that wins. Reverse the order and you reverse which description the speaker actually stands behind — 忙しいというより大変だ and 大変というより忙しい are not the same claim.

```jp
今日は忙しいというより大変だ。
```

*Today isn't so much busy as it is rough.*

```jp
この映画はつまらないというより悲しい。
```

*This movie isn't so much boring as it is sad.*

It's also easy to reach for というより when what you actually mean is instead of. English rather than can carry both jobs — a corrected description, or a chosen action instead of a different one — but というより only does the first. It never swaps one action for another; that job belongs to 代わりに, which shows up again below.

A related trap: というより doesn't mean more than in the sense of quantity, the way you might compare heights or prices. It's not measuring which word is bigger; it's judging which word is truer. 忙しいというより大変だ isn't claiming 大変 outranks 忙しい on some scale — it's saying 大変 is simply the more accurate word for today.

## Casual speech

In relaxed speech, と regularly contracts to って — というのは becomes っていうのは, and というより follows the same pattern: っていうより. You'll hear native speakers drop it mid-sentence, correcting themselves or someone else out loud, the same way you might in English with or, actually.

The full というより never disappears from writing or from careful, polite speech — a work email or a formal apology keeps it intact. っていうより belongs to texting, chatting with friends, and the kind of spoken Japanese where you're thinking as you talk. Treat the choice between them the same way you'd treat any other casual contraction you've already met: register, not meaning, is what changes.

If you're ever unsure which one to reach for, default to the full というより. It's never wrong, even in a casual conversation — it just sounds a shade more careful. っていうより only sounds wrong the other way around, dropped into a formal email where it reads as too loose for the occasion.

```jp
っていうより、面倒くさいだけだよ。
```

*More accurately, it's just annoying, that's all.*

## Contrast

から states a reason — a fact the speaker is pointing to as the cause of something. というより doesn't state a reason at all; it revises a description. 難しいから休む means because it's hard, I'm resting — no correction is happening there, just a plain cause and effect.

```jp
難しいから、今日は休む。
```

*Because it's difficult, I'll rest today.*

代わりに is the pattern actually built for swapping — one action standing in for another, not one word standing in for another. Reach for というより when you're fixing how you described something, and 代わりに when you're fixing what actually happened.

```jp
休む代わりに、部屋を掃除した。
```

*Instead of resting, I cleaned the room.*

Put side by side, the difference is easy to feel: 休む代わりに means resting got replaced by something else entirely — cleaning happened, not resting. If you swapped in というより there instead, you would need two descriptions of the same single event, which resting and cleaning simply are not. The pattern you pick tells the listener what kind of correction they're getting — a fact about the world, or a better choice of words.

というより never argues that the first word was wrong — only that the second one is closer. Later, として will pick up a different job entirely, handing a noun a role to stand in rather than correcting anything about it. Keep them separate in your head from the start, and you won't be tempted to reach for というより out of habit on a sentence where nothing actually needs revising.
