Your friend messaged twenty minutes ago to say they were getting on the train. The ride takes fifteen minutes. Now someone turns to you and asks where they are. You cannot say 彼は駅にいます — that is a flat claim about the world, and you have not seen anything with your own eyes. But you are not guessing at random either. You have a departure time and a train that takes fifteen minutes, and those two facts point somewhere.

```jp
彼はもう駅にいるはずです。
```

*He should be at the station by now.*

That is the job はず does. You park a whole plain-form clause in front of it and it says: from what I know, this is how things must be. Not a hope, not a shrug — you have grounds, and the grounds are doing the talking.

## How it works

はず is a formal noun — a word shaped like a noun that never stands on its own, existing only so that a clause can be attached in front of it. That single fact explains almost every rule below, so it is worth holding onto.

Two consequences follow. Whatever comes *in front* of はず connects the way it would connect to any ordinary noun. Whatever comes *after* はず is just ordinary noun endings: だ when plain, です when polite, でした or だった in the past.

Verbs are the easy case. A dictionary-form verb attaches to nouns without help, so it attaches to はず without help:

```jp
電車は八時に出るはずです。
```

*The train is supposed to leave at eight.*

Notice there is no guessing in that sentence. It is the timetable speaking through you. This is the natural home of はず: schedules, arrangements, arithmetic, anything where a fact you already have implies a fact you do not have yet.

Tense goes on the verb, not on はず. If the thing you are reasoning about already happened, the verb carries the past and はず stays exactly as it is:

```jp
母は昨日この手紙を出したはずです。
```

*My mother should have sent this letter yesterday.*

English smears the past across the whole phrase there — *should have sent* — which is why learners reach for something like `出すはずでした` when they mean this. Resist it. Past on the verb means the event is expected to have happened; past on はず means something else entirely, and we will get to it in a moment.

Negatives behave the same way. Negate the clause, leave はず alone:

```jp
彼女は今日の会議に来ないはずだ。
```

*She is not due to come to today's meeting.*

い-adjectives also attach bare, since they already modify nouns with no connector:

```jp
この店のコーヒーは安いはずです。
```

*The coffee at this shop should be cheap.*

Now the two forms that trip everyone up in the first week — and it really is everyone, so do not take it personally. な-adjectives keep their な. It is the same な you already use in 静かな部屋; はず is noun-shaped, so it wants the same treatment a noun would get:

```jp
夜の図書館は静かなはずだ。
```

*The library at night should be quiet.*

And a plain noun needs の to hook onto はず, exactly as one noun needs の to modify another:

```jp
あの人は医者のはずです。
```

*That person should be a doctor.*

> `静かはず` and `医者はず` are the two most common wrong guesses in this pattern. If you can remember that はず is a noun in disguise, you can rebuild both rules from scratch instead of memorising them.

Because the ending is ordinary, you can put はず itself into the past — and that is where the meaning shifts:

```jp
私たちは先週旅行するはずでした。
```

*We were supposed to travel last week.*

> Past-tense はず almost always means the expectation *failed*. はずでした and its plain twin はずだった say: this was the arrangement, and it did not happen. A Japanese speaker hearing 旅行するはずでした will immediately want to know what went wrong.

## Rules and exceptions

The first sharp edge is the English word *should* itself. In English it does two unrelated jobs: it calculates (*the train should be here by now*) and it instructs (*you should call your mother*). はず only ever does the first one. It has nothing to say about duty, advice, or what a decent person would do. If you tell a friend 電話するはずです, you have not urged them to call — you have announced that, as far as you understand the schedule, a call is going to occur.

The second edge follows from the first. Be careful using はず about your own actions. 私は明日行くはずです is not wrong, but it means your going is fixed by something outside you: a booking, a rota, somebody else's plan. If *you* are the one who decided, say 行きます. Using はず about your own free choice makes you sound strangely detached from your own life, like you are reading your day off a printout.

Third: はず needs grounds. There has to be a reason sitting behind the sentence, even an unspoken one. If you have no reason and you are simply guessing, 多分 is the honest word. And the two are not rivals — 多分 is an adverb, so it can sit inside a はず sentence when your grounds are real but thin.

Fourth, a small mercy: politeness lives entirely on the ending. はずだ is plain, はずです is polite, and nothing about the clause in front has to change. You can build the whole sentence casually and then decide at the last syllable how formal you are being.

## Casual speech

In relaxed speech the だ falls off and はず ends the sentence bare. This is extremely common — arguably more common than the full form when you are talking to a friend:

```jp
鍵はかばんの中にあるはず。
```

*The key should be in the bag.*

Said with a slight rise, that bare はず carries a faint *I think, anyway* — the speaker is inviting you to check. Adding よ (あるはずだよ) pushes the other way and sounds more certain, like you are settling a small argument. でしょ in place of だ (あるはずでしょ) drags the listener in and asks them to agree with you.

All three are the same grammar with different social weather. The clause in front never moves.

## Contrast

The closest thing you already know is ようになる, and holding the two side by side sharpens both. ようになる reports a change you have actually observed landing — it asserts that the world is now this way:

```jp
妹は日本語が話せるようになった。
```

*My little sister has got to the point where she can speak Japanese.*

Say that and you are claiming to have heard her do it. Swap in はず and you get 妹は日本語が話せるはずだ, which is a different speech act altogether: you have not heard her, but you know she has been studying for two years, so you are computing the result. ようになる is a report. はず is an inference.

なくなる splits the same way. 彼はもうお酒を飲まなくなった says the drinking stopped and you watched it stop. 彼はもうお酒を飲まないはずだ says you have some reason to believe he does not drink any more — his doctor, something he told you last month — but you are reconstructing, not reporting. Both sentences can be true at once; only one of them is a claim you would defend if challenged.

The test is simple. If someone said *how do you know?*, could you answer *I saw it*? Then you do not want はず. If your answer would be *well, because…* followed by a reason, はず is the word.

So: はず is not a soft guess and not a piece of advice. It is you doing arithmetic out loud on the facts you already hold, with な after な-adjectives and の after nouns, and with the past tense meaning very different things depending on whether it lands on the verb or on はず itself. Hold onto that last part — when we meet はずがない shortly, you will find it does not merely negate this pattern, it flips it into something much more forceful.
