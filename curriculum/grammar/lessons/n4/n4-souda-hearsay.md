Your friend texts you from the other side of town: the new shop by the station is cheap, the food is good, go. You have never set foot in it. The next day someone asks you where to eat, and here is the problem. If you say あの新しい店は安いです, you are speaking as though you walked in and checked the prices yourself. You did not. You are relaying somebody else's report, and Japanese would like you to say so out loud.

That is the whole job of そうだ. You build the sentence exactly the way you would say it plainly, and then you park そうだ on the end.

```jp
あの新しい店は安いそうです。
```

*I hear that new shop is cheap.*

One small ending, and the sentence changes owner. The content is unchanged — cheap shop, still cheap — but you have stepped back from it and handed the responsibility to whoever told you. Adults spend a startling amount of conversation on things they did not personally witness, so this ending earns its keep fast.

## How it works

The rule fits on one line: **plain form + そうだ**. The plain form is the dictionary-style form, the one with no です or ます hanging off it. It carries all the tense and all the negation. そうだ sits behind it and never moves.

Verbs are the easy case. Take the plain form, whatever it is, and clip そうだ onto the back.

```jp
兄は来年日本に行くそうです。
```

*I hear my older brother is going to Japan next year.*

Notice that nothing about 行く changed. There is no special "hearsay form" to learn — you already know how to make the plain forms, so you already know how to do this. Past and negative go on the verb, never on そうだ. If the report is about something that did not happen, the verb goes negative and そうだ carries on exactly as before.

```jp
彼は昨日の会議に来なかったそうだ。
```

*Apparently he didn't come to yesterday's meeting.*

い-adjectives behave the same way, because an い-adjective's plain form can already finish a sentence by itself. 寒い is a complete plain sentence, so 寒いそうです is a complete report.

```jp
今年の冬はとても寒いそうです。
```

*I hear this winter is going to be very cold.*

Now the part that trips people up in week one. な-adjectives and nouns cannot end a plain sentence on their own — they need だ, the plain version of です (the word that works like *is/am/are*). That だ is part of the plain form, so it does not disappear. It stays, and そうだ goes behind it.

```jp
静かだ → 静かだそうだ
```

*it is quiet → I hear it is quiet*

```jp
あの町は夜とても静かだそうです。
```

*I hear that town is very quiet at night.*

Nouns work identically: noun, then だ, then そうだ. If you can say 医者だ as a plain sentence, you can say 医者だそうだ as a report of one.

```jp
彼女のお父さんは医者だそうです。
```

*I hear her father is a doctor.*

And when the report is about the past, the noun's plain past form だった does the work, with そうだ unchanged behind it as always.

```jp
あの人は去年まで学生だったそうです。
```

*I hear that person was a student until last year.*

So the system is: build the sentence plainly, get the tense and the negation right there in front, then attach そうだ. Four shapes, one rule, and three of the four need no thought at all.

## Rules and exceptions

そうだ is a dead end, grammatically speaking. Once you attach it, the sentence is over. It does not take a past form, it does not take a negative form, and it does not politely agree with anything around it.

> そうだ itself never conjugates. `寒いそうでした`, `寒いそうだった` and `寒いそうじゃない` are all wrong, no matter what the sentence is doing.

The reason is worth holding onto, because it makes the rule stop feeling arbitrary. そうだ is not a claim about the world; it is a label stuck on the outside of a sentence saying where that sentence came from. A label has no tense. So if the cold weather is already over, that fact belongs to the adjective, not to the label: 寒かったそうです. And if the report is that something is *not* the case, the negation belongs to the verb or adjective in front: 来なかったそうです.

The one thing that does change is politeness, and only in one place. そうだ is the plain version, そうです the polite one — that is the entire politeness system for this pattern. What you must not do is push the politeness forward into the sentence itself.

> Never attach そうだ to a ます form. `行きますそうです` is wrong even in the most formal conversation you will ever have; it is 行くそうです, with the plain 行く sitting inside the polite ending.

This surprises people, because everywhere else politeness seems to spread. Here it does not, and that is actually the pattern telling you something true: the sentence in front of そうだ is a quotation of what you were told, and quotations keep their own plain shape.

Two more edges. First, hearsay そうだ does not go into questions. `雨が降るそうですか` is not how you ask someone whether they heard about the rain — to ask, you just ask normally and let them answer with そうです. Second, do not use it for anything you experienced yourself. If you stood in the rain this morning, 雨が降りました is the honest sentence; 雨が降るそうです says plainly that you are working from someone else's information.

It is also worth knowing what そうだ does *not* do. It is not a hedge. It does not signal that you doubt the report or that you think it is unreliable — it is neutral relay, the same tone a news reader uses. If you want to name where you got it, do that in a separate sentence first (友達から聞きました) and then deliver the report. The そうだ sentence itself stays clean.

## Politeness and casual speech

そうだ does not contract, which is unusual — most endings of this size shrink in fast speech. What changes instead is where you meet it. Plain そうだ is completely normal in writing, especially anything news-shaped, and it is what you will see on a page. In polite conversation, そうです is standard and safe.

In casual talk between friends it takes the usual final particles and nothing else: 安いそうだよ, 寒いそうだね. That works, though among close friends そうだ can sound slightly bookish — there are shorter, chattier ways of relaying gossip that you will meet later. Nothing you are learning here becomes wrong; you are just learning the version that is correct everywhere, which is the right order to learn things in.

## Contrast: そうだ against ので and のに

You already know two patterns described with the same three words, *plain form plus*: ので for a neutral reason, のに for "even though". They live in the same slot, they attach to the same forms, and for verbs and い-adjectives all three behave identically. Then you hit a な-adjective or a noun, and they split.

ので and のに take な after a な-adjective or a noun. そうだ takes だ.

```jp
あの町は静かなので、私は好きです。
```

*That town is quiet, so I like it.*

```jp
あの町は静かなのに、私はあまり好きじゃありません。
```

*Even though that town is quiet, I don't like it much.*

Put those next to あの町は夜とても静かだそうです and the difference is one syllable: な for ので and のに, だ for そうだ. This one trips up nearly everyone, and the mistake always runs the same direction — `静かなそうです`, borrowed straight from ので habits. If you catch yourself writing な in front of そう, you have merged two patterns.

There is a deeper split underneath the syllable. ので and のに are joints: they connect two halves of a sentence that you are asserting, and something always follows them. そうだ is a lid: it closes the sentence and tells your listener the whole thing came from elsewhere, and nothing follows it. That is why one of them can carry tense inside a longer sentence and the other cannot — a joint is part of the machinery, a lid is not.

There is a second そうだ coming, and it looks identical from the outside. The difference is entirely in what sits in front of it: hearsay needs a complete plain form, while the other one attaches to a bare stem and talks about how something looks to you. So when you meet a そう in the wild, read backwards, not forwards — the shape in front of it is the whole answer.

Build the sentence plainly, then hang そうだ off the end and leave it alone. That is the pattern.
