Your friend texts you: 週末何する？ (What are you doing this weekend?) You haven't actually decided anything yet — maybe a movie, maybe some shopping, maybe you'll just see how you feel Saturday morning. Naming one single plan would be a lie; you don't have one. Naming nothing feels evasive, like you're dodging the question. What you want is a loose, no-commitment answer: a couple of examples that sketch the shape of your weekend without pinning down exactly what happens. That's the job とか does, and once you have it, this kind of half-decided answer stops feeling awkward to give — you're not lying about your plans, you're just being honest that they aren't plans yet.

```jp
週末は映画とか買い物とかしたいです。
```

*I want to do things like watch a movie or go shopping this weekend.*

とか rides along right after a noun and says, in effect, *this, or something like it*. Say it once and you've named one example from a bigger, unnamed set. Say it twice and you've named two, with the same understanding: there might be more you're just not bothering to list. Neither example is "the plan" — they're both stand-ins for a whole category of things you might feel like doing.

## How it works

Structurally, とか is about as simple as N4 grammar gets: Noun + とか, and if you're naming a second example, Noun + とか again. It attaches straight to the noun — no だ, no です, no conjugation to worry about — and then the rest of the sentence carries its own politeness level as normal, completely independent of とか itself. Whatever verb or adjective follows behaves exactly as it would if とか weren't there at all.

There's no fixed ceiling on how many nouns you can chain this way. Three or four, each followed by its own とか, works by the same rule as two — though past two or three, a list starts to feel less like a casual toss-off and more like you're reciting a full itinerary, which undercuts the whole point of reaching for とか in the first place.

```jp
映画とか買い物したいです。
```

*I want to do stuff like watch a movie, maybe go shopping.*

You can drop the second とか once the listener already gets the idea, the way this sentence does — 買い物 just sits there as a plain second item, and nobody blinks. The full doubled shape is the default you reach for, especially the first time you introduce a list, but it isn't mandatory once the pattern is established in the conversation.

> A single noun + とか is already a complete, valid listing on its own — it doesn't need a partner. It just means "this, and other things in the same category," with the rest left comfortably unsaid.

```jp
週末はテレビとか見ます。
```

*This weekend I'll watch stuff like TV, among other things.*

Notice テレビとか never gets a second とか here — there's no second named example, just an implied "and whatever else." That's the hedge doing its job with a single item, and it's a shape learners tend to under-use, reaching for a full two-noun list even when one example plus とか already says everything they mean.

Because とか attaches to a noun rather than a whole sentence, the resulting noun-plus-とか group behaves exactly like any other noun afterward. It can take が as a subject, を as an object, は as a topic — whatever the sentence needs, the same way any plain noun would.

```jp
果物とか野菜とかが体にいいです。
```

*Things like fruit and vegetables are good for your body.*

果物とか野菜とか as a whole becomes the subject here, then が marks it as usual. The listener understands you don't mean only those two foods — you mean the whole neighborhood of healthy things they stand in for. Try swapping が for を and the same logic carries straight over: 果物とか野菜とかを食べます would mean eating things in that same loose category, not just those two items on a plate.

## Rules and exceptions

とか is unmistakably casual. It's the register of texting a friend or chatting over lunch, not the register of a report, a job interview, or answering a doctor precisely about what hurts.

> In careful or formal speech, とか's vagueness reads as sloppy rather than friendly. If you actually need to be exact — a doctor asking what symptom you have, a boss asking what's on today's agenda — name the thing plainly and leave とか out entirely.

The other common trap is mixing up とか with か何か, which you already know. か何か gestures at one item you can't quite name or don't care to specify — "a movie or something." とか, on the other hand, lays out real, concrete examples of a category, even while leaving the exact edges of that category open. The two aren't interchangeable, even though both sound hedgy and both feel casual.

```jp
明日は映画か何か見ませんか。
```

*Want to watch a movie or something tomorrow?*

```jp
明日は映画とかテレビとか見ませんか。
```

*Want to watch stuff like a movie or TV tomorrow?*

The first sentence is one blurry idea — you haven't even decided it's a movie, could be anything. The second hands over two concrete examples standing in for a bigger, unnamed set of options. Same invitation, same politeness level, very different amount of information given to the listener. If someone answers the second question with "どっち？" (which one?), that's a fair response — you handed them two real options to choose between, not one vague placeholder.

One more thing worth knowing: とか is built for nouns, not for handing off someone else's exact words. If you're quoting what a friend said, casually, that's って's job, not とか's — don't reach for とか just because both patterns feel relaxed and conversational. They solve different problems that happen to share the same casual register.

In speech, a noun plus とか can even be the whole answer by itself. Asked 何が食べたい？ (What do you feel like eating?), you can just say 肉とか。(Something like meat, I guess.) and trail off — the noun-plus-とか group already carries a complete thought, so the rest of the sentence is genuinely optional in casual talk, not just shortened.

## Contrast

You've also met そういう for pointing back at "that kind of" thing once a category already exists in the conversation. とか is how you build that category out loud in the first place, by naming a few of its members; そういう is how you refer back to the whole category once it's already on the table. One creates, the other recalls.

```jp
そういう料理が好きです。
```

*I like that kind of cooking.*

```jp
寿司とかラーメンとか、そういう料理が好きです。
```

*I like that kind of food — sushi, ramen, things along those lines.*

Name the examples with とか first, then wrap them up with そういう, and you've done in one breath what would otherwise take a whole extra sentence: given a couple of concrete anchors, then pointed at the entire category they represent. Learners who skip straight to そういう without ever having named anything leave the listener wondering what category they even mean — そういう has nothing to point back at yet.

The two patterns also differ in how heavy they feel. とか is light — you can toss out one or two examples mid-sentence without slowing down. そういう carries a little more weight; it asks the listener to hold an entire category in mind at once, which is exactly why it works best after とか has already done the work of sketching that category out with real examples.

とか's whole job is staying loose — a couple of named examples standing in for a category you don't feel like spelling out completely. Hold onto that habit of trailing off after a short list; it's exactly the kind of half-settled sentence that ところが later loves to interrupt, flipping something you thought was decided into something unexpected. For now, the win is smaller and more immediate: you can finally answer 週末何する？ honestly, without pretending you've already decided.
