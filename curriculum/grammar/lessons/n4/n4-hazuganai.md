Your friend swears he already finished his homework. You know better — you were sitting right next to him for the last two hours, and he never once put down the remote. He watched TV, laughed at something on screen, got up for a snack, came back, and sat right back down. Not once did he open his backpack, let alone take out a pencil.

So when he tells you the homework is done, you don't quietly doubt him or ask a polite follow-up question. You don't need to ask him to prove it, either — you already have all the proof you need, sitting right there in what you watched happen. You say it flat out, because you were there and you know what you saw.

```jp
彼が宿題をしたはずがない。
```

*There's no way he did his homework.*

This is はずがない — *there's no way / can't be*. It doesn't hedge the way *maybe he didn't* would, and it isn't the same as a plain *he didn't*. It rules the thing out entirely, as a conclusion, and it can only do that because you have something concrete standing behind it: two hours of watching him do everything except homework.

You already know はず for the opposite job — something you're confident will turn out to be true. 彼が来るはずだ, *he should be coming*, says you expect something to happen based on what you know. はずがない takes that same word and closes the door on it completely: はず (the case that should hold) plus が plus ない (doesn't exist) — literally *there is no case in which that's true*. Put the pieces together and you get the strongest, flattest way Japanese has to say something is impossible. And exactly like はず, it never floats free — there's always a reason behind it, even when the speaker doesn't bother to say it out loud.

## How it works

はずがない attaches to a plain-form predicate the exact same way はず does. That's good news: you're not learning a new attachment rule from scratch, you're just adding が plus ない to the end of a shape you already build correctly.

A plain verb — dictionary form, ta-form, whatever tense the sentence needs — slots straight in, no extra piece required.

```jp
彼が今日休むはずがない。
```

*There's no way he's taking today off.*

He's got an exam this afternoon — 試験があるから — and skipping the one day that actually matters simply isn't something he'd do. The reasoning is doing all the work here; the sentence itself is just the conclusion.

An i-adjective works the same way: plain form, nothing added before はずがない, same as any plain-form sentence you'd already build with it.

```jp
この時計が安いはずがない。
```

*There's no way this watch is cheap.*

You know that one because he bought it at a well-known shop — 有名な店で買ったから — and shops like that don't sell cheap watches. Notice that nothing about 安い changes; it stays exactly as it would in any other plain sentence.

A na-adjective needs its な first, exactly like it does in front of any noun — that's the one place はずがない asks for something extra, and it's the same extra piece はず already asks for.

```jp
この家が便利なはずがない。
```

*There's no way this house is convenient.*

The reason is sitting right there in the neighborhood — it's far from the station, 駅から遠いから — so *convenient* doesn't add up, whatever the listing says.

And a noun takes の, the same の that connects any two nouns in a row.

```jp
彼が学生のはずがない。
```

*There's no way he's a student.*

He's already got a job — もう会社員だから — so *student* doesn't fit anymore, whatever his backpack might suggest.

Four different word types, one shared ending, one job every time: taking something someone claims and flatly ruling it out because the evidence you're holding says it can't be true.

## Rules and exceptions

はずがない isn't for ordinary doubt. If you just personally don't buy something, plain negation or 〜と思わない carries that weight fine. はずがない is reserved for things you can actually rule out — something you watched happen, a fact you know for certain, a conclusion you can walk someone else through step by step. Say it about a hunch with nothing behind it, and it comes out sounding like you're slamming your hand on the table over something that doesn't deserve it. A listener's next move, fairly, is to ask what makes you so sure — and if the honest answer is nothing, はずがない was the wrong tool from the start.

It's also a stronger, different claim than just negating the verb. 宿題をしていない simply reports a fact — he hasn't done the homework, full stop, no reasoning attached. 宿題をしたはずがない makes a claim about reality itself: that the very idea of him having done it doesn't hold up, before anyone even opens the backpack to check. One is an observation; the other is an argument.

> Watch what happens when the thing being denied is already negative. `ないはずがない` — a ない-form verb or adjective plus はずがない — doesn't mean *there's no way that's not true* as a passing, throwaway remark. It flips all the way around into *there's absolutely no way it isn't true*, which lands as *it definitely must be true*.

```jp
彼が分からないはずがない。
```

*There's no way he doesn't understand.*

Read that as *he must understand*, not as some weaker cousin of *he doesn't understand*. Two negatives here don't cancel into something neutral — they cancel into a strong positive claim, which is exactly the trap this pattern sets for anyone translating word by word.

はずがありません is simply the polite dress for the same sentence — same certainty, same reasoning underneath, just appropriate for a teacher, a coworker, or a stranger instead of a friend on the couch next to you.

```jp
彼が宿題をしたはずがありません。
```

*There's no way he did his homework.*

## Casual speech

In fast everyday speech, people commonly drop the が entirely.

```jp
そんなはずがない → そんなはずない
```

Both versions mean exactly the same thing — *there's no way that's true* — but そんなはずない is what comes out when someone's reacting on the spot, not writing something down. You'll hear it a lot; you just won't see it much in anything formal. Keep the full はずがない for writing and for polite conversation, and recognize はずない the instant someone blurts it out at you.

## Contrast

Line はず and はずがない up next to each other and the relationship is obvious — same core word, opposite conclusion, depending on nothing but which ending you pick.

```jp
彼が来るはずだ。
```

*He should be coming.*

```jp
彼が来るはずがない。
```

*There's no way he's coming.*

はずだ commits to *this should be true*, based on what you know. はずがない commits to *this cannot be true*, based on the exact same kind of knowledge pointed the other direction. Swap one for the other in the same sentence and you don't get a milder version of the same idea — you get the opposite claim, which is exactly why mixing them up in conversation gets noticed fast. There's no gentle middle setting on either of these — you don't reach for はず or はずがない when you're only leaning toward an answer. For that softer, more hedged territory, you'll want a different tool: らしい, built for exactly the situation where the evidence is a hint rather than a certainty, is coming up soon. For now, hold onto the gap between *should* and *can't* — it's the whole shape of this pattern.
