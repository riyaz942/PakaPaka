Your friend asks how the new restaurant near the station was, and one compliment doesn't cover it — the place was cheap, and it was good. English glues those together with "not only... but also." Japanese has its own glue for exactly this: だけでなく.

```jp
このレストランは安いだけでなく、おいしいです。
```

*This restaurant isn't only cheap — it's delicious too.*

だけでなく builds on だけ (only), a word you already use to shut other options out. Flip that shutting-out inside out with でなく (でない, "is not," wearing its て-form) and だけ stops closing a door and starts opening one: not just this — this, and something else too.

## How it works

だけでなく always needs two things: the plain form of whatever comes right before it, and something after it — usually marked with も or でも — that names the second item. Drop either half and the sentence stops going anywhere.

After a verb or an い-adjective, だけでなく attaches straight onto the plain form, no extra piece needed.

```jp
日本語は難しいだけでなく、面白い。
```

*Japanese isn't only difficult — it's interesting too.*

Nouns and な-adjectives need one extra piece first: な, the same connector they use right before a noun. Say the plain sentence to yourself first (先生だ, 親切だ), then swap だ for な before だけでなく.

```jp
彼は先生だけでなく、医者でもあります。
```

*He isn't only a teacher — he's also a doctor.*

```jp
彼は親切なだけでなく、有名だ。
```

*He isn't only kind — he's also famous.*

> Skip the な after a noun or な-adjective and you get `先生だだけでなく` or `親切だだけでなく` — both sound like a stutter, not a sentence. だ never survives directly in front of だけでなく.

Verbs behave exactly like い-adjectives here — plain form, straight in, no な involved.

```jp
彼女は日本語を話すだけでなく、英語も話す。
```

*She doesn't only speak Japanese — she speaks English too.*

## Rules and exceptions

The second half of the sentence carries half the meaning, not decoration. だけでなく promises a second item is coming, and も or でも is what actually delivers it — leave that marker off and the sentence sounds like it forgot where it was going.

> This one trips people up the first week: だけでなく feels complete on its own because the first half is already a full sentence. It never is — always finish the thought.

## Casual speech

In relaxed conversation でなく often shrinks to じゃなく, the same shift you've already seen in ではない → じゃない. Nothing about the meaning changes — it just sounds like something a friend would actually say over lunch, not something read off a menu.

```jp
高いだけじゃなく、まずい。
```

*It's not only expensive — it's bad-tasting too.*

## Contrast with ほど

You already have ほど for pushing one fact to its extreme — 死ぬほど忙しい takes a single situation and turns it up as far as it will go. だけでなく isn't turning anything up; it's setting a second, separate fact down next to the first one.

```jp
今日は忙しいだけでなく、宿題も多い。
```

*Today isn't only busy — there's a lot of homework too.*

```jp
今日は死ぬほど忙しい。
```

*Today I'm busy to the point of dying.*

Line them up and the split is clear: the ほど sentence has one fact cranked to its limit; the だけでなく sentence has two separate facts standing side by side.

Once "not only X" feels automatic, keep に限らず in your peripheral vision — it does something similar but reaches wider, saying a whole category isn't limited to just one example. Until then, だけでなく is the one to reach for whenever a single compliment, or complaint, doesn't cover it.
