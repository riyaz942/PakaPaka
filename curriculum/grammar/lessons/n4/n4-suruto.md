You open the window to let some air in, and the second your hand touches the frame you notice it — rain, coming down steady, and you had no idea it had even started. In Japanese, the connector that carries you from the first action straight into the surprise that followed it is すると.

```jp
窓を開けた。すると雨が降っていた。
```

*I opened the window. Then it was raining.*

すると sits at the very front of the second sentence, right after a full stop. It never changes shape — no conjugation, no politeness form, nothing to memorize beyond the word itself. That's the whole mechanic: one complete sentence, a period, すると, another complete sentence. The first sentence is the trigger — something you did, or something that was simply true — and the すると sentence reports what showed up right afterward, almost like you're narrating a scene as it unfolds in front of you.

## How it works

The first sentence can be in plain past tense, describing a single thing that actually happened once. That's the most common shape you'll meet: you did something, and すると hands you straight into what you found.

```jp
家に帰った。すると猫が机の上で寝ていた。
```

*I went home. Then the cat was sleeping on the desk.*

```jp
猫の名前を呼んだ。すると猫が起きた。
```

*I called the cat's name. Then the cat woke up.*

Notice who's doing what in each half. You call the cat's name — that's your action. The cat waking up is not your action; it's what happened as a direct, practically automatic consequence of yours. That's the feel すると carries: the second half is something you observed or discovered, not something you decided to do.

There's a second use of the same word, and it's easy to miss the first time you meet it. If the first sentence is in plain non-past form instead of past, すると describes a pattern that holds every time, not a one-off event.

```jp
毎朝、電気をつける。すると猫が起きる。
```

*Every morning I turn on the light. Then the cat wakes up.*

Same connector, same two-sentence shape — the only thing that shifted is the tense of the first half, from つけた (turned on, once) to つける (turn on, every time). That tense shift is what tells the listener whether you're recounting a specific morning or describing a routine.

## Rules and exceptions

The part that trips people up is what すると is *not* allowed to introduce. The second sentence has to be something that happens on its own — a fact, a discovery, someone or something else's reaction. It cannot be your own decision about what to do next.

```jp
友達を呼んだ。すると友達が来た。
```

*I called my friend. Then my friend came.*

That works because "my friend came" is their action, reported as a consequence — not a plan you're announcing. Compare that to something like `友達を呼んだ。すると友達と話しましょう。` — trying to use すると to lead into your own proposal ("let's talk") is wrong. The moment the second half is a request, an invitation, an order, or a statement of intent, すると can't carry it; that job belongs to a different connector entirely.

> すると almost always reports something that has already happened or that reliably happens — treat it as a narrator's word, not a planner's word. If you catch yourself about to write a volitional form (〜ましょう, 〜よう) or a request after すると, stop and pick another connector.

The other trap is reaching for すると when the second sentence actually contradicts what you'd expect from the first. すると is neutral — mildly surprising at most, never a reversal. A genuine reversal needs a different word, which is exactly what the contrast section below is for.

## Contrast

You've already met そこで, for when the second sentence is a deliberate response you chose to make, and ところが, for when the second sentence contradicts what the first one set up. Line all three up against the same opening sentence and the differences become obvious.

```jp
友達を呼んだ。そこで友達と話した。
```

*I called my friend. So I talked with my friend.*

Here the second half is something you decided to do next, on purpose — that's そこで's territory. Swap in すると and it reads as though the talking just happened to you, which isn't the sense you want when you're describing your own choice.

```jp
友達を呼んだ。ところが友達は来なかった。
```

*I called my friend. However, my friend didn't come.*

Here the second half undercuts the first — you called, and yet nothing came of it. That gap between expectation and reality is what ところが is built for, and it's exactly the gap すると refuses to carry. すると's version of this scene simply has the friend showing up, no surprise, no letdown — the plainest possible next beat in the story.

So the three sit in a row: すると narrates the next thing that happened, そこで narrates the next thing you chose to do, ところが narrates the next thing that shouldn't have happened but did.

Worth holding onto for later: 反対に flips a situation the opposite way rather than simply continuing it forward, which is a slightly different move than any of these three — more on that when we get there.

For now, the shape to remember is small: one finished sentence, すると, one more sentence reporting what followed. Read a few lines of any story written in Japanese and you'll start noticing it everywhere — it's one of the plainest ways native writers move a scene forward one beat at a time.
