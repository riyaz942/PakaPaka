You're wrapping up at the office, and a friend texts to ask if you want to hang out. You want to tell them you'll call them after work. You already know how to say "work" and "call", but you need the glue to stick them together.

In Japanese, to say "after doing X," you use 後で. It literally translates to "at the later (time)."

```jp
仕事の後で、電話します。
```
*After work, I will call.*

## How it works

If you want to place a noun on the timeline, you just connect it to 後で with the particle の.

```jp
授業の後で、映画を見ます。
```
*After class, I will watch a movie.*

But when you want to say "after *doing* something," you have to attach a verb. Here is the golden rule: the verb before 後で must always be in its plain past form (the た-form).

```jp
ご飯を食べた後で、お茶を飲みます。
```
*After eating a meal, I drink tea.*

Why the past tense? Because on your mental timeline, the eating has to be completely finished before the tea drinking can begin. You're saying "in the aftermath of having eaten." Do not use the dictionary form here — saying `食べる後で` is grammatically incorrect.

## Rules and exceptions

The most common trap here is overthinking the tense of your sentence. Remember that the verb before 後で is *always* locked into the past tense (た-form), even if the whole sentence is talking about your plans for tomorrow.

> The tense of the actual event is controlled entirely by the very last verb in the sentence.

If you did something yesterday, the ending verb is in the past tense. But the verb glued to 後で stays exactly the same.

```jp
本を読んだ後で、寝ました。
```
*After I read a book, I went to sleep.*

## Casual speech

You will very often see this written entirely in hiragana as あとで. Both are perfectly normal, though the kanji is slightly more common in formal writing.

In casual speech, people sometimes drop the で entirely and just say あと. If you're talking to a friend, `食べたあと` (after I eat) sounds a bit more relaxed than `食べた後で`, but keeping the で is the safest habit to build right now.

## Wait, what about てから?

You already know `てから`, which also means "after doing." They feel similar, but they have a slightly different flavor.

`てから` is a tight, direct sequence. It implies "A finishes, and then B happens as the direct next step." `後で` is a looser timeline. It just means B happens at *some point* after A.

There's also a structural difference. You can use nouns directly with `後で` (like `仕事の後で`), but you cannot do that with `てから`.

And what about `前に` (before)? They work as exact opposites, but notice the verb forms. Since "before" means the action hasn't happened yet, `前に` takes the dictionary form. Since "after" means the action is finished, `後で` takes the た-form.

```jp
映画を見る前に、ご飯を食べます。
```
*Before watching a movie, I will eat a meal.*

```jp
映画を見た後で、ご飯を食べます。
```
*After watching a movie, I will eat a meal.*

Hold onto this sense of a mental timeline. Right now we're looking at things that happen clearly before or after each other. Later, at N4, we'll look at how to describe things happening at the exact same time when we meet `ながら`.
