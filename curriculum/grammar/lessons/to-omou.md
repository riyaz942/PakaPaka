You are walking out of a cinema with a friend. They turn to you and ask what you thought of the film. You want to say "It was interesting," but stating it as a universal fact feels a little too heavy. You want to soften it to "*I think* it was interesting."

To express your opinion or make a prediction, Japanese uses the verb 思う (to think) paired with the quoting particle と. It literally works like "I think quote: [my thought]."

```jp
あの映画は面白いと思います。
```
*I think that movie is interesting.*

## How it works

You take a complete, casual sentence (the "plain form") and attach と思う. If you want to be polite to the person you are talking to, you make the final verb polite: と思います. But the thought *inside* the quote always stays casual.

```jp
彼は今日来ないと思います。
```
*I think he will not come today.*

There is one major trap here. If your thought ends in an i-adjective or a verb, you just attach と思う directly. But if it ends in a noun or a na-adjective, you have a missing piece.

In casual Japanese, nouns and na-adjectives need the casual "is" word だ to form a complete sentence. If you forget to include だ before と思う, the sentence falls apart.

```jp
この店は静かだと思います。
```
*I think this shop is quiet.*

```jp
明日は雨だと思います。
```
*I think it will rain tomorrow.*

## Rules and exceptions

The biggest mistake beginners make is making the inside of the quote polite. The quote must always be plain form. Never say `行きますと思います` — it sounds like "I think: I will politely go." Always use the plain form inside, and save the politeness for the very end of the sentence.

> **Mind reading is not allowed:** You can only use と思う for your *own* thoughts. If you want to say "He thinks," you need a different grammar pattern. Japanese grammar strictly separates what you feel from what you guess others are feeling.

When you want to ask someone else's opinion, you just ask them what they think.

```jp
これ、どう思いますか。
```
*What do you think about this?*

## Casual speech

When you are talking to friends, you drop the polite ます and just end the sentence with 思う. Everything inside the quote stays exactly the same.

```jp
この本は面白いと思う。
```
*I think this book is interesting.*

## Contrast

You already know だろう for guessing "probably." The difference between the two is who owns the guess.

```jp
明日は雪だろう。
```
*It will probably snow tomorrow.*

```jp
明日は雪だと思います。
```
*I think it will snow tomorrow.*

だろう states the guess on its own, like a forecast with no name on it. と思う marks it as your own opinion or prediction, one that you stand behind.

Hold onto this quoting particle と. It works exactly the same way when we learn how to talk about things "called" or "named" a certain way using という later on.
