You've got your weekend plans sorted. You know what you want to do and what you want to eat. But if a friend asks exactly where all this is happening, or how you plan to get there, you're going to need the particle で.

で attaches directly to the end of a noun. It operates like a tag that tells your listener about the background details of your action.

## Setting the stage

When you do an active verb—like reading, eating, or watching a movie—you need a stage for that action to happen on. You mark that location with で. Think of it as answering "where is this event taking place?"

```jp
週末、図書館で本を読む。
```
*On the weekend, I read books at the library.*

```jp
家で映画を見る。
```
*I watch movies at home.*

```jp
これ、どこで買う？
```
*Where do you buy this?*

## Marking the tool

The second job of で is marking the means or method of an action. It tells us what tool you used to get the job done. In English, this usually translates to "by" or "with".

```jp
バスで学校に行く。
```
*I go to school by bus.*

```jp
箸で魚を食べる。
```
*I eat fish with chopsticks.*

## The "at" trap: で vs. に

This one trips everyone up the first week—that's completely normal. In English, we use "at" or "in" for almost everything location-related. Japanese splits the job between で and the particle に you learned earlier.

に acts like a dart hitting a dartboard. It marks a destination you are moving toward, but it doesn't describe a stage where a full activity takes place. If you are traveling toward a place, use に (or へ). If you are actively doing something once you get there, use で.

```jp
公園に行く。
```
*I go to the park.*

```jp
公園で遊ぶ。
```
*I play at the park.*

> Be careful: if you try to say `公園に遊ぶ`, a Japanese speaker will be very confused. You can't "play toward" a park!

So で handles the "where" and the "by what means". But what if you want to say you watched a movie *with* a friend? Don't use で here. Tools take で, but companions take a different particle entirely. Hold onto that thought—it becomes important when we meet と later.
