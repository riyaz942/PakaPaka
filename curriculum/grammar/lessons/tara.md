You and a friend have tomorrow planned out: walk to the park, find a bench, eat the lunch you packed the night before. Then you look up. The sky is the colour of wet paper. Your friend wants to know what happens to the plan in that case, and you need a sentence that hangs the whole afternoon on something that has not happened yet.

```jp
明日雨が降ったら、公園に行きません。
```

*If it rains tomorrow, we're not going to the park.*

Nothing has rained. Nothing has been cancelled. All you have done is build a fork in the road: one branch has rain on it, and on that branch there is no park. Setting up that fork is the entire job of たら.

## How it works

The recipe is short enough to feel like a trick. Take the plain past form of a word — the one ending in た — and stick ら on the end. That is the whole pattern.

```jp
食べる → 食べた → 食べたら
飲む → 飲んだ → 飲んだら
```

*eat → ate → if/when (someone) eats*
*drink → drank → if/when (someone) drinks*

Look at the second one for a second. 飲む does not become `飲みたら`; its past tense is 飲んだ, so the conditional — the form that means *supposing this happens* — is 飲んだら. Same story with 泳ぐ → 泳いだ → 泳いだら. That is where the だら you sometimes see listed as a separate form comes from. It is not a second rule to memorise; it is the same ら glued onto a past form that already ended in だ. Get the past tense right and だら takes care of itself.

The irregulars stay boring, which is a genuine relief: する → した → したら, and 来る → 来た → 来たら. No surprises, no extra sounds.

There is a reason this pattern is built on the past form rather than the dictionary form, and it is worth two seconds of your attention. The た form in Japanese is not purely about yesterday — it marks an event as **completed**. たら takes that completed event and says *treat this as done, and now what?* Once you hear it that way, the fact that one pattern covers both *if* and *when* stops being strange. Both of them are just "this is finished — next".

### Adjectives and nouns join in

たら is not a verbs-only club. い-adjectives have a past tense of their own, so they take ら exactly the same way: 安い → 安かった → 安かったら.

```jp
この本が安かったら、買います。
```

*If this book is cheap, I'll buy it.*

な-adjectives and plain nouns lean on だった instead: 暇 → 暇だった → 暇だったら, and 雨 → 雨だった → 雨だったら. Nothing new to learn there either — if you can make the past tense of a noun sentence, you can make its conditional.

```jp
明日暇だったら、一緒に映画を見ませんか。
```

*If you're free tomorrow, shall we watch a movie together?*

Notice what the second half of that one is doing. It is not a prediction — it is an invitation. たら is perfectly comfortable with a request, an invitation, or a suggestion after the comma. The fork is allowed to end in *let's*, not only in *this will happen*. Hold onto that; it turns out to be one of the main things that separates たら from the other conditionals waiting for you.

Negatives need no new machinery, because every ない form behaves like an い-adjective: 分からない → 分からなかった → 分からなかったら.

One ordering rule, and it does not bend: the たら clause comes first. The condition is the setup, the second half is the payoff, and Japanese will not let you deliver them in the other order.

### When "if" is really "when"

English forces you to choose between *if* and *when*, and the choice leaks information. *If I get home* sounds like there is some chance you won't. Japanese simply refuses to make you decide — たら covers both, and the listener works out which one you meant from how likely the first half is.

```jp
家に帰ったら、すぐ電話します。
```

*When I get home, I'll call you right away.*

Nobody hearing that thinks getting home is in doubt. The first event is a certainty, so たら reads as *once that's done* — a sequence, not a gamble. Same shape as the rain sentence, same grammar, and the only thing that changed is how sure everyone is about the first clause. This one trips people up in the first week, and the fix is not more grammar: it is noticing that Japanese is leaving something to context that English spells out.

### The past-tense version is a different animal

Put the second half into the past tense and たら stops predicting anything at all. Now it reports. You did the first thing, and the second thing was already sitting there waiting for you.

```jp
冷蔵庫を開けたら、猫がいました。
```

*When I opened the fridge, there was a cat.*

You opened a fridge and met a cat. That mixture — one small ordinary action, and then something nobody ordered — is exactly the flavour of past-tense たら. It is how Japanese says *and then, of all things...*, and it shows up constantly in stories about your own day.

## Rules and exceptions

- Build it from the past form, never the dictionary form. `行くら` and `飲みたら` are the two wrong guesses everyone makes, and both come from skipping the past-tense step in the middle.
- The たら half stays plain, always. Politeness is settled once, at the end of the sentence: 電話します is polite, 帰ったら is not, and the sentence as a whole is polite anyway.
- The たら clause goes first. There is no version of this where you tack the condition on at the end as an afterthought.

> When the second clause is in the past tense, whatever follows たら has to be something that happened *to* you — a discovery, a surprise, a result you did not steer. `冷蔵庫を開けたら、猫を買いました` breaks, because buying is a decision, and you cannot discover your own decision.

> たら is not a machine for translating every English *if*. If the *if* you have in mind is really the reason for something that already happened, you have reached for the wrong tool — that is ので territory, and the two of them are worth seeing side by side.

## Casual speech

たら has no contracted form to learn; it is already about as short as Japanese gets. What it does have is a casual habit — people cut the sentence off right after it and let their voice carry the rest.

```jp
先生に聞いたら？
```

*Why don't you ask the teacher?*

Rising intonation, and the missing second half is something like *wouldn't that sort it out*. It is a suggestion, not a question about the past, and every bit of its force lives in the pitch. Say it to a friend and it sounds helpful. Say it to your own boss and it lands somewhere between blunt and nagging, so keep this one for people at your level or below.

## たら against ので and のに

Here is the comparison that makes たら click. The form 降った — the past of 降る — turns up in all three of these patterns. The only thing that changes is what you hang on the end of it.

```jp
雨が降ったので、公園に行きませんでした。
```

*Because it rained, we didn't go to the park.*

```jp
雨が降ったのに、公園に行きました。
```

*Even though it rained, we went to the park.*

Both of those are reports from the real world. ので says the rain happened and it was the reason we stayed in. のに says the rain happened and we went anyway, which was mildly ridiculous of us. Neither one leaves any doubt about the weather: it rained, full stop.

Now put the very first sentence of this lesson back next to them. The core is the same 降った, but that little ら turns the rain from a fact into a possibility. Nothing has been settled, nobody has stayed home, and the sentence is describing a branch of the future rather than a piece of the past.

So the test is quick. Has it already happened? Then you want ので for the reason, or のに for the contradiction. Has it not happened yet — or has it not happened *so far as you know*? That is where たら lives.

One line to carry away: past form plus ら, and your sentence splits neatly into *this is done* → *then that*. Everything else is just noticing whether the first half is a gamble or a certainty, and trusting context to tell your listener which. Soon you will meet ば and なら, two more ways to say *if* — ば for what is always true, なら for *well, if we're talking about that* — and たら is the one that will carry your conversations comfortably while you sort those two out.
