You are sitting in the doctor's office. The doctor turns to you and needs you to open your mouth so they can check your throat. How do they tell you to do that politely? They use the pattern てください (please do).

When you need someone to perform an action—whether you're ordering at a restaurant, giving directions to a taxi driver, or instructing a student—this is your go-to pattern.

## How it works

To make a polite request, take a verb in its て-form and attach ください. 

The て-form is a special verb shape used for connecting actions. ください is a polite word for "please" or "give me." Put them together, and you turn a plain action into a polite request.

```jp
口を開けてください。
```
*Please open your mouth.*

```jp
ここに名前を書いてください。
```
*Please write your name here.*

```jp
明日、うちに来てください。
```
*Please come to my house tomorrow.*

If the verb's て-form ends in で (like 飲む becoming 飲んで), you simply attach ください to that. The structure works exactly the same way.

```jp
水を飲んでください。
```
*Please drink water.*

## Rules and exceptions

Because ください translates to "please," a lot of learners assume it is the perfect, universally polite way to ask for anything. But Japanese politeness is tricky.

てください is fundamentally a *direction*. You are telling someone to do something. It is polite in its packaging, but it is still a command. 

It is perfect when you have the social right to direct someone (a doctor to a patient, a customer to a shop staff), when giving instructions, or when encouraging someone to do something beneficial for them (like offering food).

> **Careful:** Do not use てください to ask a boss or a superior for a favor. If you say `見てください` (Please look) to your manager, it comes across as bossy. 

## Casual speech

If ください is the polite wrapper, what happens when you are talking to a close friend or family member? You just chop it off entirely.

In casual speech, the request simply ends on the て or で. 

So, instead of the formal `ここでちょっと待ってください。` (Please wait here a little), you would just say `ここでちょっと待って。` It is short, punchy, and completely natural with people you are close to.

```jp
ここでちょっと待ってください。
```
*Please wait here a little.*

## Directing vs. Inviting

Let's look at how てください feels next to a pattern you already know: ませんか (won't you?). They both involve asking someone to do an action, but the feeling is very different.

```jp
一緒に来てください。
```
*Please come with me.*

When you use てください, you are giving an instruction. You expect them to follow your lead. 

```jp
一緒に来ませんか。
```
*Won't you come with me?*

When you use ませんか, you are offering an invitation. You are giving them the option to say yes or no. If you want to hang out with a friend, use ませんか. If you are guiding someone who needs to follow you, use てください.

Getting comfortable with the て-form is a huge milestone. Hold onto it—it becomes incredibly important when we learn how to say you are *allowed* to do something with てもいい very soon.
