You are at a shop looking at a nice bag. You check the price tag, and it has way too many zeros. You need a way to express that it crosses the line from just "expensive" to "too expensive."

When an action or a state goes beyond a reasonable limit, Japanese uses the helper verb すぎる. It attaches right to the end of your existing verbs and adjectives to mean "too much" or "excessively."

## How it works

To use すぎる, you need the stem of your word. For verbs, this is the stem (the base part you normally attach ます to).

```jp
彼は働きすぎる。
```
*He works too much.*

```jp
昨日、パンを食べすぎた。
```
*Yesterday, I ate too much bread.*

For adjectives ending in い, simply drop the final い and attach your new ending. The whole package now behaves just like a regular verb.

```jp
このかばんは高すぎる。
```
*This bag is too expensive.*

```jp
部屋が寒すぎます。
```
*The room is too cold.*

## Rules and exceptions

Because すぎる translates to "too much," it inherently carries a negative feeling. You are saying a boundary was crossed. In English, you might say "this cake is too good!" as a compliment. In Japanese, traditionally, attaching this to a positive word implies a problem.

> If you say someone is 親切すぎる (too kind), it might sound like they are overbearing or naive, rather than it being a pure compliment.

Because すぎる acts just like a regular verb that ends in る, you can conjugate it into polite speech or the past tense. Notice how we used すぎます for politeness and すぎた for the past tense in the examples above.

## Contrast: The most vs. too much

It is easy to mix up saying something has the highest degree of a trait versus saying it has too much of it. If you want to say something is the absolute peak of a category, you already know to use 一番.

```jp
あの店が一番高いです。
```
*That shop is the most expensive.*

But being the most expensive doesn't necessarily mean it is unreasonable. If you want to express that the price is out of bounds, you swap in our new pattern.

```jp
あの店は高すぎます。
```
*That shop is too expensive.*

Hold onto this concept of attaching patterns to verb stems. You will see a similar structural shift when we meet ほうがいい (had better) to give strong advice later on.
