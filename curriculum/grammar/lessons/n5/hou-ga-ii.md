You're at a friend's place and they've been coughing all evening. You want to say, gently, "you should go to the hospital." Not a command, not "you must" — just solid, caring advice. That's exactly the job of ほうがいい.

The pattern means something like *had better* or *should*. You attach it to a verb and you're handing someone a recommendation: this choice is the better one. Here's the hospital line.

```jp
病院に行ったほうがいいですよ。
```

*You should go to the hospital.*

Notice the verb is 行った — the past form — even though nothing has happened yet. That surprises everyone at first. Hold that thought; it's the whole trick of this pattern.

## How it works

There are two shapes, depending on whether you're recommending *doing* something or *not doing* it.

For "you should do X", take the plain past form of the verb (the た-form) and add ほうがいい.

```jp
薬を飲んだほうがいいです。
今日は休んだほうがいいですよ。
```

*You should take medicine.*
*You should rest today.*

Why the past form for future advice? Think of ほう as "the ~ side / the ~ option" (it's the same 方 you'll meet in comparisons). You're literally saying "the *having-taken-medicine* side is good" — you picture the action as already done and judge that outcome as the better one. The past form freezes the action into a completed picture you can weigh.

For "you should *not* do X", use the plain negative — the ない-form — and add ほうがいい. This one stays present, no past trickery.

```jp
そのお酒を飲まないほうがいいです。
今日は泳がないほうがいいですよ。
```

*You'd better not drink that alcohol.*
*You'd better not swim today.*

So the split is clean: **positive advice → past (た) + ほうがいい**, **negative advice → ない + ほうがいい**. That asymmetry looks odd but it's fixed and reliable.

You can make the whole thing more polite by keeping です on the end (ほうがいいです), or leave it off for plain speech (ほうがいい). And you can soften it further by tacking よ on the end, which makes it feel like a friendly heads-up rather than a verdict.

## Rules and exceptions

The biggest trap is reaching for the plain *present* form on the positive side. 行くほうがいい isn't the natural advice pattern — for a specific recommendation to a specific person, you want 行ったほうがいい.

> For concrete, in-the-moment advice ("you should go *now*"), use the past form: 行ったほうがいい. The present form 行くほうがいい drifts toward general comparison ("going is the better option, in general") and sounds weaker as personal advice.

The negative side has no such split — it's always ない, never past.

```jp
たくさん食べないほうがいいですよ。
```

*You'd better not eat a lot.*

One more thing about tone: ほうがいい is genuinely stronger than a bare suggestion. When you say 休んだほうがいい, you're implying there's a real downside to *not* resting. For a lighter "how about resting?" you'd reach for something else entirely (you already know どうですか for that). Save ほうがいい for when you mean it.

## Casual speech

Drop the です and you're already casual: 飲んだほうがいい. In fast speech people often shorten ほう to ほ and swallow the が, so ほうがいい can come out sounding like *ほうがいい* run together — you'll hear it, you don't need to produce it. The safe casual form is simply the plain one with よ:

```jp
早く寝たほうがいいよ。
```

*You'd better go to sleep early.*

That よ is doing a lot of work — it turns a flat statement into "hey, just so you know." Very common when you're looking out for a friend.

## Contrast

The closest thing you already know is かもしれない, *might / maybe*. They feel related but do opposite jobs. かもしれない reports a possibility; ほうがいい pushes a choice.

```jp
明日は雨かもしれません。
傘を持ったほうがいいですよ。
```

*It might rain tomorrow.*
*You'd better take an umbrella.*

See how the first sentence just floats a possibility, and the second one leans in with a recommendation because of it. Swap them and it breaks: you can't advise with かもしれない, and ほうがいい can't merely speculate.

It also brushes up against と思う, *I think that*. と思う is your private opinion; ほうがいい is a recommendation aimed at the listener. "この本はいいと思う" says what *you* feel; "この本を読んだほうがいい" tells the other person what to *do*. Same warmth, different direction.

## Closing

Recap: past (た) form for "you should," ない form for "you'd better not," both capped with ほうがいい — and add です or よ to set the tone. The past-tense-for-future-advice quirk is the one to burn in.

Hold onto the ない side of this pattern — that plain negative form is the same building block you'll need soon when we meet まだ〜ていない, "haven't done it yet."
