You are at a coffee shop waiting for a friend. They text you: "Sorry, the train stopped! How long will you wait?" You want to tell them you will wait for roughly an hour, but you do not want to commit to exactly sixty minutes.

```jp
一時間ぐらい待ちます。
```
*I will wait for about one hour.*

## How it works

To say "about" or "approximately" in Japanese, you take a quantity and attach ぐらい (or くらい) directly to the end of it. It works with money, hours, days, or the number of things.

```jp
この本は千円くらいです。
```
*This book is about one thousand yen.*

```jp
毎日三時間ぐらい勉強します。
```
*I study for about three hours every day.*

Notice that you can use both ぐらい and くらい. They mean exactly the same thing, and you will hear both in real life—sometimes from the same person in the same breath. ぐらい is slightly more common in modern spoken Japanese, but use whichever flows off your tongue easier.

You can also attach it to question words to ask someone for an estimate.

```jp
何時間ぐらい寝ますか。
```
*About how many hours do you sleep?*

```jp
八時間ぐらい寝ます。
```
*I sleep for about eight hours.*

## The messy time rule

Textbooks often draw a strict line with time. They say ぐらい is only for *amounts* and *durations* ("about two hours"), while you should use a completely different word for specific points on the clock ("around 3:00"). 

In casual, everyday Japanese, that rule is messy. People actually use ぐらい for specific points in time quite often.

```jp
明日、三時ぐらいに行きます。
```
*I will go at about three o'clock tomorrow.*

> Hold onto this exception. We will officially meet the time-marker ごろ soon, and we will untangle how it compares to ぐらい together.

## Contrast: Softening vs. Limiting

You already know だけ (only / just). These two patterns live in exactly the same slot in a sentence—right after a quantity—but they send completely opposite signals.

```jp
百円だけあります。
```
*I have exactly one hundred yen.*

```jp
百円ぐらいあります。
```
*I have about one hundred yen.*

Swap だけ for ぐらい when you want to soften a number and leave yourself a little bit of wiggle room.
