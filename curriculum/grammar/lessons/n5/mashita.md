Picture this: it is Monday morning, and you are catching up with your Japanese teacher or a coworker. They casually ask how your weekend was. You want to tell them about the movie you watched, the delicious food you ate, or the friend you visited. 

But there is a problem. Right now, your Japanese toolkit only allows you to discuss the present and the future. If you try to tell them about the movie, you can only say `映画を見ます`—which tells them you *plan* to watch a movie. It does not help you share what already happened. To actually recount your experiences, tell stories, and connect over past events, you need a way to hit the rewind button on your verbs. You need the polite past tense.

## Shifting the Timeline

You are already intimately familiar with the polite verb ending `ます`. It is your reliable anchor for stating facts, declaring habits, and announcing future plans. The beautiful thing about Japanese verb conjugation—the way words change shape to express different meanings—is that it is heavily modular. It is a lot like clicking Lego pieces together. You do not have to memorize completely new, unpredictable words for the past tense the way you do in English (think of "eat" becoming "ate", or "buy" becoming "bought"). Instead, Japanese uses a predictable, mechanical swap.

To talk about the past, you take your standard polite verb, drop the `ます` completely, and attach `ました` in its place. That is it. The core of your verb—the stem, or the part of the word that carries the actual meaning of the action—stays completely untouched. We are just changing the grammatical bumper on the end to signal that the action is finished. Think about how powerful this is: if you can say "I will eat", you are only one syllable away from saying "I ate".

Let's look at the verb for "to read" as an example.

```jp
読む → 読みます → 読みました
```
*To read → read / will read → read (past).*

```jp
昨日、本を読みました。
```
*Yesterday, I read a book.*

Notice how clean that transition is. If you know the polite form of a verb, you automatically know its past tense form. There are no wild exceptions to this rule. Whether the verb is an everyday action like `食べる` (to eat) or a movement like `来る` (to come), the process is perfectly identical.

```jp
先生は今日、学校に来ました。
```
*The teacher came to school today.*

## The Negative Past: What Didn't Happen

So, `ました` handles the things you actually did. But what about the things you skipped? The coffee you avoided, the book you did not finish, or the friend you did not meet? You need a way to express the negative past.

You already know that to say you *won't* or *don't* do something politely, you use the ending `ません`. To shift this negative reality backward in time, we do not replace `ません` entirely. Instead, we build right on top of it. You take `ません` and simply attach the word `でした` to the very end.

This creates the compound ending `ませんでした`. It functions as a single, unbreakable block that means "did not do".

```jp
飲む → 飲みません → 飲みませんでした
```
*To drink → do not drink → did not drink.*

Let's apply it to a morning routine where you opted for water instead of your usual caffeine.

```jp
今日、コーヒーを飲みませんでした。
```
*I did not drink coffee today.*

This ending can feel a little intimidating at first. It is five full syllables long, which is quite a mouthful to stick onto the end of a verb. But its length is actually a secret weapon. Because it takes a moment to say, it gives your brain a valuable fraction of a second to plan your next thought while you are speaking. When you practice, do not just read it silently. Say it out loud until the rhythm feels completely natural. You will notice that in fast, fluent speech, native speakers often swallow the "shi" sound slightly, making it sound closer to a quick "ma-sen-desh-ta".

## Asking About the Past

Turning these past tense statements into questions is incredibly straightforward. Japanese does not require you to shuffle your word order around, and you do not need to add helper words like "did" to the beginning of the sentence. You just take your finished `ました` sentence, attach the question particle `か` to the very end, and let your voice rise slightly on the final syllable.

```jp
昨日、友達と遊びましたか。
```
*Did you play with a friend yesterday?*

Answering these questions is just as smooth. Japanese thrives on efficiency and loves to drop any context that both speakers already understand. You do not need to repeat "yesterday" or "with a friend". You can just reply with a simple confirmation and the conjugated verb.

```jp
一緒に遊びました。
```
*We played together.*

## Playing with Time: Now and Today

Obviously, past tense verbs pair brilliantly with words that explicitly point backward, like `昨日` (yesterday). But Japanese gives you a lot of flexibility when it comes to framing time. You can absolutely use `ました` with a word like `今日` (today), provided the action is already over and done with. If it is five o'clock in the evening, your lunch is securely in the past.

Even more interestingly, you can use `ました` with the word `今` (now). In English, "now" feels strictly tied to the ongoing present moment. But in Japanese, combining `今` with a past tense verb creates the distinct feeling of an action having *just* been completed a second ago.

```jp
今、起きました。
```
*I woke up just now.*

## Rules and Exceptions

The mechanics of `ました` are incredibly reliable, but there is one major grammatical trap you need to watch out for. This ending is strictly, exclusively, one-hundred-percent for action verbs.

> Because English uses the word "was" to handle the past tense for both nouns and adjectives ("The test was difficult," "He was a student"), it is very tempting to try and use `ました` the exact same way. You might logically think you can stick it onto `寒い` (cold) or `楽しい` (fun) to mean "It was cold" or "It was fun." Do not fall into this trap! Never say `寒いました` or `楽しいました`. This is one of the most common mistakes beginners make because their brains are translating English grammar directly into Japanese. But Japanese organizes the world differently. Adjectives and nouns belong to entirely separate grammatical families with their own unique rules for time travel. Keep `ました` securely locked to your action verbs.

## Casual Speech

It is important to remember that `ました` and `ませんでした`, just like their present-tense cousins, are inherently polite forms. They carry a baseline level of respect and social distance. You will use them constantly at work, in stores, with acquaintances, and with your teachers.

However, if you are hanging out at home with your partner or joking around with your closest friends, `ました` will suddenly feel stiff and unnatural. Using it in a very relaxed, intimate setting is almost like calling your best friend "Sir" or "Madam"—it creates an invisible wall of formality where there shouldn't be one. In those casual settings, the polite bumpers are dropped entirely, and the past tense takes on a completely different, shorter shape. You do not need to worry about that shape today, but it is good to be aware that `ました` is your "public facing" polite past tense.

## Contrast

To truly appreciate the power of what you have learned, look at the difference a single suffix makes. Let's line up the polite present and the polite past side by side.

```jp
今日、肉を食べます。
```
*Today, I will eat meat.*

```jp
昨日、肉を食べました。
```
*Yesterday, I ate meat.*

By simply swapping `ます` for `ました`, you completely rewrite the reality of the sentence. The first sentence is an intention; the meal has not happened yet. The second sentence is history; the plates are already cleared. When you are listening to spoken Japanese, train your ear to wait for that final syllable. That tiny structural shift is the only difference between an upcoming plan and a finished memory. Master `ます` for the things you will do, and `ました` for the things you have already done, and you instantly unlock the ability to share your life stories. Hold tightly onto this concept of shifting time—it is exactly the foundation you will need when we meet the casual plain past (the `た形`) a bit later on.
