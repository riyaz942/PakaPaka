You're looking at a photo with a friend, or pointing at something on a table, and you need to identify it. In English, you connect things to reality with "is", "am", or "are". In Japanese, you use one small word at the very end of your sentence: です—the copula (the word that works like *is/am/are*).

## How it works

Japanese sentences build backward compared to English. The core action or state always anchors the end. When you want to declare what something is, です lands at the finish line.

```jp
これはお茶です。
```
*This is tea.*

```jp
私は学生です。
```
*I am a student.*

You don't always need to include the "This is" or "I am" part. If you're both looking right at a dog, you don't need to specify what you're talking about. You just say the noun and wrap it up with です.

```jp
犬です。
```
*It is a dog.*

## Time and truth

In English, "is" changes to "was" or "is not" depending on when it happened or if it's true. Japanese does the same thing, changing the shape of です.

To talk about the past, です becomes でした.

```jp
昨日は学校でした。
```
*Yesterday was school.*

To say something *isn't* the case, です becomes ではありません. We will give this negative form its own dedicated spotlight later, but you should start recognizing it now as the standard way to say "is not".

```jp
肉ではありません。
```
*It is not meat.*

## Rules and exceptions

The copula です attaches directly to nouns. You don't need any extra linking words between the noun and です.

> **Watch out:** You cannot stick です onto verbs to make them polite. Verbs have their own polite rules. Saying `食べるです` to mean "I politely eat" is a classic beginner trap.

## Casual speech

です is polite. You use it with teachers, strangers, and coworkers. But if you're talking to a close friend, です drops its polite suit and becomes だ.

```jp
水だ。
```
*It is water.*

Just be careful with it. だ sounds a bit punchy and emphatic. Often, in casual speech, people drop it entirely and just say the noun. But when you need to make a firm statement, だ is the actual casual equivalent of です.

That is the foundation of Japanese right there. Once you know how to declare that something *is*, you have the basic structure down. Hold onto this—soon we'll swap the ending for words like でしょう to say "It's probably water, right?"
