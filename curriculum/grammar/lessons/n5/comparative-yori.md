You're looking at a menu with a friend. You want to eat steak, but they suggest sushi. How do you say "I like meat more than fish"?

When you want to compare two things in Japanese, you don't change the adjective like we do in English (big becomes bigger, cheap becomes cheaper). Instead, you use the pattern より.

```jp
肉は魚より好きです。
```
*I like meat more than fish.*

## How it works

The structure for a basic comparison looks like this: **[Item A] は [Item B] より [Adjective]**.

Think of より as meaning "compared to". So the sentence literally reads as: "As for Item A, compared to Item B, it is [Adjective]."

```jp
タクシーはバスより速いです。
```
*Taxis are faster than buses.*

```jp
今日は昨日より暑いです。
```
*Today is hotter than yesterday.*

Notice how 暑い (hot) and 速い (fast) didn't change at all? Japanese adjectives don't have a special comparative form. The word より does all the heavy lifting to tell the listener you are making a comparison.

## Flipping the order

In English, "Taxis are faster than buses" and "Compared to buses, taxis are faster" mean the same thing, just with a slightly different emphasis. Japanese does this too. You can easily swap the order of the two items as long as the particles stick to their words.

**[Item B] より [Item A] は [Adjective]** is perfectly natural.

```jp
ひらがなより漢字は難しいです。
```
*Compared to hiragana, kanji is difficult.*

```jp
あの店よりこの店は安いです。
```
*Compared to that shop over there, this shop is cheaper.*

> When you flip the sentence like this, you are throwing a little extra spotlight on the thing you are comparing against. It sounds a bit like, "If we're talking about hiragana... well, kanji is definitely harder."

## Leaving parts out

If you and your friend are already talking about dogs, you don't need to explicitly name them again just to say cats are smaller. If the context is obvious, you can drop the Item A part entirely.

```jp
猫より大きいです。
```
*It is bigger than a cat.*

```jp
昨日より寒いです。
```
*It is colder than yesterday.*

## より vs. から

You already know that から means "from" (like starting from a location or a time). Historically, より also meant "from", and in very formal or older written Japanese you might still see it used that way.

Because of this, you might occasionally see letters or messages end with something like 私より (From me). But in modern, everyday spoken Japanese, から is what you use for starting points (家から学校まで — from home to school). Keep より strictly for comparing things.

Hold onto this concept of making comparisons — soon you'll meet ぐらい, a handy little word that lets you say two things are *about the same*.
