You are sitting in a restaurant, and you just finished an incredible meal. Your friend asks how the food was. You want to say "It was delicious!" But wait—how do you make an adjective past tense in Japanese?

In English, adjectives are lazy. We say "is cold," "was cold," or "is not cold." The verb (*is/was*) does all the heavy lifting. In Japanese, however, i-adjectives are independent. They conjugate—meaning they change their own endings—to show time (past or present) and reality (positive or negative).

## How they shape-shift

Every i-adjective ends in the hiragana い. That final い is the hinge. To change the meaning, you simply pop off the い and attach a new ending.

**1. The Negative (It is not): `くない`**
If something isn't a certain way, drop the い and add くない.

```jp
このカメラは高くないです。
```
*This camera is not expensive.*

**2. The Past (It was): `かった`**
To talk about the past, drop the い and add かった.

```jp
レストランの料理はおいしかったです。
```
*The restaurant's food was delicious.*

**3. The Past Negative (It was not): `くなかった`**
This one looks long, but it's just a combination of the first two rules. First you make it negative (`くない`), then you treat that new い like an adjective and make it past tense (`くなかった`).

```jp
その本は難しくなかったです。
```
*That book was not difficult.*

**4. The Adverb (Doing things adjective-ly): `く`**
Sometimes you want an adjective to describe an action instead of a noun—like saying you woke up *early*, rather than describing an *early* morning. Drop the い and add く to connect it directly to a verb.

```jp
昨日は早く寝ました。
```
*I slept early yesterday.*

## Politeness and casual speech

You might have noticed the です at the end of some of those examples. When you conjugate an i-adjective, the adjective itself is doing the grammatical work. Adding です to the very end just acts as a politeness bumper. It doesn't change the meaning, it just makes the sentence polite.

If you are talking to a friend, you can drop the です entirely. The adjective carries all the information you need.

```jp
あの映画はつまらなかった。
```
*That movie was boring.*

## The one big exception

There is one word that trips everyone up the first week, and that is いい (good). 

いい is actually a newer, slightly lazier pronunciation of an older word: 良い (よい). While we say いい in the present tense, whenever it needs to conjugate, it snaps back to its original よ root.

```jp
今日の天気はよくないです。
```
*Today's weather is not good.*

Never say `いくない` or `いかった`. Always use よくない and よかった.

Mastering these endings gives you incredible freedom to express how you feel about the world around you. Hold onto this concept—it becomes very important when we meet な-adjectives, which live in the same space but behave completely differently.
