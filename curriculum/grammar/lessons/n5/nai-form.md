You're relaxing in your friend's living room. It's late, and you have a long day tomorrow. They ask if you want coffee. If you say コーヒーを飲みません, you sound perfectly polite, but maybe a little stiff for a late-night hangout. It sounds almost formal, like you're speaking to a waiter. How do you casually tell them "I won't drink it" without putting up that formal barrier? You need the plain negative: the ない form.

## Bending the verb

When you first learned to speak politely, you used ません to say you don't or won't do something. The ない form takes over that exact same job in casual speech. To make it, we just need to change the tail end of the verb.

Think of Japanese verbs as falling into a couple of main families. For the verbs that simply drop their final る sound when they conjugate (often called ru-verbs, like 食べる and 見る), the rule is effortless. You just chop off that final る and replace it with ない.

```jp
肉を食べない。
テレビを見ない。
```
*I don't eat meat.*
*I don't watch TV.*

Now, for the other major family of verbs (often called u-verbs), it requires a slight vowel shift. You take the final syllable of the dictionary form, shift its vowel from an "u" sound to an "a" sound, and then attach ない. 

For example, 飲む ends in the syllable *mu*. Shift that to *ma*, and you get 飲ま. Then add ない. 行く ends in *ku*. Shift that to *ka*, and you get 行か.

```jp
コーヒーを飲まない。
今日は学校に行かない。
```
*I won't drink coffee.*
*I won't go to school today.*

There is one special catch with this vowel shift. If your verb ends in the pure vowel う, like 買う (to buy) or 会う (to meet), that final vowel shifts to a わ sound, not an あ sound. So 買う becomes 買わない.

```jp
新しい靴を買わない。
友達に会わない。
```
*I won't buy new shoes.*
*I won't meet my friend.*

Finally, there are always irregulars that like to break the rules. The only one you need to worry about right now is 来る (to come). It completely changes its pronunciation at the beginning of the word, becoming 来ない (こない).

```jp
彼は来ない。
```
*He will not come.*

## Talking about the past

How do you talk about the past? When you want to say you *didn't* do something, you already know to use ませんでした.

The casual ない form handles the past a bit differently. To make the casual past negative, you take the ない ending, drop the final い, and add かった. So, ない becomes なかった. 

If you want to casually say you didn't play, you conjugate 遊ぶ to 遊ばない, and then change it to 遊ばなかった.

```jp
昨日は遊ばなかった。
```
*I didn't play yesterday.*

> **Watch out:** Don't try to mix politeness levels in a single verb. A sentence is either entirely polite (using ません) or entirely casual (using ない). Never say `見ませんない` or `飲まないません`. Choose the single level that matches your relationship with the listener.

## Distance and closeness

The difference between ません and ない isn't a change in dictionary meaning—it's a change in social distance.

```jp
私は行きません。
僕は行かない。
```
*I will not go. (Polite)*
*I won't go. (Casual)*

The ません ending puts a respectful, safe buffer between you and the listener. It's the default choice for your teachers, station staff (駅員), and people you just met. It shows you respect their boundaries.

The ない form deliberately removes that buffer. You use it with close friends, your immediate family like your siblings (兄弟), and when you are just thinking out loud to yourself. In fact, if you insist on using polite forms with your best friend, it can actually sound cold, like you are intentionally pushing them away. Mastering these plain forms is crucial for building real, close relationships in Japanese.

Once you get comfortable using ない and なかった, you'll start to notice they both end in that trailing い sound. Hold onto that thought—because when you formally learn how い-adjectives (like 大きい and 小さい) conjugate later, you'll find they behave exactly the same way. The language is beautifully consistent once you know what to look for.
