You're at a friend's house and notice them putting on their coat. If you just ask 帰りますか, it translates to "Will you go home?"—but it sounds a bit cold, like you're reading a survey question. You aren't just asking a random fact; you are seeing their coat and asking for an explanation based on that context. How do you ask "Oh, are you heading home?" with natural curiosity?

You use the explanatory んです.

```jp
帰るんですか。
```
*Are you going home?*

### How it works

To build this, you take a verb in its plain, dictionary form (like 帰る instead of 帰ります) and add んです.

```jp
明日行くんです。
```
*The thing is, I'm going tomorrow.*

To ask a question, you just add the question particle か to the end.

```jp
何を買うんですか。
```
*What is it that you are buying?*

Why does this exist? Japanese has a tool called a *nominalizer*—a little word that turns an entire verb phrase into a noun-like thing you can talk about. Here, that word is の. When you add の to a verb, you turn "I go" into "The situation is that I go." 

In spoken Japanese, の often gets squished into ん, and we cap it off with です to make it polite. (In formal writing, you'll still see the unsquished version: のです).

### Casual speech

If you are talking to a friend and dropping です, this pattern changes slightly. The ん untangles back into a clear の, and you just raise your pitch at the end to make it a question.

```jp
今食べるの？
```
*Are you eating now?*

```jp
どうして飲まないの？
```
*Why aren't you drinking?*

> When answering casually, using a flat の at the end of a statement (行くの。) can sound very soft or traditionally feminine. Men often use んだ instead, but when you're just starting out, simply replying with the plain verb (行く。) is your safest bet.

### Reading the room

Because this pattern is all about explanations and shared context, it comes with a catch: you shouldn't use it for everything.

If you walk into a room and announce out of nowhere, 明日行くんです (The thing is, I'm going tomorrow), people will wait for the rest of your story. It sounds like you are making an excuse or explaining a problem. Save it for when someone asks you why, or when you are reacting to something you both can see.

### Contrast: Invitations vs. Explanations

Let's look at how this fits with what you already know. You learned that adding ませんか to a verb stem makes a polite invitation. It looks similar to asking a negative んです question, but the meaning is totally different.

```jp
コーヒー、飲みませんか。
```
*Won't you drink some coffee?*

```jp
コーヒー、飲まないんですか。
```
*Are you not drinking coffee?*

The first invites them to drink. The second notes that they don't have a cup, and asks for the reason why.

Mastering んです is the key to sounding like you're actually participating in a conversation rather than just reading textbook sentences at people. Hold onto this feeling of modifying the ends of verbs—it becomes incredibly important when we look at ongoing actions with ている later on!
