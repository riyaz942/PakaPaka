You just walked through the door. You are starving, but your hands are completely dirty from the train. You want to tell your family you are going to eat *after* you wash your hands.

```jp
手を洗ってから、ご飯を食べます。
```
*After washing my hands, I will eat.*

## How it works

You already know the て-form (the versatile form that lets you link verbs and make requests). To say you will do something *after* doing something else, just take the て-form of the first action and attach から.

The action in the てから half of the sentence always happens first. The second half of the sentence is what happens next.

```jp
うちに帰ってから、寝ます。
ご飯を食べてから、映画を見ます。
```
*After returning home, I will sleep.*
*After eating a meal, we will watch a movie.*

## Controlling time

Here is a convenient trick: the てから part of your sentence does not have a tense of its own. It just locks the two events into a sequence.

To change the sentence from future to past, you only need to change the very last verb in the sentence.

```jp
本を読んでから、寝ました。
窓を閉めてから、出ました。
```
*After reading a book, I slept.*
*After closing the window, I left.*

## The "because" trap

This trips everyone up the first week, so it is completely normal if it feels weird at first. You previously learned that から means "because." So why does it suddenly mean "after"?

It is all about what the particle attaches to. If から follows a plain verb or a polite `ます`, it gives a reason ("because"). But if it attaches *specifically* to a て-form, the meaning transforms into a strict sequence ("after").

Look at how swapping just the verb form completely flips the meaning of the sentence:

```jp
映画を見るから、帰ります。
映画を見てから、帰ります。
```
*Because I will watch a movie, I will go home.*
*After watching a movie, I will go home.*

## Casual speech

When speaking casually with friends, the pattern works exactly the same way. You just drop the polite `ます` on the final verb at the very end of the sentence.

```jp
手を洗ってから、ご飯を食べる。
```
*After washing my hands, I will eat.*

> You can only use てから with verbs, because nouns do not have a て-form. If you want to say something like "after the movie," you will need a different pattern called 後で that we will tackle a little later. For now, master your sequences!
