Japanese culture values politeness and often relies on soft, indirect ways to say no. You might say something is 'a little difficult' when you really mean it is impossible. But there are times when being indirect just does not work. When you need to make a firm, undeniable statement that something is absolutely *not* the case, you need a stronger tool.

You are sitting in a restaurant with a Japanese friend, and they just ordered a notoriously spicy dish for you to try. They watch you take the first bite, looking a bit nervous. "Is it too spicy?" they ask. You want to reassure them. You don't just want to say "It's not spicy." You want to say "It's not spicy *at all*."

To completely deny something, to drop the degree or amount down to absolute zero, you need `全然` (zenzen).

`全然` is an adverb that essentially means "completely" or "entirely." But here is the critical catch: in standard, textbook Japanese, you only ever use it to modify something that is negative.

When you pair "completely" with a negative ending, the translation becomes "not at all." You are saying that the presence of that thing—whether it is spiciness, understanding, or money—is completely non-existent.

## Emphasizing the negative

Because `全然` is just an adverb, it does not change its shape. It simply floats in your sentence, usually sitting right in front of the verb or adjective you want to negate. All the actual grammar work happens at the end of the sentence.

Let's start with adjectives. If you want to say something is not spicy at all, you first take the adjective `辛い` (spicy). You conjugate it into the negative form by dropping the final `い` and adding `くない`, which gives you `辛くない`. Then, you just place `全然` in front of it.

```jp
全然辛くないです。
```
*It is not spicy at all.*

The feeling here is absolute. You are not just saying the spice level is low; you are saying the spice is zero. When native speakers say this, they often put a slight vocal stress on `全然` itself to really drive the point home. Try saying it out loud: *zenzen karakunai*. You can feel how it gives the sentence a natural rhythm and punch.

This pattern works exactly the same way with verbs. You take the verb you want to negate, change it to its negative form (like the `ない` form or the polite `ません` form), and place `全然` before it.

If your teacher asks if you understand a difficult kanji, and your mind is completely blank, you use the negative polite form of `分かる` (to understand), which is `分かりません`.

```jp
漢字が全然分かりません。
```
*I do not understand the kanji at all.*

You can also use this to talk about things that are physically missing or completely absent. To talk about existence, you use `ある` for inanimate objects. The negative of `ある` is simply `ない` (or `ありません` to be polite).

Let's say you are checking your wallet before walking into a convenience store, and you realize it is completely empty.

```jp
お金が全然ないです。
```
*I have absolutely no money.*

Notice that `全然` does not replace any particles. In the sentence above, the subject particle `が` stays exactly where it belongs. The adverb just slips in between the particle and the verb.

## Talking about the past

You can also use this pattern to talk about things that happened—or rather, completely failed to happen—in the past.

When you do this, `全然` remains exactly the same. The time-traveling happens entirely at the end of the sentence, where you conjugate your verb or adjective into the past-negative form.

Imagine a friend recommended a new movie to you. You went to the cinema to see it, but you thought it was incredibly boring. When they ask you how it was, you conjugate `面白い` (interesting) into the past-negative `面白くなかった`.

```jp
あの映画は全然面白くなかったです。
```
*That movie was not interesting at all.*

The emotion is very strong here. It was a complete waste of time.

## The messy reality of modern slang

Now we have to talk about how this word actually behaves in the wild.

If you spend any time around native speakers, especially anyone under the age of fifty, you are going to hear something that violently breaks the rule I just taught you. You are going to hear people use `全然` with positive words.

Someone might ask if it is okay to sit next to you on a train, and a native speaker might reply with `全然いいです` (It is completely fine). Or they might eat something delicious and exclaim `全然おいしい` (This is totally delicious).

> **Wait, didn't you just say it has to be negative?** Yes. In strict, traditional grammar, `全然` must pair with a negative word. But language evolves, and over the last few decades, using `全然` as a general-purpose amplifier meaning "totally" or "completely" has become incredibly common in casual speech.

So, what should you do? For exams, written documents, and talking to your boss, strictly follow the rule: `全然` requires a negative verb or adjective. But when you are hanging out with friends, be prepared to hear it paired with positive words. Just know that if you use it that way yourself, you are officially using modern slang.

Another trap to avoid: you cannot just say "全然" on its own to answer a question, unless you are speaking in extremely casual, fragmented shorthand. In a polite conversation, you must finish the sentence with the negative verb or adjective.

## Dropping the politeness

Because `全然` is all about raw emphasis and emotion, it shows up in casual speech constantly. When you are speaking casually with your friends, you simply use the short, dictionary form of the negative verb or adjective. You drop the polite `です` or `ます`.

Imagine a friend asks if you are busy today, and you have absolutely nothing on your schedule.

```jp
今日は全然忙しくない。
```
*I am not busy at all today.*

Or maybe someone asks if your younger brother studies at home. You want to emphasize that he absolutely does not open a single book.

```jp
弟は家で全然勉強しない。
```
*My younger brother does not study at home at all.*

In very casual speech, you might even hear people contract the negative endings further, like turning `分からない` into `分かんない`. So `全然分かんない` (I totally don't get it) is a phrase you will hear constantly in anime or casual street interviews. The rule remains the same: the adverb amplifies the negative ending, no matter how much that ending gets shortened.

## Contrast: The absolute versus the restricted

Let's put this pattern next to something you already know. You have already learned how to use `しか〜ない` (only / nothing but).

Both of these grammar points are special because they are magnets for the negative form. The moment you use `全然` or `しか` in a sentence, you are locking the end of your sentence into a negative conjugation. But they do vastly different things to the meaning.

Look at these two sentences about eating vegetables.

```jp
私は野菜しか食べません。
```
*I eat nothing but vegetables.*

```jp
私は野菜を全然食べません。
```
*I do not eat vegetables at all.*

The `しか` sentence is restrictive. Think of `しか〜ない` as drawing a tiny circle around the one thing you *do*, and putting everything else outside the circle. You are actively doing the action of eating, but you are artificially limiting the scope to *only* vegetables. The negative ending `食べません` is not saying "I don't eat"—it is saying "I don't eat (anything else)."

The `全然` sentence is absolute destruction. Think of `全然〜ない` as erasing the board entirely. You are taking the action of eating vegetables and reducing it to zero. The negative `食べません` is literal here: you do not do the action, not even a little bit.

Also, look closely at the particles. `しか` is powerful enough that it actually destroys and replaces the normal object particle `を`. But `全然` is just a well-behaved adverb, so the `を` particle stays exactly where it normally belongs.

You can also contrast this absolute zero with the extreme upper limit you learned previously: `すぎる` (too much). These two patterns sit on complete opposite ends of the spectrum.

If you are looking at a shirt in a store and the price is outrageous, you would use `すぎる`.

```jp
このシャツは高すぎます。
```
*This shirt is too expensive.*

But if the shirt is incredibly cheap, surprisingly so, you drop the degree of expense down to zero.

```jp
このシャツは全然高くないです。
```
*This shirt is not expensive at all.*

Using `全然` makes your Japanese sound incredibly natural because it adds emotional weight and confidence to your denials. It turns a flat, robotic "no" into a definitive "absolutely not." Hold onto this feeling of modifying the degree of a negative—it will come in handy when you meet its softer cousin `あまり〜ない` (not very much) soon.
