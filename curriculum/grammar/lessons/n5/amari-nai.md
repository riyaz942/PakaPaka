You just walked out of a movie with a friend. They turn to you and ask if you liked it. You didn't hate it, but it certainly wasn't a masterpiece. How do you soften the blow and say it wasn't *very* good?

You use あまり.

```jp
その映画はあまり面白くないです。
```
*That movie isn't very interesting.*

## How it works

あまり is an adverb—a word that sits right before the adjective or verb you want to modify. But here is the golden rule: **あまり must always pair with a negative word.**

```jp
今日はあまり寒くないです。
```
*Today is not very cold.*

It works exactly the same way for actions. If you don't do something very often or very much, just place あまり before the negative verb.

```jp
私はあまり肉を食べません。
```
*I do not eat meat much.*

```jp
昨日はあまり寝ませんでした。
```
*I didn't sleep much yesterday.*

## The conversational shortcut

When speaking casually, あまり almost always morphs into あんまり. It means the exact same thing, but adding that little bounce in the middle makes it flow better in fast speech.

```jp
彼はあんまり勉強しません。
```
*He doesn't study much.*

## Contrast: The negative spectrum

You already know 全然 (not at all). Both of these words require a negative verb or adjective to finish the sentence, but they sit at different ends of the spectrum.

```jp
日本語はあまり難しくないです。
```
*Japanese is not very difficult.*

```jp
日本語は全然難しくないです。
```
*Japanese is not difficult at all.*

Use あまり when you want to soften a statement, and 全然 when you want to completely deny it.

> **Don't fall into the affirmative trap:** Because English speakers often think of words like "much" as positive ("I ate very much"), it is incredibly tempting to throw あまり into a positive sentence. Don't do it. A sentence like `あまり食べます` (literally: "I eat not much") sounds broken to a Japanese speaker. The verb itself must carry the negative state: you *do not* eat much.

Keep this scaling pattern in your back pocket. Soon you'll learn how to say things *become* a certain way using なる, and knowing exactly how to measure your descriptions will make you sound much more natural.
