You're in a shop, admiring a very expensive camera. A clerk approaches and asks if you want to buy it. You want to politely let them know you're not here to spend money — you're just looking.

```jp
見るだけです。
```
*I am just looking.*

To express "only" or "just" in Japanese, you use the particle だけ. It acts as a hard boundary, fencing off whatever it attaches to and excluding everything else.

## How it works

If you want to limit a noun, you attach だけ directly to the end of it. It works like a filter, telling the listener "this thing and absolutely nothing else."

```jp
水だけ飲みます。
```
*I only drink water.*

```jp
千円だけあります。
```
*I only have a thousand yen.*

It attaches to verbs exactly the same way. You just drop it right after the dictionary form (the plain, base form of the verb) before finishing your sentence.

```jp
少し休むだけです。
```
*I am just resting a little.*

## Rules and exceptions

Because だけ is a particle, it has to share space with the other particles you already know. This is where people usually trip up.

If you would normally mark a word with the subject marker `が` or the direct object marker `を`, だけ usually just eats them. You don't need to say `水をだけ飲みます` — just `水だけ飲みます` is perfectly natural and much more common.

But for other particles, like the target particle に, they team up. だけ usually goes right before に to say "only to" this specific person.

```jp
彼だけに話します。
```
*I will speak only to him.*

## Contrast: The hard limit vs the outer limit

You already know まで, which sets an outer boundary ("up to" or "until"). Let's look at how まで and だけ feel side by side when you're talking about a box of snacks on the table.

```jp
一つまでです。
```
*Up to one.*

```jp
一つだけです。
```
*Only one.*

With まで, you're setting a rule about the maximum allowed — zero is fine, one is fine, but stop there. With だけ, you're making a factual statement about an exact quantity. There isn't *up to* one; there is *exactly and only* one.

Hold onto this rigid, exact feeling. Right now, だけ sets a perfectly hard line. But real life is messy, and soon we'll look at ぐらい, a word that steps into the same space to soften the edges and say "about" or "roughly."
