You are making plans to meet a friend at a coffee shop. You don't want to set a rigid, exact time because trains can be delayed or you might be running late. How do you suggest meeting "around" a certain time to keep things flexible?

## How it works

To approximate a point in time, simply attach ごろ directly after the time word. It works exactly like saying "around" or "about" in English.

```jp
三時ごろ駅で会いますか。
```
*Shall we meet at the station around three o'clock?*

```jp
十日ごろ帰ります。
```
*I will return home around the 10th.*

```jp
来週ごろ、新しい車を買います。
```
*I will buy a new car around next week.*

You can attach particles (little grammar markers) like に right after ごろ to lock in a specific time. However, Japanese speakers frequently drop the に in casual conversation and just say ごろ.

```jp
夕方ごろに雨が降ります。
```
*It will rain around evening.*

## Rules and exceptions

You will sometimes see this written with the kanji 頃 (ごろ). It means the exact same thing.

There is one special case: when you want to talk about a time period in the past, like "when I was a child." Because a noun like "child" isn't a time word on its own, we connect it with the particle の, and the pronunciation usually shifts to ころ.

```jp
子供の頃、毎日牛乳を飲みました。
```
*When I was a child, I drank milk every day.*

> When using ごろ with periods of life (like childhood), always remember the の and expect to hear 頃 (ころ).

## ごろ vs. ぐらい

This one trips everyone up the first week — that's normal. You already learned that ぐらい (or くらい) also means "about" or "approximately."

Here is the golden rule: ごろ is for a **point in time** on the clock or calendar (when something happens). ぐらい is for an **amount** or **duration** (how long it takes or how much of something there is).

Compare these two sentences:

```jp
二時ごろ寝ます。
```
*I will sleep around two o'clock.*

```jp
二時間ぐらい寝ます。
```
*I will sleep for about two hours.*

If you swap them, it sounds very strange. You cannot sleep for "two o'clock," and you cannot go to bed at "two hours." Keep ごろ on the clock, and ぐらい on the stopwatch.

You now have the tools to make flexible plans. Hold onto this idea of softening your sentences — it becomes very important when we meet patterns like でも (or something) later on to make gentle suggestions!
