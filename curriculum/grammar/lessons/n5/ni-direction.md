You are making weekend plans. You want to tell your friend you are heading to the park, and you want to ask what time they usually wake up. To say *where* you are going or *when* an event happens, you need a way to pinpoint that information. In Japanese, we use the particle に.

## How it works

Think of に as a dart. You throw it, and it lands on a specific, fixed target. That target can be a place on a map, a specific time on a clock, or even a person.

First, let's throw the dart at a map. When you use motion verbs like 行く (to go) or 帰る (to return home), に attaches to the place to mark your exact destination.

```jp
私は学校に行きます。
```
*I go to school.*

```jp
彼は家に帰ります。
```
*He returns home.*

Next, throw the dart at a calendar or a clock. に attaches to specific times to say exactly *when* an action happens.

```jp
何時に起きますか。
```
*What time do you wake up?*

```jp
日曜日に公園に行きます。
```
*I will go to the park on Sunday.*

Finally, you can throw the dart at a person. When an action is directed at someone—like meeting them or asking them a question—に marks that person as your target.

```jp
友達に会います。
```
*I meet a friend.*

```jp
先生に聞きます。
```
*I ask the teacher.*

## Rules and exceptions

Here is where に gets tricky, and it trips everyone up the first week—that's normal! You cannot just put に after *every* time word.

Remember how に is a dart hitting a fixed target? Some time words are relative. "Tomorrow" (明日) changes depending on when you say it. "Today" (今日) is a floating concept, not a fixed number on a clock or calendar. Because they aren't fixed points, they reject the に dart.

> Relative time words like 今日 (today), 明日 (tomorrow), and いつ (when) never take に.

So, you just leave them bare in the sentence:

```jp
明日、学校に行きます。
```
*I will go to school tomorrow.*

## Casual speech

In casual, everyday conversation, Japanese speakers often drop particles completely if the meaning is obvious. The destination に is one of the first to disappear when you are talking to friends.

```jp
明日、公園行く？
```
*Are you going to the park tomorrow?*

## に vs を

You learned を (the direct object particle) for the thing you physically act upon. Compare these two sentences:

```jp
水を飲みます。
```
*I drink water.*

```jp
友達に会います。
```
*I meet a friend.*

In the first sentence, water is the object you are directly consuming. In the second, your friend is the *target* you are directing your "meeting" action toward. You aren't "consuming" your friend! That is why interaction verbs like 会う (to meet) and 聞く (to ask/listen) take the target particle に, not the direct object を.

Whether it's a place, a time, or a person, に is your trusty dart for hitting the target. Hold onto this concept of a destination—soon you will meet へ, a particle that lives in the same space but cares more about the journey than the exact landing spot.
