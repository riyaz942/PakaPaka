Our personal feelings shift over time, and we become fond of entirely new hobbies that we previously ignored.

To express this powerful idea of transformation in Japanese, we use the grammar pattern `くなる` or `になる`. This pattern is one of the most essential building blocks in the entire language because it breathes life into your sentences. Let's look at how you can celebrate your own incredible progress by describing your changing abilities.

```jp
日本語が上手になりました。
```
*I became good at Japanese.*

## How it works

When we want to attach the verb for "to become" to a descriptive adjective or a noun, we cannot simply push the two separate words together. Japanese grammar requires us to use a specific grammatical "glue" to connect them smoothly and naturally. The exact shape this connection takes depends entirely on the specific type of word that comes right before the change of state verb. To make this easy to master, we will divide this into three distinct categories: i-adjectives, na-adjectives, and nouns. Understanding these three paths will make conjugation feel automatic.

### 1. Changing states with i-adjectives

i-adjectives are the classic describing words that end in the hiragana character `い`. They have their own special, built-in conjugation rules. To show that an i-adjective is actively changing its state, you must first completely remove the final `い` character from the end of the word. You absolutely cannot leave it there. Once you have successfully dropped it, you replace it with `く` and then attach the verb `なる`.

Think of the `く` as a sturdy bridge that connects the core descriptive root of the adjective directly to the verb "to become". For example, if you take the word for hot, you drop the final character to isolate the root, then add the new ending to get `暑くなる`. This indicates that the temperature is currently rising. Let's see this in a complete sentence.

```jp
夏は暑くなる。
```
*Summer becomes hot.*

If you want to be polite and talk about a change that has already happened, you will conjugate the verb at the very end of the sentence. The polite past tense of the verb is `なりました`. This is perfect for talking about events that have concluded.

```jp
新しいパソコンは安くなりました。
```
*New computers became cheap.*

### 2. Changing states with na-adjectives

Unlike i-adjectives, na-adjectives do not use `く` as a grammatical bridge. In fact, when connecting a na-adjective to this specific grammar point, you do not use the `な` character at all. Instead, you simply take the bare dictionary root of the na-adjective and attach the particle `に` followed immediately by the verb `なる`.

The particle `に` is incredibly versatile in the Japanese language. You have likely seen it used to point toward a physical destination or a specific time on the clock. Here, it is pointing toward a *new state of being*. It clearly marks the ultimate destination of the transformation. For example, if a noisy, bustling location finally calms down for the night, you take the word for quiet, add the particle, and finish with the verb. Let's look at an example to see how elegant this is.

```jp
町は静かになりました。
```
*The town became quiet.*

### 3. Changing states with nouns

Nouns function almost exactly the same way as na-adjectives. Because they represent fixed concepts, physical objects, professions, or categories, you simply use the particle `に` to mark the noun as the final destination of your change, and then add `なる`. There is no complicated bridge required.

This is an extremely useful pattern when talking about people's big dreams for the future, or when someone transitions into a completely new phase of life. A young child becomes a mature adult, an eager student becomes a wise teacher, or in this next example, a family member enters a highly respected medical profession.

```jp
娘は医者になる。
```
*My daughter will become a doctor.*

## Rules and exceptions

Even with a relatively straightforward grammatical pattern, Japanese has a few unique quirks and sharp edges that you need to watch out for. Here is exactly what you need to keep in mind to ensure your Japanese sounds perfectly natural to native speakers.

### The special case of good

In Japanese, the most common adjective for "good" behaves like a magical shapeshifter. While you will almost always hear it spoken in its standard everyday form, its grammatical root is actually completely different. Whenever you need to conjugate this specific word—whether you are making it negative, putting it in the past tense, or turning it into a change of state—you must strictly revert to its formal, traditional root.

Therefore, you must never drop the final character of the common form and attach the bridge character. That sounds incredibly unnatural and jarring to native speakers. Instead, you switch to the formal root, drop its ending, and then reliably apply the standard i-adjective rule.

> Watch out! If a cold gets better, we say `病気がよくなりました` (The illness got better), and we never say `病気がいくなりました`.

### The dominance of the past tense

When you speak English, you might say "It is getting dark" to describe a continuous change that is happening right in front of your eyes. In Japanese, however, you will hear the past tense forms `くなりました` and `になりました` far more often than you will ever hear the present tense forms.

Why does this happen? Because human beings generally only comment on a physical or emotional change *after* the transformation is already noticeable to everyone. If you step outside and shiver, the change in temperature has already occurred. If you notice that the sun has completely set, the state of the sky has already shifted.

Using the present tense `なる` usually implies that the change will definitely happen in the future, acting as a general rule or a confident prediction. If you want to say that something *has* changed and you are currently experiencing the direct result of that change, always stick to the past tense.

```jp
空が暗くなりました。
```
*The sky became dark.*

## State vs. Change of State

When you first start building your vocabulary and fundamental grammar knowledge, you primarily learn how to describe the world exactly as it is right now. You learn how to paint a static, unchanging picture. For instance, you already know how to completely deny a physical description using the familiar grammar pattern `全然〜ない` (not at all).

When you use this older pattern, you are stating a firm, unchanging fact about the current situation. There is absolutely no movement, no shift, and no progression. The situation is entirely fixed in place.

```jp
映画は全然面白くない。
```
*The movie is not interesting at all.*

However, the real world is rarely static. Things continuously evolve. By carefully swapping out that negative ending for our newly learned change of state pattern, you completely shift the core meaning of the sentence. You are no longer painting a static picture; you are describing a thrilling, dynamic progression.

```jp
映画は面白くなる。
```
*The movie becomes interesting.*

Notice how incredibly similar the beginnings of these two sentences look, yet how wildly different their actual meanings are! The first simply observes a boring, unchangeable fact. The second promises an exciting, developing change in the near future.

Let's look at another powerful contrast, this time using the grammar pattern `もう〜ました` (already did). You use this sibling pattern to officially declare that a physical action has been fully completed. It focuses heavily on the action itself.

```jp
もう勉強しました。
```
*I already studied.*

But what if the exact thing that is completed is not a physical action, but a profound shift in your own personal feelings? If you used to hate opening your textbooks, but now you genuinely enjoy the studying process, you have undergone a massive internal transformation.

```jp
勉強が好きになりました。
```
*I became fond of studying.*

Both of these specific sentences talk about a reality that is true for you right now in the present moment. However, the first one merely reports an action you checked off your daily to-do list. The second one reveals a deep, beautifully completed change in your personality or your personal preferences.
