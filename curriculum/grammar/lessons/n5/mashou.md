You're walking around the park with an acquaintance. You're both tired and hungry, and you want to suggest getting some food. How do you step up and propose a plan?

```jp
一緒にご飯を食べましょう。
```
*Let's eat a meal together.*

## How it works

To make this "let's" form, you add ましょう to the **stem** of a verb. 

The stem is simply the core of the verb—the part you'd put before polite endings like ます. If you are building it directly from the dictionary form:

- For ru-verbs (like 食べる or 見る), drop the final る and add ましょう.
- For u-verbs (like 飲む or 行く), change that final 'u' sound to an 'i' sound (飲む becomes 飲み), then add ましょう.

```jp
学校に行きましょう。
コーヒーを飲みましょう。
```
*Let's go to school.*
*Let's drink coffee.*

## Softening the suggestion (ましょうか)

If you add the question particle か to the end, you get ましょうか. This simple tweak does two very useful things.

First, it turns a confident "Let's" into a softer "Shall we?":

```jp
帰りましょうか。
```
*Shall we go home?*

Second, it lets you offer your own help to someone else. If you see someone looking warm in a stuffy room, you can offer to fix it:

```jp
窓を開けましょうか。
```
*Shall I open the window?*

## Rules and exceptions

Because this pattern is all about proposing actions, you can only use it with things you actually have the power to do. 

> You can suggest "Let's wait here" (ここで待ちましょう), but you cannot say something like `分かりましょう` (Let's understand). If an action is out of your direct control, ましょう doesn't work.

```jp
ここで待ちましょう。
```
*Let's wait here.*

## Casual speech

ましょう is built for polite company—acquaintances, coworkers, or people you don't know perfectly well. When you are hanging out with close friends, Japanese drops this ending completely.

Instead, verbs morph into a shorter casual form (a volitional form) that usually ends in an "ou" sound. For example, 食べましょう shrinks to `食べよう`, and 行きましょう becomes `行こう`. You don't need to master those casual shapes today, but keep your ears open for them when friends talk to each other.

## Looking ahead

There is one slight catch to using ましょう. When you say "Let's watch a movie tomorrow," you are taking the lead. You are assuming the other person is basically on board.

```jp
明日、映画を見ましょう。
```
*Let's watch a movie tomorrow.*

If you aren't sure if they want to, and you want to politely ask "Would you like to watch a movie?", you need a slightly different approach. That's where ませんか comes in. It lives in the exact same space, but offers the listener an easy way to say no. You'll meet it very soon, but for now, enjoy the confidence of ましょう. You're steering the ship.
