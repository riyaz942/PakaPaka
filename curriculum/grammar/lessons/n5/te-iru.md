You get a message from a friend asking what you are up to. You're in the middle of a meal. If you reply with 食べます, it sounds like you're stating a daily habit or telling them you will eat later. To say "I *am eating* right now," you need the pattern ている.

## How it works
To show that an action is currently in progress, take the **て-form** of a verb (which you already know from making requests like てください) and add いる. In polite speech, this becomes います.

```jp
朝ご飯を食べています。
```
*I am eating breakfast.*

```jp
友達を待っています。
```
*I am waiting for a friend.*

This pattern is the Japanese equivalent of the English "-ing" ending. It frames the verb as an ongoing action.

```jp
今、雨が降っています。
```
*It is raining right now.*

## Rules and exceptions
For most verbs, the ている pattern means "is currently doing." But for a few specific verbs, it means a lasting state. Think of instantaneous actions, like "getting to know" someone. Once you cross that threshold, you remain in the state of knowing them. Because of this, knowing a person or a fact is almost always expressed with ている.

```jp
彼を知っています。
```
*I know him.*

> Watch out for the verb 分かる (to understand). It already describes a continuous state of mind all on its own, so we normally just use 分かります. Using `分かっている` usually sounds like an annoyed "I already know that!"

## Casual speech: dropping the "i"
When speaking casually with friends, native speakers naturally take shortcuts. It is incredibly common to drop the い entirely. Instead of saying ている, they just say てる.

```jp
テレビを見てるの？
```
*Are you watching TV?*

*(Notice the explanatory の you learned earlier making an appearance here to ask for context!)*

## Contrast: Habit vs. Happening Now
It is very easy to default to standard dictionary or ます forms for everything you want to say, but they have distinct jobs. A standard verb talks about general truths, routines, or the future. The ている pattern snaps the focus to right now.

```jp
毎日、お茶を飲みます。
```
*I drink tea every day.*

```jp
今、お茶を飲んでいます。
```
*I am drinking tea right now.*

Hold onto this continuous state pattern—it becomes a crucial building block later when we talk about rules and things you *must not* do (てはいけない).
