You get home, keys still in your hand, and call out ただいま — but nobody answers right away. Everyone in the house is already mid-task, and you can hear it before you even reach the living room: something's cooking, someone's humming along to a song. Nobody planned any of this for you, and none of it is a habit you could look up on a schedule — it's just what's happening in the house at this exact second, and that's precisely the gap ている exists to fill.

You open the door and look around.

```jp
父は新聞を読んでいる。
```

*My father is reading the newspaper.*

```jp
母は料理をしている。
```

*My mother is cooking.*

```jp
妹は歌を歌っている。
```

*My little sister is singing.*

None of that is what your family generally does. Your father doesn't spend his life reading the newspaper, and your sister isn't a professional singer — this is just what each of them happens to be doing at this exact second, and freezing an action mid-swing like that is the entire job of ている.

Japanese doesn't mark this the way English bolts -ing onto a verb. It literally glues "exist" onto the action, so a sentence like this says something closer to "reading, existing" — reading is the state your father exists in right now. Once that clicks, the whole pattern stops feeling like a separate tense and starts feeling like exactly what it is: one verb sitting on top of another. You already know いる as a standalone word meaning "to exist" for people and animals; this is that exact same いる, just borrowed to describe existing in the middle of doing something instead of existing in a place.

## How ている Works

The shape is simple once you already have the te-form — the て／で shape you use to connect actions and ask for things. Take a verb's te-form and add いる right after it. That's the whole rule: ている isn't a separate conjugation with its own quirks, it's always somebody's te-form plus this one extra verb sitting on top of it.

```jp
料理する → 料理して → 料理している
```

*to cook → te-form → is cooking*

```jp
歌う → 歌って → 歌っている
```

*to sing → te-form → is singing*

```jp
泣く → 泣いて → 泣いている
```

*to cry → te-form → is crying*

Whatever sound change the te-form already goes through — する becoming して, う becoming って, く becoming いて — just rides along into ている unchanged. There's nothing new to memorize about the verb itself here; you're gluing a familiar shape onto a form you can already build with your eyes closed.

It also helps to notice what ている is replacing. The plain form 読む on its own usually points at something general — a habit, a fact, a plan already on the calendar — not something unfolding this second. That gap between "reads" and "is reading" is exactly the space ている exists to fill, and every ている sentence is quietly doing two things at once: naming the base action, and timestamping it to right now.

Context carries some of that weight too. 今 (right now) often shows up right next to ている specifically to nail the timing down, because the same shape can also describe something a person does repeatedly over a stretch of time, not only this literal second. Nothing in the grammar itself decides which reading is meant — the sentence around it does that work.

## Rules and Exceptions

> ている always sits on top of the te-form, never the plain dictionary form. There's no such word as `泣くいる` — it has to be 泣いている.

This mix-up is common in the first few weeks, because いる by itself just means "to exist" (for people and animals), and it's tempting to tack it straight onto the dictionary form the way you'd add any second verb. It doesn't work like that — いる leans entirely on the te-form underneath it.

> ある and いる already describe a state on their own, so they never take ている. There's no `あっている` or `いている` — the plain forms already do that job.

This one trips people up precisely because ている usually signals "something is happening," and existing feels like the most basic kind of happening there is. But existing isn't an action you can catch someone partway through — a thing either exists right now or it doesn't — so the plain forms already carry that meaning without any help from ている.

One more thing worth knowing: every example above is in plain form, which is exactly how you'd talk to family or close friends. Swap いる for います and you get the polite version instead — 読んでいます, 歌っています — for anyone you'd normally address politely. The rule underneath doesn't change at all; only the last piece does. Nothing else in the sentence moves around for any of this, either — particles, time words, and objects all stay exactly where they already were, since ている is simply replacing the verb's ending, not rearranging anything around it.

## Casual Speech

In fast, everyday speech, ている often loses its い entirely and shrinks down to てる. You'll hear this constantly between friends and family members — it isn't sloppy Japanese, it's just how the spoken language moves once nobody's being careful about it.

```jp
妹は今、宿題をしてる。
```

*My little sister's doing her homework right now.*

```jp
父も今、テレビを見てる。
```

*My father's watching TV right now too.*

The dropped い isn't random, either — casual spoken Japanese regularly thins out a weak, unstressed vowel sitting in the middle of a word, and ている's い is a textbook case of exactly that. You'll notice the same instinct at work elsewhere the further you get into casual speech.

Keep the full ている for anything even slightly formal — a note to a teacher, a conversation with someone you've just met, anything written carefully — and save てる for the people you're already relaxed around. Nobody will misunderstand you either way; it's a register choice, not a correctness one.

## Contrast: ている vs らしい

You've already met らしい for reporting something you picked up from evidence or heard secondhand rather than confirmed yourself. ている and らしい aren't fighting over the same job — they actually stack — but the difference between using one alone and adding the other on top is worth feeling out carefully.

```jp
妹が泣いている。
```

*My little sister is crying.*

```jp
妹が泣いているらしい。
```

*Sounds like my little sister is crying.*

The first sentence is something you're witnessing directly — you're in the room, you can hear it and see it happening. The second is something you're relaying secondhand — a phone call, a message, a closed door you haven't opened yet. ている states the moment as a plain fact you're standing right in front of; stacking らしい on top of that same ている quietly tells the listener you weren't actually there to confirm it yourself. Swap one for the other and you've changed whether you're a witness or a messenger, even though the underlying event is identical.

ようだ and みたいだ can layer on top of ている the same way らしい does — the flavor of your evidence shifts a little between them, but the underlying move, adding a layer of distance on top of a plain fact, stays the same across all three.

This is worth sitting with, because it's easy to assume ている is only ever about certainty. It isn't — ている just states what's unfolding; how sure you are, or how you found out, is a separate layer you add or leave off on top of it.

ている is going to keep showing up for the rest of your Japanese, and it won't always be doing this exact job. Soon you'll run into verbs where this same shape describes a state that already happened rather than one still unfolding — but that's a story for another lesson, and nothing you need to worry about yet. For now, every time you catch somebody mid-action — reading, cooking, singing, crying — this is the shape that says so.
