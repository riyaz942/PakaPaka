Picture this. You just arrived at a friend's house. You can smell something amazing coming from the kitchen. They just finished cooking dinner, and the table is set. You are starving and ready to dig right in. You pull up a chair, but your friend points a stern finger toward the bathroom sink.

You need to wash your hands *before* you eat.

In English, "before" is a very simple word. You just slap it in front of a noun or a verb phrase and move on with your life. But to talk about the sequence of events in Japanese, you have to understand how the language treats time. It helps to think of time as a physical space.

## How it works

To say "before" in Japanese, we use the pattern `前に` (まえに). Let's break this down.

The word `前` translates directly to "front." Think of time as a road you are walking down. The things that haven't happened yet are physically in "front" of you.

Then we have the particle `に`. You already know that `に` is used to pin an action to a specific time on the clock. It works the exact same way here. By combining them, `前に` literally means "at the time in front of" or "at the point just before."

Let's look at how to attach it to the actions you want to take.

### Attaching to verbs

When you are attaching an action to `前に`, you must always use the verb's **dictionary form**. This is the plain, basic form of the verb you look up in the dictionary.

```jp
食べる前に手を洗う。
```
*I wash my hands before eating.*

Why the dictionary form? This is where Japanese logic is incredibly consistent. The dictionary form is technically called the "non-past" or "incomplete" form.

Think about the exact moment you are standing at the sink washing your hands. Has the eating happened yet? No. The eating is still in the future. It is completely incomplete. Because the eating hasn't happened yet relative to washing your hands, it must stay in its basic, un-conjugated state.

### Attaching to nouns

What if you want to use a noun instead of a verb? Maybe you want to say "before class" or "before dinner."

Grammatically speaking, `前` is actually a noun itself. In Japanese, you cannot just smash two nouns together like building blocks. You need a way to link them. This is where your trusty noun-gluing particle `の` comes to the rescue.

The formula is straightforward: Noun + `の` + `前に`.

```jp
授業の前にコーヒーを飲む。
```
*I drink coffee before class.*

```jp
晩ご飯の前にテレビを見る。
```
*I watch TV before dinner.*

You can use this structure with almost any "event" noun. It works perfectly for meals, school, work, or any scheduled block of time in your day.

## The past tense trap

Now we arrive at the single biggest trap for English speakers learning this pattern. This one trips almost everyone up the first week, so it is completely normal if it feels a little backwards at first.

What happens if you are telling a story about yesterday? In English, we say "Before I *slept*, I *read* a book." Both verbs are safely conjugated into the past tense.

Let's see how Japanese handles that exact same sentence.

```jp
寝る前に本を読んだ。
```
*I read a book before sleeping.*

Look closely at that first verb. `寝る` (to sleep) is still in the dictionary form! Even though you read the book last night—an action firmly in the past, shown by the past tense `読んだ` at the end of the sentence—the sleeping is not conjugated.

> **Never put the verb before 前に in the past tense.** Even if your entire story happened ten years ago, `寝た前に` is always grammatically incorrect and will confuse the person you are talking to.

Why does Japanese do this? It comes back to that relative time logic we talked about earlier.

Japanese grammar doesn't care about the absolute time on the clock right now. It cares about the time *relative to the actions*. At the exact moment you were reading the book last night, the sleeping was still in your future.

The verb at the very end of the sentence acts like a time machine. That final verb (`読んだ`) is the anchor. It pulls the entire sentence into the past. Every other verb in the sentence just relates to that final anchor point. 

## Using it to say "ago"

There is a special, extremely useful shortcut you can take when dealing with exact numbers and time durations.

If you attach `前に` directly to a duration of time—like minutes, hours, or days—it translates to "ago." It literally means "at the time before this duration."

```jp
三時間前に来た。
```
*I came three hours ago.*

Notice what is missing here? There is no `の`. When you use a specific time duration like "three hours" (`三時間`) or "five minutes" (`五分`), you attach it directly to the pattern. You do not say `三時間の前に`.

```jp
五分前に起きた。
```
*I woke up five minutes ago.*

This is one of the most common and natural ways to answer the question "When did you get here?" or "When did that happen?"

## Casual speech drops

As you start listening to native speakers, you will notice that casual Japanese is all about efficiency. When speaking casually with friends, people love to drop particles to speed up their sentences.

The `に` in `前に` is a prime target for this. In casual, everyday speech, it is often dropped entirely, leaving just `前`.

```jp
友達が来る前、部屋を出る。
```
*Before my friend comes, I'll leave the room.*

This doesn't change the meaning at all. It just lowers the formality and makes the sentence flow a bit faster. You can safely use this shorter version around people you are close with.

## Contrast: Setting the sequence

You already know how to use `てから` to say "after doing" something. You use it to sequence events, like saying "After I wake up, I eat."

`てから` and `前に` live in the exact same grammatical space. They are two tools for the exact same job, but they are mirror images of each other. Knowing when to use which depends entirely on what part of the sequence you want to emphasize.

Let's say you have a movie you want to watch and you also want to get some sleep.

If you want to emphasize the first action, you use `てから`. Remember that `てから` requires the `て`-form of the verb.

```jp
映画を見てから、寝る。
```
*After watching the movie, I sleep.*

If you want to emphasize the second action, you use `前に`. And as we just covered extensively, `前に` demands the dictionary form.

```jp
寝る前に、映画を見る。
```
*Before sleeping, I watch a movie.*

Notice how swapping from `てから` to `前に` means you have to physically flip the order of the actions in your sentence.

With `てから`, the first thing out of your mouth is the first thing you actually do. You watch the movie, then you sleep.

With `前に`, the first thing out of your mouth is the *second* thing you actually do. You mention sleeping first, but watching the movie is what actually happens first in reality.

It takes a little bit of mental gymnastics to flip these around on the fly. Don't worry if you have to pause for a second while speaking to line up your timeline. Everyone goes through that phase!

Mastering `前に` gives you immense control over telling stories and scheduling your day. Just remember the golden rule: lock that first verb in the dictionary form, and let the end of the sentence do all the time traveling for you. 

Hold onto this relative-time logic tightly. It's going to become incredibly important when we learn the exact opposite pattern—`後で` (after)—very soon!
