You are at a friend's house. They hand you a glass of clear liquid and you take a big gulp, expecting water. It burns. It is definitely not water. How do you tell them that?

## How it works

You already know the copula です (the little word that works like *is, am, or are*). In English, you negate a sentence by tossing "not" next to the verb. In Japanese, the copula itself shape-shifts to carry the negative meaning.

To say "is not" politely, you take a noun and attach either じゃないです or ではありません to it.

```jp
これは水じゃないです。
お酒です。
```
*This is not water.*
*It is alcohol.*

Both じゃないです and ではありません mean the **exact same thing**, but they carry slightly different vibes. じゃないです is the standard, everyday polite form. It sounds natural in almost any spoken conversation. ではありません is much more formal and crisp. You will see it constantly in writing, but in speech, it sounds like you are giving a presentation or speaking to a strict boss.

```jp
私は学生ではありません。
```
*I am not a student.*

There is also a middle-ground spoken form: じゃありません. It feels a bit softer and slightly more traditional, but it is just as useful to recognize in daily life.

```jp
彼は先生じゃありません。
```
*He is not a teacher.*

## Rules and exceptions

This pattern is incredibly common, but it has a strict diet: right now, you can only attach it to nouns.

> Do not try to attach じゃないです to a verb. If you want to say you aren't going to eat, you cannot say `食べるじゃないです`. Verbs have their own completely separate rules for becoming negative!

## Casual speech

When you are speaking casually with friends, the polite padding falls away completely. You drop the です or ありません and are left with just じゃない (or ではない in formal writing).

```jp
これは肉じゃない。
```
*This is not meat.*

Notice how the sentence ends abruptly. In casual Japanese, じゃない does all the heavy lifting on its own.

## Contrast

Let's line this up right next to what you already know. The structure of the sentence stays exactly the same; only the ending flips.

```jp
あれは学校です。
あれは学校じゃないです。
```
*That is a school.*
*That is not a school.*

Hold onto じゃない. You will see it pop up again later, attached to questions like じゃないですか to seek agreement and mean "isn't it?".
