You're at the convenience store to buy a nice boxed lunch, but when you look inside your wallet, you see a single 100-yen coin. It's definitely not enough. How do you express that specific feeling of "I only have this, and it's a problem"?

Enter しか (shika). When paired with a negative verb, it allows you to say "only" or "nothing but" while emphasizing that the amount or situation is limited.

## How it works

The structure is simple: **Noun + しか + Negative Verb**.

This throws a lot of learners off at first. Why use a negative verb to say you *do* have something? Think of `しか` as meaning "except for" or "other than". If you say you "don't have money other than 100 yen," you are saying there is only 100 yen.

```jp
財布の中に百円しかありません。
```
*I only have 100 yen in my wallet.* (Literally: Other than 100 yen, there is not.)

```jp
朝ご飯はパンしか食べませんでした。
```
*I only ate bread for breakfast.*

Notice how the verb at the end is always negative (`ありません`, `食べませんでした`), even though the English translation usually sounds positive ("I have", "I ate").

## The missing particles

Just like the topic marker `は`, `しか` is a bit of a bully. When it attaches to a noun, it completely overwrites the basic object and subject particles `を` and `が`.

> You will never see `をしか` or `がしか`. The `しか` simply takes their place.

```jp
水しか飲みません。
```
*I drink nothing but water.* (Instead of 水を)

However, other particles like `に` (location or direction) and `で` (context or action location) survive the collision. The `しか` simply stacks right after them.

```jp
私は家でしか勉強しません。
```
*I only study at home.*

## Casual speech

When you are talking to friends, the pattern works exactly the same way, but the negative verb at the end drops into plain form (like `ない` instead of `ありません`).

```jp
今日は千円しかない。
```
*I only have 1,000 yen today.*

## Contrast: Limits vs Excess

You already know how to talk about doing something *too much* with `すぎる`. Let's look at how `しか〜ない` sits at the exact opposite end of the spectrum.

```jp
昨日は肉を食べすぎました。
```
*Yesterday, I ate too much meat.*

```jp
昨日は肉しか食べませんでした。
```
*Yesterday, I only ate meat.* (And maybe I really needed vegetables.)

In both cases, you're focusing on an extreme imbalance. `すぎる` points out an excess, while `しか〜ない` points out a strict limit or a lack of something else.

Hold onto this idea of pairing a specific word with a negative verb to create a new meaning. You'll soon meet patterns like 全然 (not at all), which play exactly the same game!
