You check your watch. Your friend is thirty minutes late to meet you at the restaurant. You know they missed their train, and they had a lot of work to finish today anyway. Another friend asks if you think they are still showing up. You can't predict the future with absolute certainty, but all the evidence points to one conclusion. How do you say they probably aren't coming?

Whenever you need to make an educated guess, express a probability, or just soften a statement so you don't sound like a know-it-all, you will reach for だろう.

It is the easiest way to add a layer of "probably" or "I suspect" to your sentences in Japanese. Let's break down exactly how to use it.

## Attaching to verbs and adjectives

だろう acts like a caboose that you attach to the end of a train. The train, in this case, is the plain form of a word—the casual, dictionary shape of the word, rather than the polite polite forms you might be used to.

When you want to make a guess about an action, just take the plain form of the verb and add だろう.

```jp
彼は帰るだろう。
```
*He will probably return home.*

You can also use this to guess that an action won't happen. Just use the negative plain form of the verb.

```jp
今日は彼女は来ないだろう。
```
*She probably won't come today.*

This same logic applies directly to i-adjectives. If you are guessing that something is expensive, hot, or difficult, you just leave the adjective in its natural state and tack だろう on the end. Imagine you are looking at a fancy department store from the outside. You haven't looked at the price tags, but you can guess.

```jp
あの店の服は高いだろう。
```
*The clothes at that shop are probably expensive.*

## The noun and na-adjective trap

Because だろう is the designated word for guessing, it naturally works with nouns and na-adjectives too. But there is one major pitfall here that trips up almost everyone in their first week of using it.

When you speak casually in Japanese, you usually attach the plain copula だ (the casual version of です, the word that works like *is/am/are*) to nouns and na-adjectives to say that something "is."

You might be very tempted to leave that だ attached and simply add だろう on top of it. Do not do this. Stacking them into `雨だだろう` is a grammatical traffic jam.

Why? Because だろう is actually just a modified, guessing version of だ itself. It already has that fundamental "to be" energy baked directly into its DNA. If you put another だ right before it, you are essentially stuttering the verb.

Instead, you must drop any だ and attach だろう directly to the bare noun or na-adjective.

```jp
明日は雨だろう。
```
*Tomorrow will probably be rain.*

```jp
あの図書館は静かだろう。
```
*That library is probably quiet.*

## Fact versus probability

Let's look at the space this new pattern occupies compared to the grammar you already know. Until now, you've relied heavily on です to describe things. 

When you use です, you are stating something as a concrete, objective fact. You are telling your listener that you are certain of the information.

```jp
この本は面白いです。
```
*This book is interesting.*

Using です here implies you have already read the book, or you know for an absolute fact that it is a universally great read. But what if you haven't read it yet? What if you are just looking at the cover, and you want to recommend it based on a guess?

Using です would feel a bit too strong. You need to soften your statement.

```jp
この本は面白いだろう。
```
*This book is probably interesting.*

Swapping です for だろう immediately signals to your listener that this is your prediction. It leaves room for you to be wrong, which is an incredibly important social tool in Japanese conversation. People generally prefer to avoid stating things too aggressively, and making educated guesses is a great way to soften your speech.

## When things are probably not true

We mentioned earlier that you can use the negative plain form of verbs. You can do the exact same thing with nouns and adjectives by using their negative plain forms.

You already know that the negative of です is ではありません, and its plain equivalent is じゃない. To say something is *probably not* true, just attach だろう to that negative plain form.

```jp
彼は医者じゃないだろう。
```
*He is probably not a doctor.*

## Guessing locations and existence

You can also use this pattern with verbs of existence, like いる (for living things) and ある (for objects), to guess where something is right now.

Imagine you are looking for your cat, but you can't find her in the living room. It's a cold day, so you make a logical guess about her location without knowing for sure.

```jp
猫はベッドにいるだろう。
```
*The cat is probably on the bed.*

> A quick note on talking to others: In casual conversations, asking someone a direct question about themselves using this pattern (like "Will you probably go?") sounds unnatural, as people don't usually need to guess their own plans. Also, directing だろう at another person can carry a slightly masculine or blunt nuance. However, everyone—regardless of gender—uses it when they are thinking out loud or muttering a pure internal guess to themselves.

Hold onto this guessing mechanic. Right now, the pattern we've learned is perfect for casual speech with your friends or musing to yourself. But eventually, you'll need to make these same guesses in polite situations, like talking to a teacher or a boss. When we step up the politeness later on, this exact same pattern will upgrade to its polite twin: でしょう.
