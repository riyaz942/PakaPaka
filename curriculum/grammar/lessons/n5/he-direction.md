You pack your bag and step out the door. The weather is beautiful, and you're heading toward the park. How do you describe that trajectory—the physical direction you are moving in?

```jp
私は公園へ行きます。
```
*I am heading to the park.*

## How it works

To mark the direction you are traveling, attach the particle へ directly to your destination noun. It works almost exclusively with movement verbs like `行く` (to go), `来る` (to come), and `帰る` (to return).

> When reading, this character is written as へ (he), but when used as a particle, it is always pronounced "e" (like the "e" in "elephant"). You already saw this quirk when the character は became the topic particle "wa".

The structure is always the place you are heading toward, followed by へ, followed by the movement.

```jp
先生が学校へ来ます。
```
*The teacher comes to the school.*

```jp
明日、海へ行きます。
```
*Tomorrow, we will head to the sea.*

```jp
彼は家へ帰ります。
```
*He is returning home.*

## Rules and exceptions

Because へ is an arrow pointing outward, it only makes sense when there is actual physical movement involved. You cannot use it to describe where an action takes place if you are staying still. For example, you cannot say `部屋へ本を読みます` to mean reading a book inside a room.

Additionally, while the particle に can attach to a time word to mark when something happens, へ is strictly spatial. You cannot attach it to a time word to say something like `何時へ行きますか` (toward what time are you going?).

## Contrast: The arrow vs. the thumbtack

If you're thinking, "Wait, didn't I already learn に for going to a place?", you are absolutely right. 

```jp
公園に行きます。
```
*I go to the park.*

```jp
公園へ行きます。
```
*I head toward the park.*

In modern, everyday spoken Japanese, these two sentences are practically interchangeable. Most of the time, nobody will bat an eye if you use に instead of へ when talking about your destination.

The difference is nuance. に acts like a thumbtack on a map—it focuses strictly on the final endpoint. へ acts like an arrow—it highlights the direction of travel and the journey itself. Because it draws attention to the path, へ can sometimes sound a bit more sweeping or poetic. It is also common in formal announcements (like a train heading toward a specific station).

Remember: へ points the arrow, and に marks the spot. Hold onto this spatial logic, because you will soon meet で—a particle that builds the stage for where actions actually happen once you arrive.
