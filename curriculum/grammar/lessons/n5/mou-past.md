You're meeting a friend at the station to catch a train, but you realize you forgot to buy your ticket. You start to apologize and say you need to go to the machine, but your friend smiles and hands you a piece of paper. They want to say, "I already bought the ticket."

When an action is completely done and dusted, you need a way to express *already*. In Japanese, that job belongs to a tiny, powerful word: もう.

## How it works

To say you already did something, simply drop もう into the sentence and put your verb in the past tense (ました). It usually sits right before the object or the verb itself.

```jp
もう切符を買いました。
```
*I already bought the ticket.*

```jp
映画はもう終わりました。
```
*The movie already ended.*

Because もう is just a modifier for the verb, it floats quite freely in the sentence. As long as it comes before the action it modifies, it sounds perfectly natural.

```jp
彼はもう帰りました。
```
*He already went home.*

## Rules and exceptions

> **Watch your endings!** If you use もう with a present/future tense negative verb, it stops meaning "already" and starts meaning "not anymore".

This trips everyone up at least once. If you say `もう食べました`, you are saying "I already ate." But if you say `もう食べません`, you are saying "I won't eat anymore." Keep your verbs firmly in the past tense (ました) when you want to talk about things you have *already* done.

## Contrast: done vs. not yet

You learned まだ〜ていない for talking about things you haven't gotten around to yet. These two patterns are exact mirror images of each other. 

When someone asks if you finished a task, you will answer with one of these two forms.

```jp
もう宿題をしましたか。
```
*Did you already do the homework?*

```jp
まだ宿題をしていません。
```
*I have not done the homework yet.*

Notice how the question uses the simple past tense ました, but the negative answer requires the continuous state form ていません. You can't just say `まだしませんでした` (I didn't do it yet). 

Let's look at one more pair.

```jp
もう薬を飲みましたか。
```
*Did you already take the medicine?*

```jp
まだ飲んでいません。
```
*I have not taken it yet.*

Keep holding onto these time-related words. You know まだ means "not yet", but when we eventually meet 全然〜ない, we'll see how to take a negative sentence and crank it up to "not at all".
