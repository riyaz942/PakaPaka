You've had a long week, and your friend asks what you're doing this weekend. You don't just plan to sleep—you actively crave it. To express what you want to do, you need a specific pattern.

## How it works

To build this pattern, take the stem of a verb (the part right before `ます`) and attach `たい`. The verb `寝ます` (sleep) becomes `寝` plus `たい`. Add `です` at the end to keep things polite.

```jp
週末は寝たいです。
私は日本へ行きたいです。
あの映画を見たいです。
```
*I want to sleep on the weekend.*
*I want to go to Japan.*
*I want to watch that movie.*

### Shapeshifting like an adjective

The real magic of `たい` is that once you attach it, your verb transforms into an *i-adjective*. It ends in `い`, so it conjugates exactly like descriptive words you already know, such as `高い` (expensive) or `新しい` (new). 

To make it negative, drop the `い` and add `くない`. For the past tense, drop the `い` and add `かった`.

```jp
今日は会社へ行きたくないです。
昨日、あの本を買いたかったです。
```
*I don't want to go to the office today.*
*Yesterday, I wanted to buy that book.*

## The unwritten rules of desire

> You can only use `たい` to talk about your *own* desires, or to ask someone directly about theirs (like "Do you want to go?"). You cannot use this pattern to state "He wants to go" or "She wants to eat."

There is also a quirk with particles. When you use a verb that normally takes `を` (like drinking water), you will often hear Japanese speakers swap it for `が` when using `たい`.

Because `たい` acts like an adjective, "wanting to drink" becomes a descriptive state rather than a direct action. Both `を` and `が` are perfectly natural here.

```jp
水が飲みたいです。
```
*I want to drink water.*

## Keeping it casual

Because `たい` behaves exactly like an i-adjective, making it casual is as simple as dropping `です`. In questions, just use a rising intonation instead of adding `か`.

```jp
今、帰りたい。
明日、会いたい？
```
*I want to go home now.*
*Do you want to meet tomorrow?*

## When desire meets obligation

Sometimes what you want to do and what you *must* do are entirely different things. You already know `なければならない` for obligations. Notice how these two patterns stack up against each other in real life.

```jp
病院へ行きたくないです。
病院へ行かなければなりません。
```
*I don't want to go to the hospital.*
*I have to go to the hospital.*

We've spent this whole time talking about actions you want to *do*. If you just want a physical *thing*—like money or a new camera—you won't use `たい`. There is a different word for that coming right up.
