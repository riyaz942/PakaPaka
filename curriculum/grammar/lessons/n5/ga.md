You're sitting in a quiet room and something moves in the corner. You want to say it out loud — "a cat!" — but not as the topic of a conversation, just pointing at the plain fact that a cat is *there*. This is the moment が was built for.

が is the subject marker: the little word that tags **who or what is actually doing the verb or being the thing**. Where は steps back and says "as for this, here's my comment," が leans in and points: *this one, right here, is the one.*

```jp
猫がいる。
```

*There's a cat.*

Notice there's no "as for" flavor here. You're not commenting on the cat as an already-known topic — you're announcing it. が does that announcing work.

## How it works

The structure is as simple as it looks: **Noun + が + Predicate**. The noun before が is the subject — the thing that exists, acts, or is described by whatever comes after.

The clearest place to feel が is with question words. When you ask *who* or *what* is doing something, you almost always reach for が, because you're asking the listener to fill in the unknown subject.

```jp
誰がいる。
```

*Who's there?*

You can't make 誰 a topic — you can't comment on something you don't know yet. So は is off the table, and が steps in naturally. This is the single most reliable が instinct you can build: **question-word subjects take が.**

And the answer comes back with が too, because the answer is the newly-identified subject:

```jp
彼がいる。
```

*He's there.*

が works the same way for existence and possession. When you're stating that something simply *exists* or that you *have* it, the thing that exists is marked with が.

```jp
お金がある。
```

*There's money. / I have money.*

Same shape every time: the thing being pointed at, then が, then the predicate. You don't conjugate が or change it — it's one fixed syllable doing one job.

## Rules and exceptions

The trap almost every learner falls into is reaching for が when they mean a general topic. If you're talking *about* yourself as the subject of an ongoing conversation, は is usually what you want. が is for the *new*, the *specific*, the *answer to a question* — the piece of information that wasn't already on the table.

> When a question word like 誰 or 何 is the subject, use が, never は. "As for who?" is not a sentence.

There's a mirror rule that surprises people: the *answer* to a が question also takes が, but if you then shift to talking about that person generally, you switch back to は. The particle tracks what's new versus what's settled, not the grammar of the word itself.

> が marks the subject, but not every subject wears が. Once something becomes the known topic of the conversation, it often trades が for は.

## Casual speech

が itself doesn't contract — it's already a single clean syllable, so there's nothing to shorten. But in fast, casual speech, people often just *drop* it entirely when the meaning is obvious.

```jp
今、猫がいる。
猫いる。
```

*Right now, there's a cat. / A cat, there is.*

The first line is the full, neutral form. The second is what a friend might actually say pointing across the room — が gone, meaning intact. This dropping is casual only; in careful or polite speech, keep the が in.

## Contrast

Here's where が earns its keep against は, the topic marker you already know. Swap one for the other and the *feeling* of the sentence changes completely.

```jp
彼がいる。
彼は来る。
```

*HE's the one who's here. / As for him, he'll come.*

The first line answers "who's here?" — が spotlights 彼 as the new, specific subject. The second uses は to set 彼 up as the topic and then comments about him. が points a finger; は sets a stage.

And against を, the direct object marker: を marks *what the action lands on*, while が marks *who does the action*. In a fuller sentence they'd sit on opposite sides of the verb — the doer in が, the done-to in を. For now, just hold the split: が is the actor, を is the target.

That's the whole core: が points at the subject, especially when it's new information or an answer to *who* or *what*. Hold onto this feeling — it comes back the moment we meet の and start linking nouns together.
