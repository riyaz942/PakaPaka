Imagine you are catching up with a coworker on a Monday morning, and they ask what you did over the weekend. If you try to list out your actions strictly—saying, "I read a book, I watched TV, and I slept"—it can sound a bit like a police interrogation report. You are giving a rigid, step-by-step timeline of your weekend that feels almost too precise.

In real life, we usually want to give a more relaxed summary. You want to say, "Oh, you know, I did things like reading and watching TV." You aren't claiming that those two activities are the *only* things you did, nor are you saying you did them in a specific order. You are just pulling a couple of representative examples out of a hat to give a general vibe of how you spent your day.

To do this in Japanese, you use the `〜たり〜たりする` pattern. It is the absolute best tool for creating a relaxed, non-exhaustive list of actions, and mastering it will instantly make your Japanese sound more natural and less robotic.

## How it works

To build this pattern, you need the plain past tense form of your verb. You already know this form from when we learned how to talk about past experiences with `たことがある` (have done before). Because it always ends in a "ta" sound, teachers often just call it the "た-form".

Take your verb's た-form, and simply attach `り` to the very end.

- `読む` (to read) becomes `読んだ` (read), which becomes `読んだり`.
- `見る` (to watch) becomes `見た` (watched), which becomes `見たり`.
- `買う` (to buy) becomes `買った` (bought), which becomes `買ったり`.

Once you have your verbs ready, you string them together and finish the entire package with the verb `する` (to do).

```jp
週末、本を読んだり、テレビを見たりしました。
```
*Over the weekend, I did things like read books and watch TV.*

Think of these `たり` verbs as floating ideas. By themselves, they do not carry any actual tense, and they aren't polite or casual. They are just raw, timeless concepts: "things like reading", "things like watching". 

It is that final `する` at the end of the sentence that acts as the engine. It grounds those floating ideas in time and determines exactly how polite you are being. In the example above, `する` becomes `しました` because the weekend is in the past, and you are speaking politely to your coworker.

If you are talking about a daily habit or something you do regularly, the final verb is just the present tense `します`.

```jp
毎日、友達と話したり、お茶を飲んだりします。
```
*Every day, I do things like talk with friends and drink tea.*

## Rules and exceptions

Because English speakers are used to assigning time to every single verb in a sentence ("Tomorrow I *will* go and I *will* buy"), this pattern holds a very common trap: trying to make the `たり` verbs match the time of the event.

If you are talking about your plans for tomorrow, you might feel a strong urge to use a future or present form for the list. Do not do this! The verbs inside the list are *always* frozen in the past `た` form, even if the action hasn't happened yet. Only the final `する` gets to change to show the future tense.

```jp
明日、町へ行ったり、服を買ったりします。
```
*Tomorrow, I will do things like go to town and buy clothes.*

Notice how it is still `行ったり` and `買ったり`, even though the sentence is clearly about tomorrow (`明日`). The final `します` does all the heavy lifting to tell the listener this is a future plan. Never try to say something like `行くなり` or `買うたり`—the form is strictly locked to the past tense shape.

> **The phonetic shift:** Just like with the `て` and `た` forms, verbs that end in a voiced, heavy sound in their dictionary form (like `読む` or `遊ぶ`) carry that heaviness over. Because their た-form ends in `だ` instead of `た`, they take `だり` instead of `たり`.

For example, `遊ぶ` (to play) becomes `遊んだ`, which becomes `遊んだり`. `飲む` (to drink) becomes `飲んだ`, which becomes `飲んだり`.

```jp
公園で遊んだり、水を飲んだりしました。
```
*I did things like play in the park and drink water.*

Another massive feature of this grammar point: you do not actually have to list two things. You can absolutely use just a single `たり` to soften a statement. When you use it with just one verb, it implies, "I did this... among other things." It acts like a verbal shrug that keeps you from sounding overly declarative.

```jp
昨日は、家で寝たりしました。
```
*Yesterday, I did things like sleep at home.*

## Casual speech

When you are speaking casually with close friends or family members, you simply drop the polite `します` or `しました` and use the casual forms of `する`, which are `する` (for present/future) and `した` (for past).

In extremely casual, fast-paced Japanese, people sometimes drop the `する` completely if the context is totally obvious, leaving the sentence hanging on `〜たり`. However, as a learner, you should strongly stick to always including the final `する` or `した`. Leaving it off by accident in a polite conversation sounds abruptly unfinished and a bit rude, so build the muscle memory of attaching `する` every time first.

## Mixing menus and rules

You already know a few powerful rules for giving permission and setting boundaries. Let's look at how beautifully the `たり〜たり` list integrates with the grammar you already know. 

Think about the pattern `てもいい` (it's okay to / you may). If you say `テレビを見てもいいです` (you may watch TV), it is a specific green light for one single action.

But what if you are hosting a guest, and you want to offer them a menu of options? You just take your `たり` list and swap the final `する` engine for `してもいい`.

```jp
私の部屋で、テレビを見たり、本を読んだりしてもいいです。
```
*In my room, you may do things like watch TV or read a book.*

Suddenly, you aren't just granting rigid permission; you are being an accommodating host offering a variety of choices. The list provides the options, and the final `してもいい` grants the permission for all of them at once.

The exact same magic works in reverse with `てはいけない` (must not). A library doesn't just have one single rule; they have a blanket policy of disruptive behaviors to avoid. By changing the final `する` to `してはいけません` (the polite form of must not do), you create a comprehensive ban.

```jp
図書館で話したり、電話を使ったりしてはいけません。
```
*You must not do things like talk or use the phone in the library.*

And if you want to reassure someone that they have the day off and absolutely no obligations to worry about, you can plug in `なくてもいい` (don't have to).

```jp
明日、学校へ行ったり、本を読んだりしなくてもいいです。
```
*Tomorrow, you don't have to do things like go to school or read a book.*

The `たり〜たり` pattern turns rigid, singular actions into flexible, representative lists. Because it relies completely on that final `する` to decide the sentence's ultimate meaning, it is incredibly modular. Hold onto this concept of swapping the final engine. When we learn how to express personal desire with `たい` (want to do) later on, you will already know exactly how to say that you want to do things like watch a movie and sleep—you will just swap that hardworking `する` for a `したい`.
