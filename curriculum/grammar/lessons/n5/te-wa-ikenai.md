You're standing in a quiet library, and your friend pulls out their phone to make a call. Before they even tap the screen, you want to stop them — not with a polite "please don't," but with the flat, no-exceptions "you can't do that here." That's the job of てはいけない.

This is the pattern for rules and prohibitions: the sign on the wall, the thing the teacher says, the line you don't cross. It's the mirror image of something you already know — てもいい, which grants permission. Where てもいい opens a door, てはいけない slams it shut.

```jp
ここで電話を使ってはいけません。
```

*You must not use the phone here.*

## How it works

The build is simple because you already own half of it. Take a verb, put it in its て-form (the same て-form you use for てください and てもいい), and attach はいけない. That's the whole machine.

```jp
食べる → 食べて → 食べてはいけない
飲む → 飲んで → 飲んではいけない
```

*eat → (te-form) → must not eat*
*drink → (te-form) → must not drink*

The polite version just swaps いけない for いけません. Same meaning, more formal register — the kind of thing you'd see on a posted notice or hear from station staff.

```jp
ここでお酒を飲んではいけません。
```

*You must not drink alcohol here.*

Why はいけない? Literally いけない is a word meaning "it won't do" or "it's no good." So the whole thing reads like "doing X won't do." That's a useful intuition: you're not commanding a person directly, you're declaring that a certain *action* is not acceptable. The は in the middle is the topic particle — it's marking "as for doing this thing" — which is exactly why it's pronounced *wa*, not *ha*.

```jp
夜にコーヒーを飲んではいけない。
```

*You must not drink coffee at night.*

## Rules and exceptions

The て-form does the heavy lifting, so every irregular て-form you've already met carries straight over. 行く becomes 行って (not `行きて`), 話す becomes 話して, 待つ becomes 待って. If your て-form is right, the prohibition is right.

```jp
その部屋に入ってはいけません。
```

*You must not enter that room.*

One thing to watch: because いけない ends like a negative, learners sometimes try to negate it again to say "don't have to." That's a different pattern entirely, and stacking negatives here just produces something broken. Don't reach for `食べてはいけなくない` — it isn't a thing.

> てはいけない is genuinely strong. It's the register of laws, safety signs, and adults laying down rules to children. Saying it to your host at a dinner party — even about something small — lands like scolding. For softening someone's behavior gently, this is the wrong tool.

The subject is usually left unspoken, because a prohibition tends to apply to everyone or to "you." But you can name who's bound by the rule with は or が when you need to be clear about it.

```jp
子供はお酒を飲んではいけません。
```

*Children must not drink alcohol.*

## Casual speech

In real conversation, てはいけない contracts hard. The ては collapses into ちゃ, so 食べてはいけない becomes 食べちゃいけない. This is everywhere in casual speech — friends, family, anyone you'd already be speaking plainly with.

```jp
それを食べちゃいけない。
```

*You can't eat that.*

The で-form verbs (the ones whose て-form is で, like 飲む → 飲んで) shift to じゃ instead: 飲んではいけない → 飲んじゃいけない. Same logic, just following the voicing of the sound before it.

```jp
今、帰っちゃいけない。
```

*You can't go home right now.*

Even the ending often gets trimmed — people drop off after ちゃ and just let the tone or a trailing だめ carry the "no good" meaning. But for now, ちゃいけない is the reliable casual form to lock in.

## Contrast

Line てはいけない up against てもいい, the permission pattern you learned right before it. They share the て-form base and differ only in the tail, but they're opposites — and that's the cleanest way to feel the meaning.

```jp
ここで写真を見てはいけません。
```

*You must not look at photos here.*

```jp
ここで写真を見てもいいです。
```

*You may look at photos here.*

One tail says "no," the other says "yes." If you can build one, you can build the other — same verb, same て-form, just choose the door.

It's also worth separating this from てください, your request pattern. てください asks someone to *do* something as a favor; てはいけない tells them an action is *off-limits* as a rule. "Please close the door" and "you must not close the door" are not two shades of the same idea — one is a polite ask, the other is a prohibition.

So: て-form plus はいけない is your "off-limits" stamp, softening to ちゃいけない among friends. When you later want to say "please *don't*" as a gentle request rather than a hard rule, you'll meet ないでください — a much softer way to hold someone back, and the natural next step from here.
