You're making weekend plans with a new friend, and they ask if you want to check out a famous sushi restaurant. You hate fish. Or maybe you're at a job interview and need to confidently state your language skills. You need a way to clearly say what you can—and cannot—do.

To talk about your abilities or what is physically possible, you use the pattern **ことができる**.

## How it works

To build this, take any verb in its dictionary form (the basic, present-tense form like 食べる or 行く), and attach **ことができる** (casual) or **ことができます** (polite).

Why does this string of words mean "can"? Let's look at the machinery. You already know that you cannot just stick a noun particle like が directly onto a verb. So, we use こと, a nominalizer—a little word that turns a whole verb phrase into a noun thing. 食べる (to eat) becomes 食べること (the act of eating).

Finally, you add が (the subject marker) and できる (a verb meaning "is possible" or "can be done"). Literally, you are saying "the act of eating is possible."

```jp
彼女は日本語を話すことができます。
明日、学校に行くことができます。
```
*She can speak Japanese.*
*I can go to school tomorrow.*

## Rules and exceptions

Making this pattern negative or turning it into a question works exactly the same as any other polite verb. You just change the final できます to できません (cannot) or add か to make できますか (can you?).

```jp
あの鳥は飛ぶことができません。
ここで本を読むことができますか。
```
*That bird cannot fly.*
*Can I read a book here?*

One structural detail to watch out for is your particles. Even though the whole verb phrase is eventually marked with が, the object of your action usually keeps its original を particle. You say 日本語**を**話す (speak Japanese), so you keep that exact phrasing right before こと.

## Casual speech

In casual speech with friends or family, you simply drop the polite ます endings. You will use the plain form できる for "can" and できない for "cannot."

```jp
僕は漢字を書くことができる。
昨日、寝ることができなかった。
```
*I can write kanji.*
*I could not sleep yesterday.*

## Potential vs. Experience

You recently learned how to talk about your past experiences using たことがある (have done before). Because these two patterns sound so incredibly similar, they trip up almost everyone at first.

The secret to keeping them straight is the verb form that comes immediately before こと.

```jp
魚を食べたことがあります。
魚を食べることができます。
```
*I have eaten fish before.*
*I can eat fish.*

> Swapping the dictionary form for the past-tense た-form completely changes the meaning from "I am able to" to "I have done it before." Take an extra second to verify your verb tense before you speak!

Now you can clearly explain exactly what you are capable of doing. Hang onto this dictionary form structure—you'll see a very similar shape when we learn how to say what *might* happen using かもしれない soon.
