You're showing a friend pictures of a hotel you stayed at. You want to tell them it was a quiet place, but you also need to warn them that the location wasn't very convenient.

To do this, you need the other half of the Japanese adjective system: **na-adjectives** (な形容詞).

You already know *i-adjectives* like 高い (expensive) or 新しい (new). Those words are highly independent—they change their own endings to show past or negative tense (like 高くなかった).

Na-adjectives are completely different. Think of them as noun-like words that are a bit lazy. They don't change their own shapes at all. Instead, they rely on helper words (like the copula です, which functions like *is/am/are*) to do the grammatical heavy lifting.

## Ending a sentence

When a na-adjective is the last piece of information in a sentence, you treat it exactly like a noun. You just attach です (or its past tense でした) right after the dictionary form of the word.

```jp
この町は静かです。
```
*This town is quiet.*

```jp
部屋はきれいでした。
```
*The room was clean.*

> **Wait, isn't きれい an i-adjective?** It ends in an "i" sound, so it's a very common trap! But きれい is actually a na-adjective. There are a handful of these tricksters (like 有名, famous, and 嫌い, disliked). You just have to memorize them as you meet them.

## Connecting to a noun

Here is where na-adjectives get their name. When you want to put the adjective directly in front of a noun to describe it (like saying "a quiet town" instead of "the town is quiet"), you must use **な** as the glue between them.

```jp
ここは静かな町です。
```
*This is a quiet town.*

```jp
有名な人に会いました。
```
*I met a famous person.*

Without that な, the two words would just crash into each other awkwardly. The な tells the listener, "Hey, the word I just said is describing the noun coming up right now."

## Making it negative

Because na-adjectives act like nouns, you make them negative the exact same way you make a noun negative: by adding ではありません (is not).

```jp
私は暇ではありません。
```
*I am not free.*

```jp
犬が好きではありません。
```
*I do not like dogs.*

## Casual speech

In real conversation with friends, the stiff ではありません almost always contracts. You'll hear people say じゃない instead. So, 暇ではありません shrinks down to 暇じゃない (I'm not free).

## The biggest trap: mixing up the two groups

When you're speaking quickly, your brain will try to apply i-adjective rules to na-adjectives. 

If a book isn't new, you know to change the i-adjective 新しい to 新しくない:

```jp
本は新しくないです。
```
*The book is not new.*

But if you want to say you don't like something, you absolutely cannot chop off the end and turn 好き into `好きくない`. Because 好き is a na-adjective, it can't conjugate itself. You have to use the noun-style negative.

```jp
魚が好きではありません。
```
*I do not like fish.*

Learning which adjective belongs to which group takes a bit of practice, but whenever you learn a new word, pay attention to whether it needs that な glue. Speaking of glue, hold onto the idea of な connecting these words to nouns. Later on, when you want to connect them to verbs (to say you did something "quietly"), you'll swap that な out for a に.
