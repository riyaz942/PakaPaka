You're at a friend's place in Tokyo, and the tea on the table smells amazing. You want a cup, but it's not yours to just grab. In English you'd ask "can I have some?" — in Japanese you reach for てもいい, the pattern that asks *is it okay if I…?*

```jp
このお茶を飲んでもいいですか。
```

*Is it okay if I drink this tea?*

That's the whole job of てもいい: you describe an action, then ask whether doing it is permitted — or, without the question, you *grant* that permission to someone else. It's the polite handshake around "may I" and "you may."

## How it works

The build is simple because you already own the hard part. Take a verb, put it into its て-form (the same form you learned for てください and ている), then bolt on もいい.

```jp
食べる → 食べて → 食べてもいい
```

*eat → eating(-form) → may eat*

The literal pieces are worth seeing once. も means *even*, and いい means *good*. So 食べてもいい is *even if I eat, it's good* — "even doing this is fine," which is exactly how permission feels. That's your *why*: the も is what makes it *permission* rather than a command.

To make it polite, add です on the end. Both of these are correct; the first is plain, the second is the one you'll say to a stranger or a teacher.

```jp
ここに座ってもいい。
ここに座ってもいいですか。
```

*I may sit here. / Is it okay if I sit here?*

When you're the one *giving* permission, drop the question and just state it. A parent to a child, a host to a guest:

```jp
テレビを見てもいいですよ。
```

*You may watch TV.*

There's also a more formal, more generous-sounding variant: てもかまいません, literally *even doing it, I don't mind*. Use it when you want to sound extra accommodating — a station attendant, say, telling you it's no trouble.

```jp
ここで待ってもかまいません。
```

*You may wait here (I don't mind at all).*

## Rules and exceptions

The most common early mistake is dropping the も. Learners hear that permission is "て + いい" and write `食べていい` thinking that's the neutral form. In casual speech that contraction does happen (more on that below), but in careful, polite Japanese the も carries the *permission* meaning — leave it out in a formal setting and it sounds unfinished. When in doubt, keep the も.

> てもいい asks about *your own* action or grants one to someone else. It does **not** work as a general "is X allowed here?" sign-style rule. For that broader "is it forbidden?" feeling you'll want a different pattern (coming very soon).

Another sharp edge: adding か makes it a genuine request for permission, and the answer matters. If you ask 飲んでもいいですか and the host says だめ (no good), you don't drink. Without か, you're not asking — you're telling someone it's fine. Mixing those up can make you sound like you're granting yourself permission out loud, which is a little odd.

One more: because いい is an adjective, some learners try to bend it like `食べてもいかった` for the past. Don't chase that at N5 — stick to the present *may / it's okay* meaning, which covers almost every situation you'll meet.

## Casual speech

Among friends, two things happen. First, the polite です falls away. Second — and this is the big one — the も itself often contracts out, so てもいい becomes ていい in fast speech.

```jp
これ食べてもいい。
これ食べていい。
```

*Can I eat this? (both casual)*

Both are casual and both are fine between friends; the second, with the も gone, is the more relaxed of the two. The tone alone (a rising, questioning tone) does the work that か does in polite speech.

> Keep the も in anything written, formal, or spoken to someone above you. The dropped-も version is friends-and-family territory only.

## Contrast

You've met てください — *please do this* — and it lives right next door, because both attach to the て-form. But they point in opposite directions. てください *asks the other person to act*; てもいい asks whether *you* may act, or tells the other person they *may*.

```jp
窓を開けてください。
窓を開けてもいいですか。
```

*Please open the window. / Is it okay if I open the window?*

The first hands the job to your listener. The second keeps the action with you and just checks permission. Swap one for the other and you've flipped who's doing the opening — a real change in meaning, not just politeness.

It's also worth setting てもいい beside ませんか, the invitation you learned. ませんか pulls the other person into an activity with you — *won't you…?* — while てもいい is purely about whether an action is allowed.

```jp
一緒に行きませんか。
一緒に行ってもいいですか。
```

*Won't you go together (with me)? / Is it okay if I come along?*

The first is a warm invitation you extend; the second is you asking to be let in. Same activity, completely different social move.

Here's the one-line recap: て-form + もいい asks or grants permission — *may I / you may*. Hold onto the shape, because the moment you can say "you may," the natural next question is "and what may I *not* do?" — that's てはいけない, *must not*, waiting for you just around the corner.
