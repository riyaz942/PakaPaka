You are chatting with a new friend and want to share a few stories. Maybe you want to mention that you hated vegetables as a kid, or explain that you always drink iced coffee when it is hot outside. To link a specific situation to an action, you need a way to say "when" or "at the time of".

Enter とき.

## The "Time" trick

The secret to mastering this pattern is simple: とき (sometimes written in kanji as 時) literally means "time". It is not a magical grammar particle; it is just a regular noun.

Because it behaves like a noun, you connect other words to it using the exact same rules you already know for modifying nouns.

When you want to attach another noun to it, you need the particle の to bridge them together:

```jp
子供の時、野菜が嫌いでした。
```
*When I was a child, I hated vegetables.*

```jp
学生の時、毎日勉強しました。
```
*When I was a student, I studied every day.*

If you want to use a Na-adjective to describe the time, you leave the な attached, just as you would when describing any other noun:

```jp
暇な時、本を読みます。
```
*When I am free, I read books.*

I-adjectives are the easiest. Because they are designed to attach directly to nouns on their own, you just stick them right in front of とき:

```jp
暑い時、冷たい水を飲みます。
```
*When it is hot, I drink cold water.*

## The Time Travel Trap

You can also attach verbs to とき to say "when I do X". Verbs connect using their plain forms (like the dictionary form `帰る` or the casual past form `帰った`). 

However, this is where English and Japanese disagree, and it trips almost everyone up at first. In English, if you are telling a story about yesterday, you put every verb in the past tense. Japanese does not do this.

In Japanese, the tense of the verb right before とき depends *only* on whether that specific action was finished at that exact moment in the story.

If the first action hasn't finished yet, you use the dictionary form:

```jp
家へ帰る時、パンを買いました。
```
*When I was going home (on the way), I bought bread.*

Because you were still in transit and had not arrived home yet, `帰る` stays in the dictionary form. It does not matter that you bought the bread in the past (`買いました`)—relative to buying the bread, the "going home" part was still happening.

If the action was already entirely finished, you use the past (`た`) form:

```jp
家へ帰った時、テレビを見ました。
```
*When I went home (after arriving), I watched TV.*

Here, the action of going home was completely finished before the TV watching began, so you use `帰った`.

> Don't let the end of the sentence drag the とき clause into the wrong tense. A good rule of thumb: if you can replace "when" with "on the way to", use the dictionary form. If you can replace it with "after arriving", use the past form.

## Setting the scene vs. Strict sequence

You already know how to use 後で (after doing) to put events in a timeline.

```jp
ご飯を食べた後で、コーヒーを飲みます。
```
*After I eat a meal, I drink coffee.*

This gives you a strict sequence. Step one is completely finished, and then step two begins. とき covers similar ground but is looser—it just sets the scene or the general timeframe for the main event.

```jp
ご飯を食べる時、コーヒーを飲みます。
```
*When I eat a meal, I drink coffee.*

Using とき here means the coffee drinking happens as part of the mealtime experience, rather than waiting strictly until the food is gone.

The same difference applies to 前に (before doing).

```jp
寝る前に、本を読みます。
```
*Before I sleep, I read a book.*

```jp
寝る時、電気を消します。
```
*When I go to sleep, I turn off the light.*

Using とき simply frames the situation. Hold onto this idea of setting a scene. When you eventually want to talk about doing two specific things simultaneously—like walking exactly *while* drinking coffee—there is a different pattern waiting for you.
